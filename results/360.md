# Puzzle 360: Complementary divisors to make a prime

> **In short:** N qualifies when d + N/d and |d − N/d| are 1 or prime for every divisor d. The known terms are 2, 6, 30, 42 and 462. We prove that **any further term is divisible by 2 and by every prime ≡ 3 (mod 4) below 5000**, so it has more than 1079 digits. The proof makes Fred Schneider's "vicious cycle" (on the page) rigorous. If ℓ ∤ N, then ℓ | d ∓ N/d exactly when N ≡ ±d² (mod ℓ). For ℓ ≡ 3 (mod 4) the values ±d² of the divisors already forced cover every nonzero residue, so ℓ is forced too. The only ways out of the first three steps are exactly the known terms 30, 42 and 462. A term with 340 prime factors would need 2³⁴⁰ numbers of more than 1000 digits all to be prime at once, so the sequence is almost certainly finite with 462 its last term.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_360.htm)

**Status:** answered · Q1: no term after 462 below 10¹⁰⁷⁹ (proof); Q2: properties explained · finiteness heuristic

## The question

Parviz Afereidoon asks for numbers N such that for every divisor d of N, with d′ = N/d, both |d + d′| and |d − d′| are 1 or prime. He found 2, 6, 30, 42 and 462 and observed that (a) N = 6k for N > 3 and (b) every term is a product of two consecutive numbers. Q1 asks for the next term, Q2 to explain (a) and (b).

On the page:

- Farideh Firoozbakht proved that terms are squarefree, that 6 | N for N > 4, and that any term above 462 is divisible by 462. She guessed 462 is the last term.
- Fred Schneider (2006) noted that 19 must then divide N too, unless N = x(x − 19), and that 23, 31 and 47 follow, "unless" N has a special form. He then listed twelve more primes and wrote: "Clearly, this is vicious cycle and there are no other solutions." The exceptions after 19 were not checked, and the cycle was not shown to go on.

## The math

The chain of claims, each checked before the next is used:

| # | Claim | Why | Validated |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | the valid N ≤ 10⁵ are 2, 6, 30, 42, 462 | direct check | exhaustive |
| 2 | for a prime ℓ ∤ N and d \| N: ℓ \| d − N/d ⇔ N ≡ d², and ℓ \| d + N/d ⇔ N ≡ −d² (mod ℓ) | d(d ∓ N/d) = d² ∓ N, and ℓ ∤ d | algebra |
| 3 | a value divisible by ℓ must equal ℓ, and this can happen for at most one pair {d, N/d} | d + N/d ≥ 2√N > ℓ; d − N/d is strictly monotone in d | algebra (N > 10⁵, or N at least the forced product) |
| 4 | if every r ≢ 0 (mod ℓ) is ±d² for at least three distinct d built from the primes already forced, then ℓ \| N | by claim 3, at most two of the d can escape, so N ≡ r is impossible | the cascade script |
| 5 | if some r is reached by only one or two d, then ℓ ∤ N forces N = d(d ± ℓ) for one of them | claim 3 | each candidate N is tested |
| 6 | 2 and 3 divide every term N > 6 | N ± 1 prime (Afereidoon, Firoozbakht) | — |
| 7 | from {2, 3}: 7 is forced except N = 30; then 11 except N = 42; then 19 except N = 462 | claims 4–5 | candidates computed; the only valid ones are the known terms |
| 8 | every prime ≡ 3 (mod 4) below 5000 is then forced, with no valid exception | claims 4–5 | the cascade script, all 335 steps after 19 (candidates arise only at ℓ = 23: 14, none valid) |
| 9 | primes ℓ ≡ 1 (mod 4) are never forced this way | ±d² are then all quadratic residues | algebra |

**Why the cascade runs.** For ℓ ≡ 3 (mod 4), −1 is a non-residue, so every nonzero residue is +x² or −x² for some x. So ℓ is forced as soon as the subset products of the forced primes reach every class ±x mod ℓ. That needs only about log₂ ℓ well-spread primes. Each forced prime doubles the number of divisors, which is why the cascade speeds up instead of dying out. From ℓ = 31 on, every class is reached at least three times, so no candidate N appears at all; at ℓ = 23 the 14 candidates all fail.

**Why 30, 42 and 462 exist (Q2).** Each is the single escape at its step: a pair with |d − N/d| equal to the prime ℓ that would otherwise be forced.

- 30 = 3·10, with 10 − 3 = 7.
- 42 = 3·14, with 14 − 3 = 11.
- 462 = 14·33, with 33 − 14 = 19.

The cascade's candidate lists contain no other valid numbers.

Property (a) is claim 6. Property (b), N = x(x + 1), holds for all five terms, but it is not a necessary property. It comes from the pair with |d − d′| = 1 near √N, the cheapest way to make one value equal 1. Every larger N must be divisible by the 1080-digit product of the forced primes, so no further term of either shape exists below 10¹⁰⁷⁹.

**Why the sequence is almost certainly finite.** A further term N has at least 340 prime factors, hence 2³³⁹ divisor pairs, and each pair needs two primes near N/d. Even the simplest pair, N ± 1, has chance about (2.3/ln N)² ≈ 10⁻⁶ at 1080 digits. All 2³⁴⁰ values at once have chance indistinguishable from 0. We have not proved that the cascade continues past 5000. That needs a covering lemma for subset products in (ℤ/ℓ)*/{±1}, which is what Schneider took for granted.

[`results/code/p360_cascade.py`](code/p360_cascade.py) with arguments `5000 --small` (about 30 seconds):
