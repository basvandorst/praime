Puzzle 65
How: Equal power sums survive shifting and scaling, so we took a known degree-5
Prouhet-Tarry-Escott solution, doubled it (gaps between odd primes are even) and
sieved for a start c where all 12 numbers are consecutive primes.
Solution: p = 19765897565467; the consecutive primes p+{0,16,22,54,60,76} and
p+{4,6,36,40,70,72} have equal sums of k-th powers for k = 1..5 (degree 5).

Puzzle 87 (Q1, Q2)

need small parts; we listed every 3x3 prime magic square with entries below 2000

Puzzle 92 (n = 5)
How: The bottom level decides the whole pile, so we fixed the bottom balls'
remainders mod 3 (and a few small primes) so no upper ball can be divisible by
them, then filled the bottom with small primes by a guided depth-first search.
Solution: bottom layers 23 | 61 127 | 41 29 71 | 139 43 7 103 | 17 101 137 89 37
(each ball above = sum of the three below), top 4999, total 16561.

Puzzle 99
How: If p is a palindrome and squaring p causes no carries, p^2 is a palindrome
too and the condition holds by itself; so we only needed a sparse palindrome whose
square stays carry-free, then a primality test.
Solution: p = 10^3000 + 10^1889 + 10^1500 + 10^1111 + 1 (3001 digits, PRP).

Puzzle 134 (Q3)
How: Make the last digit the only active one, so the four images are the decade
10B+1, 10B+3, 10B+7, 10B+9; we built B (digits 0,2,4,5,6,8) with the Chinese
remainder theorem so no small prime divides any of the four.
Solution: 201-digit carrousel prime 10*B + {1,3,7,9}, B in
https://github.com/basvandorst/praime/blob/main/results/134.md

Puzzle 227
How: Each new prime narrows the interval for t; starting near t = 3000 keeps the
interval for t^k wide enough to hold a prime, and we backtracked instead of
stopping at a dead end.
Solution: t = a / 2^2760 with floor(t) = 3001 (a has 835 digits); floor(t^j) is
prime for j = 1..239. a in https://github.com/basvandorst/praime/blob/main/results/data/227.txt

Puzzle 249
How: Split each 12-digit number into two halves that each use five of the ten
digits, so pandigitality is automatic and only primality has to be searched.
Solution: [c-b, c+a+b, c-a / c-a+b, c, c+a-b / c+a, c-a-b, c+b] with
c = 320515678943, a = 3000000, b = 4554; magic sum 961547036829.

Puzzle 307
How: Costello's recipe small prime x Mersenne prime x power of 10, applied to the
newest Mersenne prime with the smallest small prime that balances the digit sums.
Solution: 7 * (2^136279841 - 1) * 10^2192 (41,026,513 digits).

Puzzle 324

so we took the largest possible one (74 digits, Manning 2025) and listed its pair
orderings from the top down until one was prime.
Solution: 99989897979696968585858484848473737373737373626262626262515140405151404051

Puzzle 340
How: Resta's idea: aim for a square of fixed shape (prefix, then p, then a tail),
which turns into a quadratic whose root gives p directly.
Solution: p = ceil(10^3923 * (1 + sqrt(22001)) / 2) (3925 digits, PRP); p^2
contains p right after the digits "55".

Puzzle 342
How: Every face is at most the total, so below 658 only 1^2..25^2 can occur, and
the corner sums depend only on the 15 ways to split six faces into opposite pairs;
an exhaustive check of that small space.
Solution: opposite faces (4, 64), (36, 144), (49, 361); total 658, minimal.

Puzzle 359
How: Exactly 101 of the first 202 primes contain a 1 and no two may touch, so they
must alternate; with that fixed, simulated annealing finished the circle. The same

Puzzle 373
How: A generator of n differs from n only in the last few digits, so the self test
collapses to two small equations on the last 9 digits (Pebody's test), which we
ran on the known giant primes.
Solution: 2^82589933 - 1 (24,862,048 digits) is a self number.

Puzzle 419
How: Use only primes q with q + 1 = 2^a 3^b 5^c, so sigma(N) is a product of 2, 3
and 5, and pick 336 of them whose exponent sums and prime sum match a 47th power.
Solution: N = product of 336 primes, SOPF = 3^42 * 5^25, sigma(N) = (SOPF*tau(N))^47.
Primes in https://github.com/basvandorst/praime/blob/main/results/data/419.txt

Puzzle 450
How: Twin primes k*2^n +- 1 are consecutive and sum to k*2^(n+1), so the count is
n + 1 plus the factors of k; the largest known twin primes win.
Solution: 2996863034895*2^1290000 +- 1; their sum has 1,290,006 prime factors.

Puzzle 476
How: Build the chain backwards (q = (p*2^n - 1)/3 leads into p), but keep the 40
smallest predecessors per step (beam search) instead of only the smallest.
Solution: 458-digit seed with 30 consecutive prime odd terms (PRP):
https://github.com/basvandorst/praime/blob/main/results/data/476.txt

Puzzle 522
How: The condition only depends on remainders, so each run gets a key (x_i mod p_i)
and two runs fit when one key is the negative of the other: sorting instead of
pairing, over all runs below 2*10^10.
Solution: x1 = 3355845769, y1 = 5130974053, k = 15 (divisors 2..47).

Puzzle 553
How: The 18 known solutions share a pattern: k+1 is a product of run primes, k/2
avoids them, and p + k/2 = product/(k+1) fixes p exactly; no search needed.
Solution: p = (523*541*...*3001)/2593 - 1296, q = p + 2592 (1060 digits);
p + (p+1) + ... + q = 523*541*...*3001 (333 primes).

Puzzle 597
How: Shift the best known quadratic so its middle sits in the middle of the range;
smaller values are more often prime or semiprime.
Solution: x^2 - 9999x + 25242757 gives 9743.

Puzzle 607
How: Powers of a prime with few nonzero digits are long runs of 9s and 0s with
small blocks between, so most digits never appear; we used the largest known
palindromic prime.
Solution: (10^1888529 - 10^944264 - 1)^7 has 13,219,703 digits and no digit 6.

Puzzle 793

Puzzle 808
How: A 3-digit palindrome [1, c, 1] in base b means p - 1 = b(b + c), so every
divisor of p - 1 near sqrt(p) gives a base; we chose p = 1 mod 720720 so p - 1
has a huge number of divisors.
Solution: 108864424468801 is palindromic in 227 bases.

Puzzle 830
How: Let the largest prime that still has to cancel decide the next factor, which
builds the product step by step instead of searching.
Solution:
  K = 2012: N = 3520 * 21 * 4 * 2^11 * 3^10 = 35757277839360
  K = 2013: N = 60 * 54 * 2^13 * 3^8 = 174142586880

Puzzle 905
How: Any polynomial that agrees with x + d on the 26 points of PrimeGrid's AP-27
walks along the same primes, so we added c*(x - a0)...(x - a25), which vanishes
there, and chose c to make the 28th value prime.
Solution: P(x) = x + d + 18*(x - a0)(x - a1)...(x - a25), a0 = 224584605939537911,
d = 81292139*223092870; 28 primes by iteration (last one PRP).

Puzzle 908
How: Choose q = p^2 - p + r1 so the first remainder is forced to be r1, then steer
the rest of the chain with the Chinese remainder theorem.
Solution: p = 68516818704324257201795329467591133865835633315118443095966860117,
q = p^2 - p + 199; remainders 199, 197, 193, ..., 5, 2, 1 (34 numbers, PRP).

Puzzle 957
How: Aim for the size where there are enough candidate expressions to expect a
prime while each primality test is still fast.
Solution (PRP):
  1-2+3*(4*5^6)^(7*89)    = 3*62500^623 - 1  (2989 digits)
  -(1+2)+34^(5*6*7+890)   = 34^1100 - 3      (1685 digits)
  -9+8*7^(6*54*3^2)*1     = 8*7^2916 - 9     (2466 digits)
  -9+8*76^(54-3+2^10)     = 8*76^1075 - 9    (2023 digits)

Puzzle 1001
How: For a prime P, SAD(P) = sigma(2P-1) + sigma(2P+1) - 4P, and Q is only odd when
2P - 1 is a square; choosing that square root with many small factors makes Q large.
Solution: P = 391177608521960466267478281835624430526821956454882813,
Q/P = 5.5201061257.

Puzzle 1028
How: (A*n mod (A+1)) is a falling line and a nearly-multiple modulus gives a step
every L values; together they build a staircase through blocks of ten primes in
arithmetic progression with difference 9699690 = 19#.
Solution: (35968n mod 35969) + (9481023n mod 94764426) + (204182n mod 4083624)
+ (1230n mod 36886) + (13256n mod 530202): 49 primes for n = 1..49.

Puzzle 1097
How: Same blocks of ten primes as 1099, glued with Paolo Lava's trick: a sum
sum e_j*((m + j) mod k) can hit any k values by solving a linear system for e_j.
Solution: 9699690*(n mod 10) + sum_j e_j*((floor(n/10) + j) mod 13),
e = [933516151, -104434997, 23700127, -128911085, 56766199, 48442819, -1599797,
-152799521, 32343931, -71698241, 45728731, 44720503, 43956415] / 1014; 130 distinct primes.

Puzzle 1099
How: The record is groups of ten primes in progression with difference 19#; such
progressions are common, so we kept adding groups, each one floor term more.
Solution: H(n) = 35969 + 9699690n + sum_j c_j*floor(n/(10j)): 570 primes for n = 0..569.
Coefficients: https://github.com/basvandorst/praime/blob/main/results/data/1099.txt

Puzzle 1135
How: A number whose last two digits are both odd can never alternate parity; our
quartic is always 17 mod 40, so every value ends in "odd digit, 7".
Solution: 5n^4 - 330n^3 + 9395n^2 - 130510n + 709337, 30 primes for n = 0..29.

Puzzle 1190
How: Take the largest 10-digit prime, 10^10 - 33: eight 9s build 10^10, and 6, 7
with one more 9 give -33. The very largest candidate works, so the case is closed.
Solution: 9999999967 = ((9*9+9)/9)^(9+9/9) - 6*7 + 9.

Puzzle 1209
How: The second step of the lucky sieve removes every number that is 5 mod 6, so
a polynomial that is always 5 mod 6 gives only unlucky values; we searched that
family only.
Solution: -3x^2 + 2799x + 22433, 34 unlucky primes for x = 0..33. (The listed 45
uses absolute values, which turns 5 mod 6 into 1 mod 6: five values are lucky.)

Puzzle 1217
How: Prime gaps stay small for a very long time, so feed half the gap into a
polynomial that is prime on a whole window of small inputs (Euler's, Dress-Landreau).
Solution: with h = (p(n+1) - p(n))/2, h^2 - 79h + 1601 is prime for 1,319,943
successive n; |S(h - 43)| with the Dress-Landreau sextic S for 103,518 n (53 distinct primes).

Puzzle 1230
How: Primes that are 17 or 11 mod 40 have (10/p) = -1, which forces an even period
of 1/p; so we built a polynomial that is always 17 mod 40.
Solution: 5n^4 - 330n^3 + 9395n^2 - 130510n + 709337, 30 primes for n = 0..29.

Puzzle 1251
How: When b + 1 has very many divisors, each way of splitting it into factors
builds a cycle almost by itself.
Solution: b = 518918399 (b + 1 = 2^8*3^4*5^2*7*11*13) gives 477 cycles below 10^9:
https://github.com/basvandorst/praime/blob/main/results/data/1251.txt

Puzzle 179 (Q1)
How: First balance the words (3 + 11 = 7 + 7), then each alphametic is one linear

Puzzle 189

46, 47, 49, 51, 54 and 57, so n0 = 57. Paths:

Puzzle 325 (Q4)
How: Choose the first two primes yourself; the rule A, B then follows from them,

Puzzle 490
How: Rewritten as y^2 + (2^k - 1) = 2^n, this is Ramanujan's equation for k = 3 and
a theorem of Beukers for every other k.
Solution: no other odd n exists, and k = 3 always.

Puzzle 493
How: Expand (sqrt(x) + i*sqrt(y))^p; its size squared is (x + y)^p = p^p.
Solution: yes, solvable for every odd prime p and every split x + y = p.

Puzzle 516 (Q1)
How: A digit product grows far more slowly than the number, so beyond 7 digits it
can never catch up; a direct check below 10^7 does the rest.
Solution: 59 is the only solution (proof).

Puzzle 631
How: Shiu's theorem gives four consecutive primes all 1 mod q, so the three gaps
between them are all multiples of q.
Solution: yes, three consecutive gaps can all be arbitrarily large.

Puzzle 639
How: n = P*(P + 6m + 2) works whenever P, P+2, P+6m, P+6m+2 are all prime.
Solution: checked for every m up to 3.3*10^7; true for all m under the prime
k-tuples conjecture.

Puzzle 677

and there are almost always too many primes 3 mod 4 (Chebyshev's bias).
Solution: the values tried on the page are impossible; the possible range is narrowed.

Puzzle 1204
