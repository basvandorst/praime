1 A equivalent condition
In this ﬁrst section, we shall exhibit a great simpliﬁcation of Crump’s equation.
Proposition 1. Crump’s equation is equivalent to
2zp ≡ 2z mod 2zp
Proof. We have n = 2 zp + 1. This means that 2 zp ≡ − 1 mod n, which implies
that 2 2zp ≡ 1 mod n. The multiplicative order o of 2 modulo n must divide 2 zp.
Furthermore, 1 < 2a < n when 0 < a ≤ zp, which shows that o > zp . The
consequence of this is that o = 2 zp. Crump’s equation is of the form 2 α ≡ 2β
mod n. By deﬁnition of multiplicative order, this is equivalent to α ≡ β mod o.
Replacing the exponents by their values, one gets:
2zp ≡ 2z mod 2 zp
⊓ ⊔
2 Counterexample of second question
Using the previous proposition, one can easily verify that if p = 53 and z = 14,
we have p ≡ 1 mod ( z − 1) but:
2zp ≡ 1332 mod 2 zp
2z ≡ 60 mod 2 zp
3 Solving Crump’s Equation
Lets ﬁx the integer z and let q be an odd prime factor of z. One can then write
z = qαm with m coprime to q. We are now dealing with equation
Eq : 2 zp ≡ 2z mod qα
We denote oqα the multiplicative order of 2 modulo qα and oq the multiplicative
order of 2 modulo q. We have oqα = qβoq with β ≤ α − 1. Equation Eq is then
equivalent to:
zp ≡ p mod oqα ⇔ oqα | z(p − 1)
⇔ oqqβ | qαm(p − 1)
⇔ oq | m(p − 1)
⇔ uq | (p − 1) with uq = oqgcd(oq,m)

The equation Eq=p is naturally satisﬁed by Fermat’s Little Theorem. It remains
to deal with the case q = 2, this case is also naturally satisﬁed. Crump’s Equation
is equivalent to the conjonction of all Eq for q dividing z, i.e. ∀q, uq|(p − 1). Let’s
denote by u(z) the least common multiple of u(q) for all prime q dividing z. We
have proved along the way:
Proposition 2. With previous deﬁnitions, Crump’s Equation is equivalent to:
u(z)|p − 1
One can notice that a divisibility condition is the correct answer to Crump’s
Equation but the correct number that has to divide p − 1 is u(z), which does
not always divide z − 1.
Computing u(z) is not a diﬃcult task as long as factoring z is easy. An
interesting corollary of previous proposition is that Crump’s Equation is satisﬁed
for all prime p when u(z) ∈ { 1, 2}. The following table gives the ﬁrst values of
function u:
u(1) = 1 u(2) = 1 u(3) = 2 u(4) = 1 u(5) = 4
u(6) = 2 u(7) = 6 u(8) = 1 u(9) = 2 u(10) = 1
u(11) = 10 u(12) = 1 u(13) = 12 u(14) = 3 u(15) = 4
u(16) = 1 u(17) = 8 u(18) = 1 u(19) = 18 u(20) = 1
u(21) = 2 u(22) = 5 u(23) = 22 u(24) = 1 u(25) = 4
The values in this table, combined with the above corollary, show that Crump’s
Equation is satisﬁed for all prime p when z ≤ 4 but also for many other values:
8, 10, 12, 16, 18, 20, 21, 24.
