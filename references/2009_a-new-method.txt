Abstract 
 
This is a presentation of new procedures  for calculating the number of prime numbers up to x  
by summation of  prime powers. The idea is that the  natural numbers count themselves, the 
prime numbers in some way do as well. Besides, the procedures are compared with existing 
formulas. In addition to the 1 st  edition the prime twins and k-tuples are now included.  
 
 
Introduction 
 
The object of this paper is to present a new method  to determine the “Anzahl der Primzahlen 
unter einer gegebenen Größe” (Riemann), which means  the number of primes less than a 
given x. This is not about competition with existin g procedures, but only to increase 
knowledge.

Let  log x  be the natural logarithm of x.

The character  ~  means asymptotic equality, i.e. t he quotient of both sides of the equation 
approaches  1  with increasing  x.  Yet the (absolu te) difference of the sides of the equation 
goes to infinity,  which is usually kept secret and  gives the approximation formulas only 
fundamental importance. The formulas are suitable f or the theoretical understanding and a 
rough estimate of dimensions, an accurate evaluatio n of the number of primes cannot be 
expected. If authors speak of “increasing accuracy with increasing x”, this means always 
relative accuracy. Thus the formulas offer little (absolute) accuracy at low computing time. 
 
From formulas in the strict sense of the term expre ssions have to be distinguished which 
contain e.g.  continuous summation or multiplicatio n (Σ, Π) and which should rather be called 
“algorithms” or “calculating prescriptions”.  Examp les are the logarithmus integralis and the 
Riemann formula.  The Hardy algorithm (1979) counts  the prime numbers even exactly. Upon 
closer inspection these algorithms are more or less  a counting.  The “New Method” is a 
mixture.

7.  Extending the method to prime twins 
If we are to use a similar procedure for prime numb er twins  as we used for “singles”, we 
assume that their number is infinite, which for now  must remain speculation, as a proof has 
yet to be found. The fact that there are formulas f or the number of twins just as for single 
primes  is not proof for infiniteness of their numb er. A prime number twin is defined by the 
span d=2 between adjacent prime numbers: d=q-p=2, p <q. This means that (2,3) is not a twin. 
The number of prime twins below x is represented by π 2 (x). Their simplest formula is:

1/(log x)². Strictly speaking, it is a bit more tha n that, because if x is a prime, either x+2 or x-2 
can also be prime. This is taken into account by a 2 “prime twin constant”  c 2 = 0,66016…,  so 
the formula is now, somewhat more precisely,:

Corresponding to the simple primes, there are still  other more precise formulas which to 
elaborate would go beyond the scope of this essay. Read, for instance, what Hardy and 
Littlewood have found on the number of k-tuples; it  corresponds to the integral logarithm 
applying to simple primes. It should be noted at th is point that the exactitude of Sand’s 
summation theorem is somewhere between formula 7.2 and the formula by Hardy and 
Littlewood.  
 
It still needs to be explained what it actually is we count when we count prime number twins. 
The twin is regarded as a unit, as a double, which means it is counted only once. As our 
intention is obviously to sum twins, we want to kno w the value of the individual twin. The 
smaller (p) or the larger (q) partner of the two or  the arithmetic mean value (m) can be taken 
here. Which one we choose  does not really matter f or our scheme, but we have to decide, so 
we go for the impartial m.  Thus follows that the s um of twins here is the sum of their mean 
values m=(p+q)/2.

8.  Extending the method to k-tuples 
 
Let k be an arbitrary but firm natural number > 0. Then a k-tuple is a  set of k consecutive 
prime numbers, where the difference between the gre atest and the smallest is a minimum. 
The difference is even and produces more than 1 tuple. One assumes that then infinitely many 
tuples are produced. Thus e.g. (2,3) is not a twin and (3,5,7) not a triplet. The simple prime 
numbers are 1-tuples, the twins are 2-tuples. In th is context we disregard the structure of the 
tuple, so e.g.  (5,7,11) and (7,11,13) are simply triplets.

9.  Closing remark 
 
 
The reasonable summation of prime numbers resp. pri me powers has certainly not come to 
an end. The procedure seems also to be applicable e .g. to “primeth primes” (prime numbers 
with prime index) or quite generally to “patterns”,  which are repeating sequences of  gaps 
between adjacent prime numbers.  I ask interested p eople to participate in the subject with 
ideas and discoveries of their own. Furthermore I a sk to tell me about maybe still existing 
faults in this script. If there are understanding questions, I will readily answer them. Above all I 
ask to point out other work in this field which has  been done perhaps by other means and 
which have escaped me. I hope to have shown a littl e of the fascination which lies in the 
addition resp. summation of numbers actually made f or multiplication, see Goldbach’s 
conjecture.  Actually mathematics gets truly exciting not before being brushed the wrong way.
