1 Introduction
A magic square of order 3 consists of 9 numbers Aij∈N (usually) ,with i,j∈{ 1,2,3}, such that the sum of the
elements in each row, column and diagonal equals the same number S.

area of a region matches the corresponding value of Aij, given that these numbers constitute a magic square.

2.6 Invariance relation between the parameters
Using 24(A11 +A33) = 16S and 24(A13 +A31) = 16S one gets 2 equations relating the mi. If one adds these
we get 24(A11+A33+A13+A31) = 32S, which simpliﬁes the resulting equation considerably, due to cancellation.
9(m1−m3)(m2−m4)(m1 +m2 +m3 +m4) + 2(m1m2 +m2m3 +m3m4 +m4m1) = 8m1m2m3m4 (2.6.1)
If the Aij are approximately equal we should have: m1m2 +m2m3 +m3m4 +m4m1≈ 0. (2.6.2)
3 Algorithm
The 4 equations (2.5.3) can be written in the form Fi(m1,m 2,m 3,m 4) = 0, each is a polynomial in two of the
mi and of order 2. It is advised to divide both sides of (2.5.1) by S.
Initialise: m :=m0
repeat (i≥ 0):
solve F (mi) +J (mi)·dmi = 0
updatemi+1 :=mi +dmi
until‖dmi‖ is small enough
Boldface characters (F,m,dm, 0) are chosen to indicate that vectors are implied. J equals the Jacobian of F .
In a sense this is just an extension to the Newton-Raphson method. We have mi+1 =mi−J (mi)−1·F (mi).
Instead of computing the inverse of J (mi) it is recommended to use a LU-decomposition. This algorithm
might behave badly, because the intital guess m0must be close enough to the ﬁnal solution in order for this
procedure to work, especially if Jis nearly singular. Note that J (0)is singular.

5 Area semi-magic squares with integer coordinates
It would be nice if the coordinates of the polygons are in N as well. It is possible to prove that the magic sum
must be even. However no solution was found with the property that the diagonal sums where equal to the
magic square sum S. All squares do have the property that the sum of both diagonals is equal to 2S, the sum of
the elements in the rows and columns do add to S, hence the name area semi-magic squares.
WithS = 12u2 all semi-magic squares withu≤ 300 have the property that u has at least two factors. No solutions
withu odd are found. The solutions are in the set{28,56,84,110,112,120,140,168,180,182,196,200,220,224,240,252,280}.
It helps if u is a highly composite number in order to compute the various intersection points.

6 Shoelace formula
The Shoelace formula3 relates the area of a non self-intersecting-polygon to the coordinates in a Cartesian plane.
Applied to 4 vertices of a polygon (visited counterclockwise, like all mathematicians tend to do ):
A = 12((x1y2 +x2y3 +x3y4 +x4y1)− (x1y4 +x2y1 +x3y2 +x4y3)) (6.1)
If this formula is applied to the 4 points deﬁned by the coordinates ( x∗,y ∗) of the boundary points described in
section 2.2 we get the following relation assuming 4 u2 =S/3, a property of area magic squares:
A =S

Which gives a geometric interpretation to part of the invariance relation (the left hand side of (2.6.2)), in a
sense that it measures the deviation in area from the central quadrilateral.
5.1: Geometric interpretation of m1m2 +m2m3 +m3m4 +m4m1
The blue area equals S/3 (assuming an area magic square), the red area equals A. Upon reordering:
m1m2+m2m3+m3m4+m4m1 = 49(1− AS/3) (6.3)
If we drop the link with area magic squares (but retain the ﬁxed points):
m1m2+m2m3+m3m4+m4m1 = 49(1− AT/9) withT the total area of the square (6.4)
If m1 =−m3 or m2 =−m4 we haveA =T/9.
