# Seven lines, one law: what Hardy–Littlewood's singular series predict

> **In short:** One object, the Hardy–Littlewood correlation functions S(B)/ln^k x, predicts the structure of the primes along seven lines (gap statistics, repeated numbers, residue classes, Ulam's diagonals, the gap sequence as a chain, its information content, reproducibility) with no fitted parameter. Tested in windows from 10⁷ to 10¹⁸, it matches the gap shares in every window and the number of primes in intervals of 30 and 60 to the third or fourth decimal (Poisson is off by up to 0.05); summed exactly, it reproduces the number of occurrences of every gap from 2 to 70 below 4·10¹⁸ (10¹⁴ to 10¹⁶ each, counted by Oliveira e Silva) with differences of the size of the square root of the count (largest 2.0√N), and it puts the change of the most common gap from 6 to 30 at 1.7427435·10³⁵ (the same Hardy–Littlewood computation in the literature gives 1.7427436·10³⁵). The same law predicts twin primes, Sophie Germain primes, prime values of n² + 1 and n² + n + 41, and the nearest neighbours of Gaussian and Eisenstein primes; and, as a check of the explicit formula (a theorem), R(x) and the first 2,419 zeros of ζ reproduce the swings of prime counts in blocks (all within 2σ). On top of it come constructions of ours or of ours in part (listed, with the literature, under *What is new here* below): a conditioned expansion (essentially Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan's construction) that sums the law for long gaps, reproducing the counts of every tested gap from 40 to 700 below 4·10¹⁸ to within 0.065 % (0.001 % up to g = 100; the truncated law was 0.55–1.34 % off), accounting for the study's own large-gap finding of round 24 up to a 1.9σ remainder (our synthesis of rounds 51–55; round 51's own reading leaves it open), and giving the distribution of distances between consecutive twin primes up to 1,200 (χ² = 30.7 on 30 bands; the tail beyond misses at z = 3.7, 2.8 with a finer conditioning); a heuristic law for how predictable the next prime is from p's residues mod the primes up to y (roughly 1/(u·ω(u)), u = ln p/ln y; 50 % needs y ≈ p^0.28; out of sample to 0.0016); closed constants for the entropy of the next gap (K = 0.76275) and the information between consecutive gaps (0.26965 and 0.15093 bits), with a coupon-collector formula that makes the memory of the gap sequence unbounded at leading order; and the rigidity of gap-event counts per residue class. Of 716 pre-registered tests of the law with a pass rule 34 miss, against 10.5 expected by chance; counting the exact sums of round 21 in place of the expansion they correct and the repeated large-gap test once, 16 miss in 658, of which 5 have a found cause, 10 are at chance level and 1 is open: gap classes counted per residue class mod 210 scatter 10–30 % less than independent draws from the calibrated law; the long-range Hardy–Littlewood rigidity accounts for half of that at 10⁷–10⁸ (by round 39's own reading not enough: 0.847 predicted against 0.691 ± 0.06) and for about 70 % and 40 % of the smaller deficits at 10¹⁰ and 10¹⁵ (remainders within the noise), the remainder lies in the counts of each gap class per residue class (about 20 % of the predicted variance in both long windows, 1.6–1.7σ each), and the extra evenness of twin counts at coarse moduli is unresolved at about 2σ (rounds 13, 39–44).

**The formula in one place.** Write λ = ln x. For a pattern H = {h₁, …, h_k} (for example {0, 2} for twins):

  P(n + h₁, …, n + h_k all prime, n near x) ≈ S(H) / λ^k,  S(H) = ∏_p (1 − ν_p(H)/p) / (1 − 1/p)^k,

with ν_p(H) the number of different remainders of h₁, …, h_k mod p (Hardy–Littlewood; S({0, 2}) = 1.3203, and S = 0 when some p sees every remainder, as {0, 2, 4} mod 3). Everything in this study follows from it in these ways:
1. *Gaps*: the next prime after p is p + g when p + g is prime and nothing in between is: P(gap = g) = Σ_B (−1)^|B| S({0, g} ∪ B) / λ^(|B|+2), summed over the sets B of points strictly between 0 and g (exact, sections 6 and 10), or its Bahadur expansion (section 3), or, far more accurate for long gaps, the same expansion after averaging exactly over p mod 30030 (rounds 47–55).
2. *Counts in an interval of length H*: Var/mean = 1 + (2/(Hλ)) Σ_{h<H} (H − h)(S({0, h}) − 1), about 1 − (ln H − 1 + γ + ln 2π)/λ (section 4).
3. *Counts per residue class*: the same sum over points 210k apart, with S restricted to the primes p ∤ 210; it makes the counts per class far more even than chance (round 39).
4. *Predicting the next gap*: knowing p mod every prime q ≤ y, the best guess (the first y-rough number after p) is right with probability ⟨S_{>y}({0, h₁})⟩ / (ln p · ∏_{q≤y}(1 − 1/q) · e^γ · ω(u)), u = ln p / ln y, ω Buchstab's function (roughly 1/(u·ω(u)) for large y; a heuristic product of Hardy–Littlewood and Buchstab); about h₂(hit)/hit bits remain (section 15).
5. *The entropy of the next gap*: H = log₂(e·ln p) − K/ln 2 bits with K = ln(2C₂) + Σ_{p>2} ln((p − 1)/(p − 2))/(p − 1) = 0.76275 (round 52).
6. *The information between consecutive gaps*: I → Σ_{p≥3} I_p = 0.26965 bits, a closed sum over primes of local residue averages (round 56; the data approach it as about 0.95 bit/λ, as the discrete leading-order law does, so its pre-registered 0.02-bit reading at 10¹² failed).
7. *What the gap before last adds*: I(g_{n+1}; g_{n−1} | g_n) → J_∞ = 0.15093 bits, the same kind of closed sum over primes (round 58).
8. *Memory of the gap sequence*: the last k gaps carry I_k = Σ_q [H_q(1) − E H_q(m_k)] about the next (a coupon-collector sum from a hidden forbidden residue mod each prime), 0.27, 0.42, 0.53, … bits, growing without bound like ln ln k at leading order (x → ∞ first; round 61, theory; on gap residues mod 30 the data follow its shape for k = 1–4 but exceed the registered q = 3, 5 comparator by up to 0.017 bits, so round 62's 0.01-bit reading failed; with q = 7, which the finite-λ law also needs, the differences at k = 1–3 are 0.0014–0.0076 bits).

**What is new here**, and what is not (after a literature search on each item; it was not exhaustive):
- *A conditioned expansion of the gap law* (rounds 47–55, 63): average exactly over p mod 30030 (Chinese remainder theorem) and expand only the weak correlations of the larger primes. The construction itself is essentially known: Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan ([arXiv:1603.03720](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.03720), 2016) condition on n mod q with the same ρ₀ = q/(φ(q) log n) and expand the avoidance of the interior points in the connected singular series of Montgomery and Soundararajan; Granville's model and Banks, Ford and Tao ([arXiv:1908.08613](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08613)) contain the averaging over residue classes. What is ours is its use as the expansion point for the whole gap law of long gaps (order 3 with the overlap correction) and the precision tests below. It reproduces the exact sums for the gaps 40–90, the counts of every tested gap 40–700 below 4·10¹⁸ to within 0.065 % (0.001 % up to g = 100; the truncated law: 0.55–1.34 %) and the counts of large gaps at 10⁸–10¹⁰ and, out of sample, at 10¹² (round 63: 4,341 against 4,296); with it the study's own large-gap finding (round 24) is accounted for up to a 1.9σ remainder in the first occurrences (a synthesis; round 51's pre-registered reading says open). Applied to twin events it gives the distribution of the distances between consecutive twin primes up to 1,200, χ² = 30.7 on 30 bands over 2.96 million gaps at 10⁹, against 2,294 for an exponential with the Hardy–Littlewood pair factor; the tail beyond 1,200 misses at z = 3.7 (2.8 with y = 17), the order-2 truncation; out of sample at 10¹¹, χ² = 29.7 on the same 30 bands over 20.5 million gaps, tail +0.24 % (z = 3.1), all of it beyond 2,400, and at 10¹³ χ² = 19.3 with the tail fitting too (z = +0.04) (rounds 59, 64, 67; earlier work on twin gaps: Wolf, Kelly–Pilling).
- *The predictability of the next prime* (rounds 45, 46, 50; the ingredients, Buchstab's density of rough numbers and Granville's model, are classical, the predictor framing and the threshold were not found in the literature): knowing p mod every prime up to y, the best guess is right with probability ⟨S_{>y}⟩/(ln p ∏_{q≤y}(1 − 1/q) e^γ ω(u)), u = ln p/ln y (a heuristic product of Hardy–Littlewood and Buchstab; out of sample at 10¹⁵ to 0.0016), and about h₂(hit)/hit bits remain (for y ≳ 100; below that the gap is more predictable).
- *Three information constants of the gap sequence* (rounds 52, 56–58; not found in the literature, the closest being Lacasa et al., Entropy 20 (2018) 131, [arXiv:1802.08349](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08349), who use Hardy–Littlewood for block entropies of gap residues without closed constants): the entropy of the next gap is log₂(e ln p) − K/ln 2 bits with K = ln(2C₂) + Σ_{p>2} ln((p − 1)/(p − 2))/(p − 1) = 0.762747, the information one gap carries about the next tends to Σ_{p≥3} I_p = 0.26965 bits, and the gap before last adds J_∞ = 0.15093 bits; all three are closed sums over primes, and one coupon-collector formula, from the random-sieve reading of the singular series, gives the information carried by the last k gaps for every k, unbounded as ln ln k at leading order (round 61), and the same construction gives the entropy of the gap between consecutive twin primes, with K_tw = 0.11434 (round 60). The data approach them slowly (the pre-registered readings of rounds 56 and 62 failed, the second mostly because its comparator left out the prime 7; with the right comparator fixed in advance, rounds 68 and 69 at 10¹² pass), following the discrete leading-order law at each λ; for K the full law was computed out to λ = 100 (0.004 bits from it there), and a fit puts its limit within 0.002 bits.
- *The rigidity of counts per residue class* (rounds 36, 39–44): the long-range Hardy–Littlewood sums predict, with no fitted parameter, how evenly gap events spread over the classes mod 210 (four-point sums; not found in the literature). For the primes themselves the mechanism is known: Friedlander and Goldston (Quart. J. Math. 47, 1996) derive the variance across classes from a prime-pair conjecture (for large q), and for small q it is the explicit formula with L-function zeros. A remainder of about 20 % of the variance of gap-event counts and the evenness of twin counts at coarse moduli stay open at about 2σ.

## 1. The question and the method

The protocol asked for seven independent lines (numerical, geometric, statistical, algebraic, dynamic, information-theoretic, computational) and for every result to be checked against known theory: the prime number theorem, residue classes, analytic number theory.

- **Windows.** train = [10⁷, 10⁸) (5,096,876 primes); A = [10¹⁰, 10¹⁰ + 5·10⁷) (2,171,134); B = [10¹², 10¹² + 5·10⁷) (1,808,833); C = [10¹⁵, 10¹⁵ + 2·10⁷) (578,837; opened only in round 3). Each window is split into 10 equal blocks.
- **The test.** For a statistic s with prediction T: r_b = s_b − T_b on the ten blocks, z = mean(r)/(sd(r)/√10). The theory value is recomputed per block (λ = ln x changes inside a window). The prediction fits when |z| ≤ 3. The noise comes from the primes themselves, not from a model.
- **No sieve models.** Fake primes built from a sieve explain nothing about why the primes behave as they do, so the only fakes are coin flips with chance 1/ln n (Cramér) and the window's own gaps in random order. They answer one question: is there structure at all.
- **Pre-registration.** Before each round a file in [preregister/](writeup/data/study/preregister/) fixed the formulas, the cut-offs and the pass rule; the commit that adds each file comes before the commit with its results (rounds 1–11: f802663, 3f6d791, ee457c0, f9d6fee, 4a15168, 6de4fb1, 204ca7e, 8cd9279, 5cbecb5, 520ab35, 92d808c). The clock times written inside the files are estimates and run 10–25 minutes later than these commits. The commit order shows the order of committing; since some result commits followed their pre-registration within a minute, the repository alone cannot prove that each plan was fixed before its computation, and the pre-registration should be read as stated, not as verified. Round 2 was built on the train window (single-gap shares only) and then run on A and B unchanged.
- **Added after seeing data** (marked where they appear): the grid term of the variance (section 4; round 2 had registered the unaligned pair sum, which missed H = 50 at train: 0.70049 against 0.70526), the variance law along quadratics (section 5; tested afterwards on fresh data in round 9), and the estimator-bias correction of the information statistics (section 6; the registered test missed at C before it).

## 2. Round 1: the textbook formulas ([round1.txt](writeup/data/study/preregister/round1.txt), [verdicts_r1.json](writeup/data/study/verdicts_r1.json))

## 3. Round 2: one law for consecutive primes ([theory2.py](writeup/code/study/theory2.py), [round2.txt](writeup/data/study/preregister/round2.txt))

**Step 1 (conjecture: Hardy–Littlewood).** For a finite set B, the numbers n + b (b ∈ B) are all prime with density S(B)/λ^|B|, λ = ln n, S(B) = ∏_p (1 − ν_p(B)/p)/(1 − 1/p)^|B|, ν_p = number of residues of B mod p. Computation: for p larger than the span of B every residue is distinct, so S(B) = ∏_(p ≤ span) (…) · G_k(span) with G_k(P) = ∏_(p > P) (1 − k/p)/(1 − 1/p)^k computed once (primes to 2·10⁷ plus the tail k(k−1)/(2X ln X)). Check: S({0, 2}) = 1.3203236, twice the twin prime constant 0.6601618 (computed).

**Step 2 (identity).** B is a run of consecutive primes when its interior I = {0 < m < span, m ∉ B} has no prime. By inclusion–exclusion, P(B) = Σ_(A ⊆ I) (−1)^|A| S(B ∪ A)/λ^(|B|+|A|). Divided by S(B)/λ^|B|, this is the chance that a 0/1 process X_m on I with correlation functions E∏_(m∈A) X_m = S(B ∪ A)/(S(B) λ^|A|) avoids every point.

**Step 3 (a wrong turn, kept because it explains the size of the misses).** A first version exponentiated the Ursell functions of distinct interior points, exp(−Σκ₁ + Σκ₂ − …), with κ the connected parts of the correlation functions over distinct points. On the train window this converged, but to the wrong values: 1.6 times too high at gap 90. The exponential formula for set functions assumes the blocks of a partition can overlap; for 0/1 variables X² = X, and the diagonal terms are lost. (The true factorial-cumulant series of the number of interior primes, ln P(N = 0) = Σ(−1)^k κ_[k]/k!, is exact and contains these diagonal terms; version 1 was not that series.) At gap 6 the interior is {2, 4} and S(0, 2, 4, 6) = 0, so the exact chance is 1 − x − y (x = ρ₂/λ, y = ρ₄/λ), while the exponential gives e^(−x−y−xy).

**Step 4 (derived; heuristic truncation).** Expand around independent interior numbers instead. With p_m = ρ_m/λ, ρ_m = S(B ∪ {m})/S(B), and Z_m = (p_m − X_m)/(1 − p_m), which has mean 0, exactly

  E∏(1 − X_m) = ∏(1 − p_m) · E∏(1 + Z_m) = ∏(1 − p_m) · Σ_A E[∏_(m∈A) Z_m].

The single terms vanish. The connected parts of the pair, triple and quadruple terms are c_ab/((1 − p_a)(1 − p_b)), −κ₃/∏(1 − p) and +κ₄/∏(1 − p), where c, κ₃, κ₄ are the joint cumulants of the X's (set-partition formulas in `Pattern.log_avoid`). Exponentiating the connected parts:

  ln P(B) = ln S(B) − |B| ln λ + Σ ln(1 − p_m) + Σ c̃ − Σ κ̃₃ + Σ κ̃₄.

At gap 6 the exact value is (1 − x)(1 − y)(1 + t) with t = −xy/((1 − x)(1 − y)), while the exponentiated form gives (1 − x)(1 − y)e^t: a relative error of 9·10⁻⁶ at λ = 17.8, so the overlap problem is pushed to higher order, not removed. The share per prime is P(B)·λ, averaged over the window with weight 1/ln t (integrated in u = ln t).

**Step 5 (computed, train only, the development data).** The prediction divided by the actual share at train: gap 30 gives 1.165 / 1.017 / 1.005 / 1.003 at orders 1 to 4; gap 90 gives 2.06 / 1.09 / 1.01 / 0.99. Order 1 alone (independent interior numbers with their Hardy–Littlewood densities) is the form already used in [FINDINGS.txt](FINDINGS.txt) for Euler–Rodríguez chains; it is 1–16 % off at gaps 6–30. Order 4 was frozen for spans ≤ 120 and order 3 above, before the holdouts were opened.

**The truncation error (from the review; independent code in [review/c3_bahadur.py](writeup/code/study/review/c3_bahadur.py) → [c3_bahadur.out](writeup/data/study/review/c3_bahadur.out), [c3_bahadur_big.out](writeup/data/study/review/c3_bahadur_big.out)).** For gaps up to 42 the live interior has at most 20 points, so the exact Hardy–Littlewood inclusion–exclusion over all its subsets can be summed. Order 4 lies above the exact value by 0.017 % (gap 12), 0.062 % (18), 0.185 % (30), 0.254 % (36) and 0.322 % (42) at λ = 17.8, and by 0.012 % at gap 42 at λ = 34.5. So the agreement of 0.1–0.3 % with the primes at train is at the size of the truncation error itself, and the error grows with h/λ: it is the natural suspect for the tail miss of section 6. **The error is a bias, not only truncation** ([r22c.py](writeup/code/study/r22c.py) → [r22c.json](writeup/data/study/r22c.json)): against the exact sums of round 22, orders 3 and 4 at λ = 17.8 are +0.72 % and +0.69 % off at gap 52 and +1.41 % and +1.31 % at gap 70, and Aitken's acceleration of orders 2–4 changes little (+0.69 %, +1.31 %). At gaps 52 and 70 orders 3 and 4 agree with each other but not with the exact law, so there the series converges to a wrong value: exponentiating the connected parts adds products of overlapping clusters (κ(a, b)·κ(a, c)) that the true sum over disjoint blocks does not have (at other gaps the orders still move, and the sign of these terms is not general: the correction below is positive at g = 300). The Bahadur form makes these terms small (second order in the connected correlations) without removing them; the exact sums of rounds 21–23 have no such bias. The next term of the polymer (cluster) expansion of the true sum over disjoint blocks, −½ [Σ_B κ̃_B² + Σ_i Σ_(j≠k) κ̃_ij κ̃_ik] for pair clusters, removes most of it: at λ = 17.8 the bias at gap 70 falls from +1.3 % to +0.26 %, at λ = 23 from +0.37 % to +0.05 % (`overlap_correction` in theory2.py; checked against the exact sums, no data involved).

**Everything else is a sum over patterns.** The share of gap h is P({0, h})·λ; two equal gaps sum P({0, h, 2h}) over h; the class share mod q sums the gap law over q | h and divides by the total (the law sums to 1.0010, 1.0005, 1.0003 and 1.0002 at train, A, B and C); the joint law of two consecutive gaps is P({0, h₁, h₁ + h₂}); mutual information, entropies and the Markov gain are functions of the joint law.

## 4. Counts on a grid ([theory2.py](writeup/code/study/theory2.py), `var_ratio_aligned`)

The variance of the count in [n, n + H) needs only pairs. With the exact Hardy–Littlewood pair sum, Var/mean = 1 − 1/λ + (2/(Hλ)) Σ_(h<H) (H − h)(S(0, h) − 1). This still missed H = 50 (0.7005 against 0.7053). The reason is the grid: the intervals start at multiples of H, so position a inside every interval is the same residue mod 2 and mod 5, and only the pairs (a, a + h) inside one interval count. With u(a) = 1 when n + a is prime to 10, R(h) the pair factor without the primes 2 and 5 (R(h) = R₀ ∏_(p|h, p≠2,5) (p − 1)/(p − 2), R₀ = C₂/(0.6/0.64)) and c = 1/(½·⅘)²:

  Var/mean = 1 + (2c Σ_h R(h) N_h − H²)/(Hλ),  N_h = Σ_a u(a) u(a + h).

(Derivation: E c² = Σ_a p_a + 2 Σ_(a<b) E[X_a X_b] with p_a = c^(1/2) u(a)/λ and E[X_a X_b] = c u(a) u(b) R(b − a)/λ²; Σ_a p_a = H/λ.) For H = 50 this gives 0.70556 / 0.77451 / 0.81207 at train / A / B against 0.70526 / 0.77551 / 0.81187. ## 5. Ulam's diagonals scatter less than Poisson ([theory_geo.py](writeup/code/study/theory_geo.py))

On each quadratic f the count of prime values has mean Σ_n C(f)/ln f(n) (Bateman–Horn), and round 1 found that the counts scatter with χ²/curve = 0.386 instead of 1. The same pair argument as in section 4, with polynomials:

  Var/E = 1 − C/λ + (2/(C·N·λ)) Σ_d (N − d)(C_d − C²)  (λ-weights done exactly per n in the code),

where C_d is the Bateman–Horn constant of the pair {f(x), f(x + d)}: C_d/C² = ∏_p (1 − ω_d(p)/p)/(1 − w(p)/p)², w(p) the number of roots of f mod p and ω_d(p) = |R ∪ (R − d)|. For large p the factor is generic (ω = 2w) except at p | d (ω = w: the shifted curve has the same roots) and at p | 16d² − D, D = b² − 16c (ω = 3: one root of f(x + d) is a root of f(x)). For 4n² + 1 the neighbours repel: C₁/C² = 0.5175 (brute-force product to 3,000: 0.51751). The predicted χ²/curve is 0.406 at train, 0.694 at A and 0.831 at B, against 0.386, 0.715 and 0.721 (block z −0.0, 1.1, −1.0). This is a Montgomery–Soundararajan-type law for a curve instead of an interval: the counts are rigid because values a short step apart along the curve share prime factors in a structured way. The law was derived after round 1 had shown the low scatter, so the three windows above are not an independent test; round 9 below is. The collision primes p | 16d² − D are included up to 3·10⁴; including all of them moves the train value by about +0.001 (review, c5_ulam.out).

**Rounds 47–55 and 63: a conditioned expansion and the large gaps** (the Lemke Oliver–Soundararajan conditioning, used as the expansion point for long gaps; [r47.py](writeup/code/study/r47.py), [r47_check3.py](writeup/code/study/r47_check3.py), [r48.py](writeup/code/study/r48.py) → [r47_check_y13.json](writeup/data/study/r47_check_y13.json), [r47_check3_y13.json](writeup/data/study/r47_check3_y13.json), [r47_u_y13.json](writeup/data/study/r47_u_y13.json), [r47_u_y17.json](writeup/data/study/r47_u_y17.json), [r47_u3_y13.json](writeup/data/study/r47_u3_y13.json), [r48.json](writeup/data/study/r48.json); [round47.txt](writeup/data/study/preregister/round47.txt), [round48.txt](writeup/data/study/preregister/round48.txt)). The Bahadur series of section 3 expands around independent interior points, but the correlations of the small primes are strong, and that is why it converges slowly for long gaps, worst for multiples of 6. The singular series factorises exactly, S(B) = S_{≤y}(B)·S_{>y}(B), and by the Chinese remainder theorem the small-prime part is exactly an average over the classes a of p mod ∏_{q≤y} q: in each class an interior point is either impossible or "rough", and a rough point is prime with chance ρ₀ = 1/(λ ∏_{q≤y}(1 − 1/q)) times a weak factor. So

  P(gap = g | p prime) = E_a[ 1(a + g rough) · ρ₀ S_{>y}({0, g}) · Π_a ],

with Π_a the chance that none of the rough interior points of class a is prime, a measure whose only correlations come from the primes q > y (at most about 1/(q − 2) ≤ 1/15 for y = 13). The classes are averaged exactly (y = 13: 5,760 classes) and only Π_a is expanded, in Bahadur form with the overlap (polymer) correction. Against the exact sums for g = 74–90 (r28.json) the error is at most 0.27 % at second order and 0.04 % at third order with the overlap correction (λ = 17.8; 0.011 % at λ = 23), against 0.7–1.7 % for the unconditioned order 4 (0.3–0.9 % with its overlap correction). Order 3 alone is worse than order 2; the agreement comes from order 3 and the overlap term together, which partly cancel (+0.66 % and −1.26 % at g = 150), and it is validated only for g = 74–90; for g = 240–300 at λ = 16 the step from order 2 is 11–19 % in the share, so the method is least certain there, and the next Mayer term was not computed. The construction is essentially that of Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan ([arXiv:1603.03720](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.03720)), who condition on n mod q with ρ₀ = q/(φ(q) log n) and expand the avoidance of the interior points in the connected singular series of Montgomery and Soundararajan, for biases between consecutive residues; the averaging over residue classes is also in Granville's model and in Banks, Ford and Tao ([arXiv:1908.08613](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08613)). What is ours is its use as the expansion point for the whole law of long gaps and the tests below.

## 8. The same law on a parabola: Landau's n² + 1 ([theory_landau.py](writeup/code/study/theory_landau.py), [round5.txt](writeup/data/study/preregister/round5.txt), [verdicts_landau.json](writeup/data/study/verdicts_landau.json))

Whether n² + 1 is prime infinitely often is Landau's fourth problem. Here the question is how the n with n² + 1 prime follow each other. The singular series of a set of shifts B is now Bateman–Horn's: S_f(B) = ∏_p (1 − ω_B(p)/p)/(1 − 1/p)^|B| with ω_B(p) = |{(r − b) mod p}|, r running over the roots of x² + 1 mod p (two roots for p ≡ 1 mod 4, none for p ≡ 3 mod 4, the root 1 for p = 2). For large p the factor is generic, (1 − |B|(1 + χ₄(p))/p)/(1 − 1/p)^|B|, except where two shifted roots coincide: r − b = r′ − b′ with r − r′ = ±2i, that is p | (b − b′)² + 4. Check: S_f({0}) = 1.37279, the Bateman–Horn constant of n² + 1 being 1.37281 (the product converges conditionally; it runs to 2·10⁷ here). ## 9. Second differences, and the gap sequence in bits ([r6.py](writeup/code/study/r6.py), [r7.py](writeup/code/study/r7.py), [round6.txt](writeup/data/study/preregister/round6.txt), [round7.txt](writeup/data/study/preregister/round7.txt))

**Second differences (numerical line).** The second difference of the primes, p_(n+1) − 2p_n + p_(n−1) = g_n − g_(n−1), has P(d = k) = Σ_h P(0, h, 2h + k). The 20 tests (k = −6, −2, 0, 2, 6 in four windows) all fit ([verdicts_r6.json](writeup/data/study/verdicts_r6.json); 0 block-test misses, 0.3 expected). Two gaps that differ by 2 are 2.4 times as common as two equal gaps (0.0683 and 0.0285 at train): {0, h, 2h} survives mod 3 only when 6 | h (theorem), and the law carries the rest. P(d = k) = P(d = −k) holds exactly for the law, because (0, h, 2h + k) and (0, h + k, 2h + k) are mirror images and S is invariant under reflection; the primes agree within the noise (0.068348 and 0.068196 at train).

**Where those bits live: a hidden chain on the residues (dynamic line).** Two previous gaps can reveal p_n mod 3 where one cannot (a gap ≡ 0 mod 6 hides it, the gap before may show it), and likewise mod 5 and 7. (identity check: the average of S_a(B) over all a mod 30 is S(B) to 10⁻¹⁶), and the same Bahadur expansion gives the gap law P_a(h) of each state. ## 11. The Gaussian primes a + bi (a² + b² prime) are a point process in the plane. Hardy–Littlewood for ℤ[i] uses the singular series S_G(B) = ∏_π (1 − ν_π(B)/N(π))/(1 − 1/N(π))^|B| over the Gaussian primes π up to units: 1 + i (residue x + y mod 2), the two primes over each p ≡ 1 (mod 4) (residues x + y·i₀ and x − y·i₀ mod p, i₀² ≡ −1), and the inert p ≡ 3 (mod 4) (residues (x mod p, y mod p), norm p²). By the prime ideal theorem a lattice point of norm N is a Gaussian prime with chance (4/π)/ln N (0.05363 predicted, 0.05366 counted in the first box). **Where the low χ² sits** (diagnostics, not pre-registered; [r13e.py](writeup/code/study/r13e.py) → [r13e.json](writeup/data/study/r13e.json), [r13f.py](writeup/code/study/r13f.py) → [r13f.json](writeup/data/study/r13f.json)). For gap 2 every admissible residue has the same law, so that class compares the twin counts per residue class with the prime counts per class. For the primes this is classical: by the explicit formula (under GRH) the counts in progressions deviate by an amount of order √x/(√φ(q) ln x), times a factor that grows slowly with q, against √(x/(φ(q) ln x)) for independent counts: a variance ratio of order 1/ln x (about 0.05 near 10⁸). **Round 39: the rigidity of the residue tables, derived** ([r39.py](writeup/code/study/r39.py) → [r39.json](writeup/data/study/r39.json), [round39.txt](writeup/data/study/preregister/round39.txt); written before the computation but not blind, since round 13 had measured the values; checked against the general singular-series code in [r39_check.py](writeup/code/study/r39_check.py) → [r39_check.out](writeup/data/study/r39_check.out), largest difference 1·10⁻⁸). For a row a (mod 210) and a gap g, let X count the gap-g events (p, p + g consecutive) with p ≡ a, Y the primes ≡ a, and P the law's share P_a(g). The χ² cell is (X − P·Y)²/(P·m), with E[(X − P·Y)²] = Var X − 2P·Cov(X, Y) + P²·Var Y, and each term is a long-range Hardy–Littlewood sum over the points of the class, 210k apart: Var Y over the pairs {0, d}, Var X over the four-point sets {0, g, d, d + g}, Cov over the three-point sets {0, g, ±d}. The factors at p | 210 cancel, so each correlation is a closed product over p ≥ 11 (the p dividing k, 210k − g or 210k + g change it; the last two are found by dividing out the small primes, the cofactor being one large prime); the density of one class follows 1/ln x across the window. For g = 2 and 4 this is exact within the law; for g ≥ 6 the emptiness of two interiors 210k apart is taken as independent (heuristic). No parameter is fitted; the multinomial null is 1 − P per cell. The prime counts per class come out at Var/mean 0.074 (train; round 36 had 0.084 with a constant λ). Per gap class the predicted mean of the cell (measured/predicted at train: gaps 2, 4, 6: 0.29/0.42, 0.31/0.42, 0.31/0.49; null 0.76–0.79) rises towards the null for long gaps, as measured (section above); at train the measured values lie below the prediction in 15 of the 19 classes from gap 2 to 38 (sign test p ≈ 0.02), so the shortfall is spread over the classes ([review7/perclass.out](writeup/data/study/review7/perclass.out)). ## 13. Sophie Germain primes: two forms with different slopes ([r14.py](writeup/code/study/r14.py), [round14.txt](writeup/data/study/preregister/round14.txt), [verdicts_r14.json](writeup/data/study/verdicts_r14.json))

A Sophie Germain prime p has 2p + 1 prime too: two linear forms, x and 2x + 1. Write the form x + b as the token 2b and the form 2x + 2m + 1 as the token 2m + 1; for an odd prime q both have the root −t/2 (mod q), so the number of roots mod q is the number of distinct tokens mod q, and only q = 2 is special (only the forms x + b have roots there). ## 14. What the seven lines turn out to be

**The seven questions of the protocol, answered.**

- **Numerical, statistical, algebraic, dynamic, information-theoretic**: five views of one object, the law of runs of consecutive primes. Its only input is the singular series; its only scale is ln x. The textbook versions miss because they truncate it: the Poisson factor e^(−h/ln x) ignores that the numbers between two primes are correlated with them (round 1: up to 30 %), exponentiating the Ursell functions of distinct points mistreats X² = X (step 3), and the Montgomery–Soundararajan asymptotics ignore the grid (H = 50).
- **Geometric**: Ulam's diagonals are the same law for polynomials: the means are Bateman–Horn and the scatter is set by the Bateman–Horn constants of polynomial pairs. In the plane, the Gaussian primes follow the same expansion with the singular series of ℤ[i] (section 11).
- **Computational**: the pre-registered windows and the block test. Windows from 10⁷ to 10¹⁸ (short windows at 10¹⁰, 10¹², 10¹⁵ and 10¹⁸, and the decade windows [10⁷, 10⁸), [10⁸, 10⁹), [10⁹, 10¹⁰) and [10¹⁰, 2·10¹⁰)), several opened only after the law was frozen.
- **Open**: the tail of the twin-gap law beyond 1,200 (z = 3.7, round 59); a remainder of the rigidity of gap-event counts per residue class (rounds 39–41); the evenness of twin counts per class at the coarse moduli (rounds 42–44).
- **What is not new** (for what is, see *What is new here* at the top): the Hardy–Littlewood and Bateman–Horn conjectures, Gallagher's Poisson law, the Lemke Oliver–Soundararajan bias and the Montgomery–Soundararajan variance are literature; that a parameter-free Hardy–Littlewood count fits gap counts has been used before (Brent 1974 for small gaps; Odlyzko, Rubinstein and Wolf 1999 for jumping champions). Hardy–Littlewood inclusion–exclusion for differences of consecutive primes, with lower-order terms, has been studied (for example by Goldston and Ledoan, and by Funkhouser, Goldston and Ledoan for large gaps); those papers were not read for this study. What this study adds, as far as the files in this archive show, is the Bahadur form and its accuracy (0.1–0.3 % for gaps up to 30 at four scales, which is the size of its own bias at train), the grid term in the variance, the variance law along quadratics, and the same expansion for twin primes, for n² + 1 and for Gaussian primes; the literature for these was not searched beyond this archive.

## 15. How predictable is the next prime? (rounds 45, 46, 50, 52, 56–58, 61, 62, 66, 68, 69)

**The question.** Knowing p, can one predict the next gap? Guessing the commonest gap per class is right 13 %, 17 % and 20 % of the time if p mod 6, 30 or 210 is known (near 10⁹). The gap is fixed once p mod q is known for every prime q ≤ √p, since that decides which of p + 2, p + 4, … are prime. So the real question is how the predictability grows with y, the largest prime whose residue is known.

**The law (own construction from classical parts: Buchstab's density of rough numbers, Granville's model, the Hardy–Littlewood pair factor; a literature search found the parts but not this predictor).** With p mod q known for all q ≤ y, the best guess is the first y-rough number after p (no prime factor ≤ y); it is right exactly when that number is prime. So hit(y) is the share of primes among the y-rough numbers that follow a prime:

  **hit(y) = ⟨S_{>y}({0, h₁})⟩ / ( ln p · ∏_{q≤y} (1 − 1/q) · e^γ · ω(u) ),  u = ln p / ln y,**

where ω is Buchstab's function (it counts rough numbers correctly up to √p, where Mertens' product would give e^γ/2 = 0.89 instead of 1), and ⟨S_{>y}({0, h₁})⟩ is the Hardy–Littlewood pair factor of the primes above y, averaged over the first rough distance h₁ (it carries the fact that p itself is prime: for y = 2 the guess is p + 2, right with chance S({0, 2})/ln p = 1.32/ln p, not 2/ln p). (This is a heuristic of Granville's type: Maier's theorem shows that prime counts in short intervals follow the local density of rough numbers rather than the Cramér density, which is the Buchstab factor's role here; that the first rough number after a prime is prime with the average chance times the pair factor is assumed, and the data at 10⁹–10¹⁵ agree with it at this precision.) Simply: **hit ≈ 1/(u·ω(u))**, a function of u alone; to guess right with probability ρ one needs the residues mod all primes up to about p^(ρ·e^(−γ)) ≈ p^(0.56ρ) (for ρ not close to 1), so 50 % needs y ≈ p^0.28.

**Round 61: at leading order the gap sequence has unbounded memory** ([r61.py](writeup/code/study/r61.py) → [r61.json](writeup/data/study/r61.json); theory only; the information formula is our derivation, the model behind it is not). At leading order the joint law of consecutive gaps factorises over the primes q, and the local factor at q is proportional to the number of residues mod q that no prime position occupies. That is exactly the random-sieve reading of the singular series (Granville's modified Cramér model; Banks, Ford and Tao, [arXiv:1908.08613](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08613)): a hidden residue a mod q, a ≡ −p (where the multiples of q sit; uniform, a ≠ 0 relative to the current prime), and prime positions independent and uniform on the other q − 1 residues. Gaps then inform about a only through which residues have appeared: with m distinct residues seen, a is uniform on the q − m others, and the next position has chance 1/(q − 1) on each seen residue and (q − m − 1)/((q − 1)(q − m)) on each unseen one. The count m among the current prime and the k before it grows by one with chance (q − 1 − m)/(q − 1). So the information the last k gaps carry about the next is a coupon-collector sum:

  **I_k = Σ_{q≥3} [ H_q(1) − E H_q(m_k) ]**,  H_q(m) the entropy of that predictive law.

It reproduces both constants above, I₁ = round 56's I_∞ (to 2e-08 nats) and I₂ − I₁ = round 58's J_∞ (to 7e-08), and gives every k:

| previous gaps k | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 10 | 30 | 100 | 1,000 | 10,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I_k (bits) | 0.270 | 0.421 | 0.527 | 0.678 | 0.903 | 1.232 | 1.583 | 2.150 | 2.595 |

As k → ∞ the hidden residue is revealed for every q, and the limit Σ_q ((q − 2)/(q − 1)) ln((q − 1)/(q − 2)) diverges like ln ln: 1.95, 2.53, 2.94, 3.26 bits for the primes up to 10², 10³, 10⁴, 10⁵. Correspondingly I_k grows like ln ln k (from k = 1,000 to 10,000 by 0.31 nats, against ln ln 10⁴ − ln ln 10³ = 0.29). So, at leading order (x → ∞ first, then k → ∞), the prime gap sequence has unbounded memory: each further past gap still says something about the next one, ever less, and the total grows without bound, but only like ln ln k (asymptotically: from k = 10 to 100 the growth is 0.47 nats against ln ln's 0.69). At any finite x the information is capped by the entropy of the next gap, log₂(e ln x) − 1.1 bits (4.9 bits at 10¹⁰), and the large q behind the divergence need residues that are uniform at the given λ, which they are not for q comparable to λ. (It is the same divergence as in the predictability law: knowing p mod every prime up to y removes about log₂ ln y bits.) At finite x the 1/λ terms of rounds 56 and 58 come on top; only k = 1, 2 have been tested against data.

The model gets the shape: every further gap adds information, decreasingly (data increments 0.163, 0.112, 0.083 bits, model 0.158, 0.107, 0.079), on the way to the λ → ∞ saturation of 0.8113 bits for these residues. But the data keep about 0.004 bits more per extra gap, the same sign as round 56's remainder above the discrete law; it was natural to suspect the finite-λ interior correlations, but a diagnostic after the failed reading ([r62b.py](writeup/code/study/r62b.py) → [r62b.json](writeup/data/study/r62b.json), [r62c.py](writeup/code/study/r62c.py) → [r62c.json](writeup/data/study/r62c.json)) shows that the registered comparator was itself truncated: at finite λ the weights e^(−g/λ) fall by e^(−30/λ) ≈ 0.27 per step inside a residue class mod 30, so the class nearly fixes g mod 7 and the prime 7 belongs in the finite-λ leading-order law. Including q = 7 brings the differences at k = 1, 2, 3 from 0.0033, 0.0085 and 0.0128 to 0.0014, 0.0047 and 0.0076 bits (k = 3 from the sixteenth review, [review16/](writeup/code/study/review16/) → [r62_model.out](writeup/data/study/review16/r62_model.out)), below the 0.01 rule at k = 3, while q = 11 adds only 0.0002 at k = 1; the remainder is presumably the interior structure of the gaps (not computed). The sixteenth review also replicated the data on [10¹⁰, 1.1·10¹⁰) (differences +0.0033 … +0.0169).

**What this answers.** The next prime cannot be predicted from p alone, but its predictability is a parameter-free function of how much of p's residue structure is known: hit as above (roughly 1/(u ω(u)) for large y, with u = ln p / ln y; the 0.001 accuracy needs the exact product ∏(1 − 1/q), not the u-only form, which is off by up to 0.03 at small y), and about h₂(hit)/hit bits remain. It is a heuristic, the product of the Hardy–Littlewood factor and Buchstab's density of rough numbers; the data cannot test the product form as such, since at every y one of the two factors is within about 1 % of 1 (the pair factor above 0.998 for y ≥ 97, e^γω(u) within 1 % at large u). The ingredients (Buchstab, Mertens, Hardy–Littlewood) are classical; their combination into this law and its tests are ours.
