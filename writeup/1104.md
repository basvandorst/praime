# Puzzle 1104: Sums of powers of primitive roots

> **In short:** S_n(p) is the sum of the n-th powers of the primitive roots of p. For p = 43, S_1 … S_5 are prime; the page asks for another such prime (Q1), for one with six prime sums (Q2), and lists the least p with S_n prime for n ≤ 24. (1) S_n ≡ μ(d)·φ(p−1)/φ(d) (mod p) with d = (p−1)/gcd(n, p−1), so p | S_n exactly when d is not squarefree; for odd n this forces p ≡ 3 (mod 4), and Q1 needs p − 1 squarefree. (2) All S_n have the parity of the number of odd primitive roots. (3) With these rules, the table extends to n = 60 (proven primes), and Q1 has no solution below 10⁶ besides 43. (4) Heuristically the next Q1 solution lies near 10⁹ and the first Q2 solution near 10¹¹.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1104.htm)

**Status:** answered (table of least p for n = 1 … 60, proven primes, complete search; Q1: 43 is the only solution with p ≤ 10⁶, exhaustive; theorem on p | S_n; Q1/Q2 beyond that heuristic)

## The question

For a prime p let S_n(p) be the sum of g^n over the primitive roots g of p, 1 < g < p. The page notes that S_1(43), …, S_5(43) = 257, 6709, 188729, 5527909, 166291577 are prime and S_6(43) is composite, and asks:

- **Q1.** Another prime p with S_1 … S_5 prime?
- **Q2.** A prime whose first six (or more) power sums are prime?

Gennady Gusev writes that he found neither, and lists the least prime p with S_n(p) prime for n = 1 … 24 (3, 5, 23, 5, 11, 67, 103, 463, 719, 1499, 1907, 2131, 1087, 43, 647, 31, 3343, 4603, 11959, 3343, 6863, 179, 2347, 4603), asking whether someone wants to continue the table.

## How we found it

The first question is why Gusev's table has p ≡ 3 (mod 4) for every odd n. For p ≡ 1 (mod 4), −1 is a square, so −g is a primitive root whenever g is; the roots pair up as {g, p − g}, and for odd n each pair contributes g^n + (p − g)^n ≡ 0 (mod p). The general version is a classical congruence for Ramanujan-type sums over the primitive roots. It and a parity rule remove most p for free. The rest is computation with proven primality.

## The math

Let m = p − 1, let g₀ be a primitive root, and write the primitive roots as g₀^i with gcd(i, m) = 1.

**Step 1 (proved). S_n ≡ μ(d)·φ(m)/φ(d) (mod p), where d = m/gcd(n, m).** Put ζ = g₀^n, an element of order d. Then S_n ≡ Σ_{gcd(i,m)=1} ζ^i = Σ_{e | m} μ(e) Σ_{j=1}^{m/e} ζ^{ej} (Möbius inversion of the coprimality condition). The inner sum runs m/e times over powers of ζ^e, which has order d/gcd(d, e); it vanishes unless ζ^e = 1, that is d | e, and then equals m/e. Writing m = d·k with k = gcd(n, m): S_n ≡ Σ_{f | k} μ(df)·k/f. If d is not squarefree every μ(df) is 0, so p | S_n. If d is squarefree, μ(df) = μ(d)μ(f) for gcd(f, d) = 1 and 0 otherwise, so S_n ≡ μ(d)·k·∏_{q | k, q ∤ d}(1 − 1/q) = μ(d)·φ(m)/φ(d), a nonzero integer of absolute value below p. Hence **p | S_n iff d is not squarefree**, and then S_n is composite (S_n > p for p ≥ 5; for p = 3, S_n = 2^n).

Consequences: for odd n, 2 ∤ k, so 4 | p − 1 makes 4 | d: every odd n needs p ≡ 3 (mod 4). For n = 1, d = p − 1 must be squarefree. For p = 43: 42 = 2·3·7, so no S_n(43) is divisible by 43.

**Step 2 (proved). Parity.** g^n ≡ g (mod 2), so S_n ≡ (number of odd primitive roots) (mod 2) for every n ≥ 1. If that number is even, every S_n is even, hence composite (except S_1(3) = 2). Likewise S_n mod 3 depends only on n mod 2 and S_n mod 5 only on n mod 4, because g^n mod ℓ has period ℓ − 1 in n (including g ≡ 0).

The validation recomputes every S_n with a different enumeration of the primitive roots, proves the 60 listed values prime with PARI's APR-CL, and checks that every smaller p is excluded by step 1 or 2 or gives a composite S_n (sympy.isprime, BPSW). The pattern of the small entries is explained by step 1: 23 appears for n = 38, 57 and 43 for n = 14, 27 because 22 = 2·11 and 42 = 2·3·7 are squarefree, so they are never excluded.

**Step 4 (computed). Q1 below 10⁶.** By step 1, p − 1 must be squarefree (29396 of the 78497 primes below 10⁶). [`code/p1104_q1.c`](code/p1104_q1.c) enumerates the primitive roots of each such p and keeps the p with S_1 and S_2 prime (1291 and 69 primes). For these 69 the validation tests S_3, S_4, S_5: only p = 43 passes (p = 23 has four prime sums, six more primes have three). So **43 is the only solution of Q1 with p ≤ 10⁶**, and Q2 has none there.

**Step 5 (heuristic). Beyond 10⁶.** S_n ≈ φ(p−1)·p^n/(n+1), so ln S_n ≈ (n+1) ln p. Given p − 1 squarefree (density 0.3745 among primes) and an odd number of odd roots (probability 1/2), each S_n is prime with probability about 2/ln S_n; by step 2 the conditions mod 3 count once per parity class of n and mod 5 once per class of n mod 4, which multiplies the joint probability by (3/2)^(k−2)·(5/4)^(k−4) for k ≥ 4 sums. The model reproduces the counts of step 4: for p in [47, 10⁶) it expects 1250 primes with S_1 prime (found 1291) and 71 with S_1 and S_2 prime (found 69). The expected number of Q1 solutions in [47, 10⁶) is 0.015 (43 itself lies below that range), and between 10⁶ and X it grows like X/ln⁶X: 0.13 up to 10⁸, 0.64 up to 10⁹, 3.3 up to 10¹⁰, 102 up to 10¹². For Q2 (six sums) it grows like X/ln⁷X: 0.02 up to 10⁹, 0.40 up to 10¹¹, 2.1 up to 10¹². So both questions should have answers, but the next Q1 solution is expected near p ≈ 10⁹, where the O(p) enumeration of primitive roots costs 10¹⁷ steps, and the first Q2 solution near 10¹¹.
