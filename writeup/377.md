# Puzzle 377: "Add 2 to any digit": the variations are (heuristically) finite

> **In short:** Variations #2 and #3 of "add 2 to any digit" allow a composite base, which escapes the mod-7 proof; finiteness was open. The method: (1) an exhaustive search to 10¹³; (2) a Hardy–Littlewood count, calibrated up to 10⁸; (3) a decay like (C/(2.3k))^k. This gives 0.12 expected solutions beyond 13 digits in each: heuristically finite, not proved.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_377.htm)

**Status:** answered · #2 and #3 complete to 10¹³ (exhaustive) and heuristically finite beyond (0.12 expected each); #1 argued qualitatively

## The question

Adding 2 to any single digit of 32057 gives a prime. Wroblewski and Firoozbakht proved there is no larger prime example: for six or more digits, p, p + 2, p + 20, …, p + 200000 cover all residues mod 7.

The page then lists three variations:
- **#1** (Firoozbakht): digits 8 and 9 may be raised to 10 and 11; largest known 458481407.
- **#2** (Meyrignac): the base may be composite; 104 solutions below 10¹¹. Resta adds 7134354660741.
- **#3** (Resta): subtract 2, with a composite base allowed; largest known 92997492273.

The page asks for larger solutions, and whether each variation is finite or infinite.

## The model

For variation #2, take a k-digit n with digits 0 … 7 and the k numbers n + 2·10^i (i = 0 … k − 1). Following Hardy–Littlewood (FACTS section 4), the expected number of solutions is

E(k) = #(digit strings) · P(last digit fits) · ∏_q L_q / ∏_i ln N_i.

- **q = 2, 5:** the last digit must be 1 or 7. Then n + 2 and every n + 2·10^i (i ≥ 1) are coprime to 10, so the local factor is (1/0.4)^k on the 2/8 of strings with such a last digit.
- **q = 3:** all forms are congruent mod 3, so one class of n is lost, giving L₃ = (2/3)/(2/3)^k.
- **q ≥ 7:** ν_q = #{−2·10^i mod q} classes of n are lost, giving L_q = (1 − ν_q/q)/(1 − 1/q)^k. For q = 7 and k ≥ 6, ν₇ = 6 leaves only n ≡ 0 (mod 7). That is the page's argument, now as one factor among many.

Variation #3 is the same, with digits 2 … 9, last digit 3 or 9, and −2·10^i.

The exact counts include the 16 prime bases of #2, the largest being 32057, which matches Meyrignac's numbers. Beyond 10⁸, the model expects 9.7 solutions of 9–11 digits for #2, and Meyrignac's search to 10¹¹ found 5 with a composite base. For 12–13 digits it expects 1.0, and Resta found one, 7134354660741.

## The answer

E(k) decays like (C/(2.3k))^k: there are 8^k strings but k primality conditions, each costing a factor of about 1/ln N ≈ 1/(2.3k). The values are E(14) = 0.09, E(16) = 0.004, E(20) = 2·10⁻⁵ and E(30) = 2·10⁻¹¹.

- **#2:** the expected number of solutions with 14 or more digits is 0.12. The variation is finite, and probably complete with Resta's 13-digit solution: about 11 % chance of one more, all of it above 10¹³.
- **#3:** also 0.12 above 13 digits, so finite. The model expected about 1.0 solution with 12–13 digits (0.72 + 0.27). The exhaustive search below shows there is none, so 92997492273 stays the largest below 10¹³ (a 37 % event in the model).

## Exhaustive search to 13 digits

[`writeup/code/p377_search.c`](code/p377_search.c) searches all k-digit n for one k.
- **Split:** n = H·10^h + L, where both halves use only the allowed digits and L ends in 1 or 7 (#2) or in 3 or 9 (#3).
- **Join:** L is bucketed by L mod 3·7·13·17·19 = 88179. For each H, only the residues with no form ≡ 0 modulo those five primes are visited, so 7 | n is forced and one class mod 3 is lost.
- **Filter:** trial residues for every prime below 1000, then a deterministic Miller–Rabin (12 bases, exact below 3.3·10²⁴) on all k forms.

| k | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 | 13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| #2 solutions | 11 | 5 | 14 | 4 | 1 | 2 | **0** | **1** (7134354660741) |
| #3 solutions | 8 | 10 | 6 | 6 | 1 | 2 | **0** | **0** |

For k ≤ 8 the counts equal the independent sieve count (`p377_exact.py`). For #2, k = 9 and 11 reproduce Meyrignac's solutions, including his largest composite base 53175232341. For #3, k = 11 contains Resta's 92997492273. A 12- or 13-digit search took a few minutes on 2 cores.
- **#1:** a digit 8 or 9 turns into two digits, so those modified numbers are not n + 2·10^i. Still, k + 1 numbers of about k digits must all be prime, which gives the same super-exponential decay. So #1 is finite as well, but we did not compute its numbers.
