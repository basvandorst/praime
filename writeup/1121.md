# Puzzle 1121: φ(p)/φ(p − 2) = 2 for Fermat numbers

> **In short:** The page asks for a proof that a Fermat number p = F_m is prime iff φ(p)/φ(p − 2) = 2. (1) p − 2 = F_0⋯F_(m−1), and every prime factor of F_m is ≡ 1 (mod 2^(m+2)). (2) These bounds, with a factor search for m = 6 … 10, give the ratio exactly 2 for m ≤ 4 and not 2 for m ≥ 5, whether F_m is prime or not. So "ratio 2 ⇒ prime" is true, and "prime ⇒ ratio 2" holds iff 65537 is the last Fermat prime, which is open.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1121.htm)

**Status:** answered · proved: φ(F_m)/φ(F_m − 2) = 2 iff m ≤ 4; the stated equivalence is therefore equivalent to "no Fermat prime beyond 65537"

## The question

S. M. Ruiz computed R_m = φ(F_m)/φ(F_m − 2) for m ≤ 7. It is 2 for m ≤ 4, where F_m is prime, and 1.99688, 2.00312, 2.00313 for m = 5, 6, 7, where F_m is composite. Q: prove that a Fermat number is prime iff R_m = 2, or find a counterexample. One contribution (Vantieghem) says the conjecture is not true; his proof is an image that our copy of the page does not include.

## The math

**Step 1 (proved): the denominator.** F_0F_1⋯F_(m−1) = F_m − 2, by induction from F_(m+1) − 2 = (F_m − 2)F_m. The Fermat numbers are pairwise coprime, so φ(F_m − 2) = ∏_(i<m) φ(F_i). For i ≤ 4, F_i is prime and φ(F_i) = 2^(2^i), so φ(F_0⋯F_4) = 2³¹. Hence for m ≤ 4, F_m prime gives R_m = 2^(2^m)/2^(2^m − 1) = 2. For m = 5, R_5 = 640·6700416/2³¹ = 261735/131072.

**Step 2 (proved): an upper bound on φ(F_m − 2)/(F_m − 2) for m ≥ 6.** Each factor φ(F_i)/F_i is ≤ 1. So

```
φ(F_m − 2)/(F_m − 2) ≤ [2³¹/(2³² − 1)] · φ(F_5)/F_5 = D,   2D = 0.99843979 = 1 − 1.5602·10⁻³.
```

**Step 3 (proved): a lower bound on φ(F_m)/F_m.** Every prime q | F_m with m ≥ 2 satisfies q ≡ 1 (mod 2^(m+2)) (Euler–Lucas). So the j-th smallest distinct prime factor is at least j·2^(m+2) + 1. There are at most 2^m of them, since their product is at most F_m < 2^(2^m + 1). Hence

```
φ(F_m)/F_m = ∏ (1 − 1/q) ≥ 1 − Σ 1/q > 1 − H_(2^m)/2^(m+2) ≥ 1 − (1 + m ln 2)/2^(m+2).
```

**Step 4 (proved): R_m > 2 for m ≥ 6.** R_m = [F_m/(F_m − 2)]·[φ(F_m)/F_m]/[φ(F_m − 2)/(F_m − 2)] > (1 − Σ 1/q)/D. So R_m > 2 as soon as Σ 1/q < 1.5602·10⁻³.

- For m ≥ 11 the bound of Step 3 gives Σ 1/q < (1 + 11 ln 2)/2¹³ = 1.05·10⁻³, and the bound decreases with m.
- For 6 ≤ m ≤ 10 we tested every q = k·2^(m+2) + 1 < 2³² for q | F_m. The only prime factors below 2³² are 274177 (m = 6), 2424833 (m = 9) and 45592577 (m = 10). The remaining factors are at most (2^m + 1)/32 primes, each ≥ 2³². That gives Σ 1/q < 3.7·10⁻⁶ in all five cases.

The argument never uses whether F_m is prime. A Fermat prime F_m with m ≥ 6 would still have R_m > 2, because the composite F_5 sits inside F_m − 2.

**Conclusion.** R_m = 2 ⇔ m ≤ 4 ⇔ F_m ∈ {3, 5, 17, 257, 65537}. The direction "R_m = 2 ⇒ F_m prime" is a theorem. The direction "F_m prime ⇒ R_m = 2" is equivalent to the open statement that 65537 is the largest Fermat prime. It holds for every m ≤ 32, since F_5 … F_32 are known composite. So the proposed equivalence is true precisely if there are no Fermat primes beyond F_4. The page's examples agree: R_6 = 2.003118 and R_7 = 2.003133 are both above 2.
