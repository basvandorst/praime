# Puzzle 804: Why all the primes P end in 9

> **In short:** We need Q = (P + 1)² + 1 prime and Q² + Rev(Q²) a palindrome.
> - **P ending in 1 or 7:** impossible, because then 5 | Q.
> - **P ending in 3:** then Q² ends in 9, and Q² + Rev(Q²) is a palindrome only for a rigid digit pattern. The first digit must be 2, every inner pair of mirrored digits must sum to 0 or 11, and the length must be even, since an odd length forces 11 | Q. Only 9^((d−2)/2) of the d-digit numbers have that pattern, so these cases thin out exponentially, and none occurs for P < 3·10⁶.
> - **P ending in 9:** the sum has the same length as Q², and then it is a palindrome only when no column carries. This proves Jan van Delden's observation.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_804.htm)

**Status:** answered · Q2 explained (1, 7 impossible; 3 reduced to a rigid pattern, none below 3·10⁶); Jan van Delden's no-carry observation proved

## The question

Find primes P such that Q = (P + 1)² + 1 is prime and Q² + Rev(Q²) is a palindrome. All 38 (and later 826) known solutions have P ending in 9. Q2 asks why.

On the page, Emmanuel Vantieghem rules out 1 and 7, and Jan van Delden notes that the known solutions have no carries.

## The answer

Write M = Q², with d digits m_(d−1) … m_0, and S = M + Rev(M). The column sums c_j = m_j + m_(d−1−j) are symmetric.

**1 and 7.** If P ≡ 1 or 7 (mod 10), then (P + 1)² + 1 ≡ 5 (mod 10), so 5 | Q.

**9, and Jan's observation.** If P ≡ 9, then Q ≡ 1 and M ends in 1.

- If the first digit is 9, the sum needs an extra digit, and the automaton below shows that no palindrome is possible.
- Otherwise S has d digits. A palindrome then needs S_i = S_(d−1−i). The two mirrored columns i and d−1−i have the same column sum, so their digits agree only if their incoming carries agree: r_i = l_i.
- The carry out of column d−1−i is then r_(i+1), and it must equal l_(i−1) = r_(i−1). This gives r_(i+1) = r_(i−1). With r_0 = 0, and r_1 = 0 because S is no longer than M, every carry is 0.
- So S is a palindrome exactly when every c_j ≤ 9 (and a middle digit is at most 4): there is never a carry.

**3.** If P ≡ 3, then Q ≡ 7 and M ends in 9. The outer column sum 9 + m_(d−1) is at least 10, so S has d + 1 digits and starts with 1.

We follow the carries from the outside in with a finite automaton. Its state is (carry into the next right column, guessed carry into the left column, digit owed on the left), and there are only four reachable states. Running through every transition shows:

- the first digit of M must be 2;
- in each state exactly one inner pair sum is allowed, 0 or 11. Which one comes next depends on the guessed carry, so the sequence of pair sums is any word over {0, 11};
- for odd d the middle digit must be 0.

This gives exactly 9^⌊(d−2)/2⌋ such M with d digits. An exhaustive count for d ≤ 9 matches this: 1, 1, 9, 9, 81, 81, 729, 729.

**Odd d is impossible for prime Q.** For odd d, the two digits of a pair sit at positions of the same parity, so each pair contributes ±(pair sum) ≡ 0 (mod 11) to M mod 11. The outer pair sums to 11 as well. So 11 | M = Q², hence 11 | Q, which is impossible for a prime Q > 11.

**How rare is ending in 3?** A random d-digit M has the pattern with probability about 9^(d/2)/10^d ≈ 0.3^d. With carries forbidden, as for P ≡ 9, it is about 0.55^(d/2). Sampling 400000 random M per length gives:

| d | M ends in 1 | M ends in 9 |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | 4.5·10⁻² | 2.5·10⁻⁶ |
| 16 | 1.4·10⁻² | 0 found |
| 20 | 4·10⁻³ | 0 found |

Among the real cases with P < 3·10⁶ and Q prime, there are 8689 with P ≡ 9, of which 50 give a palindrome, and 8854 with P ≡ 3, of which none do.

P ≡ 3 is therefore not excluded in principle. But a solution needs Q² to fall into the pattern, and the expected number of such P over all sizes is tiny. We give this as a heuristic, not a proof.
