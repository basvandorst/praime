# Puzzle 9: Sums of the first k primes that are perfect powers

> **In short:** We could not beat Resta's 2003 record (the sum of the first 126,789,311,423 primes is a square), but we verified it exactly in four seconds with Lucy's prime-sum recursion instead of adding up 126 billion primes. A full sweep to k = 1.9·10⁸ finds no cube or higher power. A probabilistic model explains every known term: it predicts 6.1 squares up to Resta's k (there are 6), says squares never run out but thin out like √(2 ln k), puts the next one at k ≈ 2·10¹³ (median), and leaves only a 4·10⁻⁴ chance for any cube beyond our sweep.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_009.htm)

**Status:** open · records verified exactly (Lucy); no cube or higher power for k ≤ 1.9·10⁸; heuristic: next square near k ≈ 2·10¹³ (median), cubes almost surely none

## The question

Let S(k) = p₁ + p₂ + … + p_k be the sum of the first k primes. Honaker noticed S(9) = 2 + 3 + … + 23 = 100 = 10² and asked: which k give a perfect square, cube or higher power? Beat the record for squares, or find another perfect power.

Zak Seidov (2018) added the variant for odd primes, T(k) = p₂ + … + p_k = S(k) − 2.

## Current record vs ours

| | Squares S(k) = n² | Cubes or higher | Odd-prime variant T(k) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Page | k = 9, 2474, 6694, 7785, 709838 (McCranie), **126789311423** (Resta, 2003) | none found (Honaker) | T(3) = 8 = 2³ and squares at k = 24, 28, 32, 46, 296, 5327, 12227, 55560480 (Seidov) |
| Literature | OEIS [A033997](https://oeis.org/A033997): the same six terms, keyword "more", no seventh known | — | OEIS [A274120](https://oeis.org/A274120): Resta (2016) searched beyond 3.6·10¹² primes, nothing new |
| **Ours** | all six verified exactly; no new term (we did not search past k = 1.9·10⁸) | **none for k ≤ 1.9·10⁸** (every prime exponent 3..53) | all nine verified; nothing else for k ≤ 1.9·10⁸ |

Not a record. What is new here is the exact verification of the big terms, the power sweep, and the model in "The math" with its forecast.

## How we went about it

1. **Read the record before attacking it.** Resta's sum is 2.2·10²³ and needs every prime up to 3.5·10¹². Re-adding them would take a long sieve. Lucy's recursion computes π(x) and Σ_{p≤x} p for a single x in O(x^{3/4}) steps, so the check takes seconds (§7).
2. **A bounded sweep.** We used a segmented sieve to 4·10⁹, which is every k ≤ 189,961,812. For each k it tests S(k) and T(k) for squares, cubes and every prime exponent up to 53, with exact integer roots (about 10 minutes on one core).
3. **A model before any longer search.** We worked out how often squares and cubes should occur (§3–§6), checked it against all known terms, and used it to price the next square (§5, §7). The price is far beyond a bounded run, so we stopped there.

## The math

### 1. Definitions and a reduction

pᵢ is the i-th prime. S(k) = Σ_{i≤k} pᵢ, and T(k) = S(k) − 2 for k ≥ 2. We look for S(k) = n^m with m ≥ 2.

It is enough to test prime exponents m. If m = ab with b prime, then n^m = (n^a)^b. Every S(k) in our sweep is below 2⁶⁴, and 2⁵⁹ > 3.7·10¹⁷ > S(189961812), so the exponents 2, 3, 5, …, 53 cover every case.

### 2. Parity and residues (proved, plus a measurement)

**Lemma 1.** S(k) ≡ k + 1 (mod 2), and T(k) ≡ k − 1 (mod 2).

*Proof.* S(k) = 2 + (p₂ + … + p_k) is 2 plus k − 1 odd numbers, so S(k) ≡ k − 1 ≡ k + 1 (mod 2). Then T(k) = S(k) − 2 ≡ S(k). ∎

So for odd k, S(k) is even, and a square S(k) = n² needs n even, which means 4 | S(k). Every known odd-k term passes that test:
- S(9) = 100
- S(7785) = 292341604
- S(126789311423) = …756

All three are divisible by 4. For even k, S(k) is odd, and an odd square is ≡ 1 (mod 8). For example, S(2474) = 25633969 ≡ 1 (mod 8).

**There is no congruence obstruction or bias.** S(k) mod q is the running sum of the residues pᵢ mod q. By the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions, those residues are uniformly distributed over the classes coprime to q. The running sum is then a walk on ℤ/q that visits every class equally often.

To check this, let ρ_q(r) be the number of y mod q with y² ≡ r. A uniform residue has E[ρ_q] = 1, so any bias towards or away from squares would show up as a value different from 1. Measured over k ≤ 5,761,455:

| q | 8 | 3 | 5 | 7 | 840 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S(k): E[ρ_q(S(k) mod q)] | 0.9999 | 1.0001 | 1.0001 | 1.0003 | 1.0015 |
| T(k) | 1.0007 | 1.0000 | 0.9997 | 0.9999 | 0.9967 |

The local correction factor is 1 to within 0.3 %. No modulus rules out squares, and no modulus favours them. The model below therefore needs no correction factor.

### 3. How big S(k) is

**Proposition (theorem).** Σ_{p≤x} p = li(x²) + O(x²·e^{−c√(ln x)}).

*Proof.* Write π(t) = li(t) + E(t). The prime number theorem with de la Vallée Poussin's error term gives E(t) = O(t·e^{−c√(ln t)}). As a Stieltjes integral,

```
Σ_{p≤x} p = ∫₂ˣ t dπ(t) = ∫₂ˣ t/ln t dt + ∫₂ˣ t dE(t).
```

In the first integral substitute u = t², so du = 2t dt and ln u = 2 ln t:

```
∫₂ˣ t/ln t dt = ∫₄^{x²} (du/2)/((ln u)/2) = ∫₄^{x²} du/ln u = li(x²) − li(4).
```

Integrate the second integral by parts: x·E(x) − 2·E(2) − ∫₂ˣ E(t) dt = O(x²·e^{−c√(ln x)}). ∎

Numeric check: li(x²)/S − 1 is 1.07·10⁻⁴ at x = 10729219 (McCranie) and 2.4·10⁻⁷ at x = 3531577135439 (Resta).

**Corollary (asymptotic in k).** Cipolla's expansion gives p_k = k(ln k + ln ln k − 1 + (ln ln k − 2)/ln k + O((ln ln k)²/ln² k)). Summing it, which is the integral up to an O(p_k) term by Euler–Maclaurin, takes four integrals:

```
∫₁ᵏ t ln t dt              = (k²/2) ln k − k²/4
∫₁ᵏ t ln ln t dt           = (k²/2) ln ln k − ∫ t/(2 ln t) dt = (k²/2) ln ln k − (k²/4)/ln k · (1 + O(1/ln k))
∫₁ᵏ (−t) dt                = −k²/2
∫₁ᵏ t (ln ln t − 2)/ln t dt = (k²/2)(ln ln k − 2)/ln k · (1 + O(1/ln k))
```

Adding them up:

```
S(k) = (k²/2) · ( ln k + ln ln k − 3/2 + (ln ln k − 5/2)/ln k ) · (1 + O((ln ln k)²/ln² k)).
```

The leading term is S(k) ~ (k²/2) ln k, the formula in OEIS A007504. The measured ratio of S(k) to the bracket is 1.0076 at k = 2474, 0.99972 at k = 709838 and 0.99971 at k = 5,761,455.

### 4. How often S(k) is a square (heuristic, checked against the data)

**Density of squares.** Near X the squares n² are spaced 2n + 1 ≈ 2√X apart. A "random" integer of size X is therefore a square with probability 1/(2√X). §2 shows that the residues add no correction.

**Why consecutive k behave independently.** The step from S(k) to S(k+1) is p_{k+1} ≈ k ln k. The gap between squares near S(k) is 2√S(k) ≈ k√(2 ln k). Their ratio is

```
p_{k+1} / (2√S(k)) ≈ (k ln k) / (k √(2 ln k)) = √(ln k / 2),
```

which is bigger than 1 for k > e² ≈ 7.4 and grows without bound. From the first few k on, every step jumps past at least one square. The fractional part of √S(k) then moves by about √(ln k/2) each step, which behaves like an irrational rotation, so it is spread evenly over [0, 1).

**Expected count.** Write E(K) = Σ_{k≤K} 1/(2√S(k)). Since 2√S(k) ≈ k√(2 ln k),

```
E(K) ≈ ∫ dk / (k √(2 ln k)) = √(2 ln K) + C,    because  d/dk √(2 ln k) = 1/(k √(2 ln k)).
```

So **E(K₁, K₂) = √(2 ln K₂) − √(2 ln K₁)**. It diverges, so the model expects infinitely many squares. They come extremely slowly: doubling ln k adds only a factor √2.

This fits the rigorous results:
- Luca (2007) proved that the k with S(k) a square have density 0. The model's count √(2 ln K) is far below that bound, so it is consistent with it.
- Cilleruelo and Luca (2008) give a lower bound for the counting function (OEIS A033997).

Neither proves that the list is infinite. Tengely asks exactly that, and it is open.

| K | Σ_{k≤K} 1/(2√S(k)) (exact S) | √(2 ln K) − √(2 ln 2) | squares found up to K |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 1.246 | 0.969 | 1 |
| 10⁴ | 3.306 | 3.115 | 4 |
| 10⁶ | 4.233 | 4.079 | 5 |
| 5,761,455 | 4.544 | 4.402 | 5 |

The closed form runs a stable 0.14 below the exact sum. That is the constant C, which comes from the second-order terms in §3.

**Prediction for the whole known range.** The model gives √(2 ln 126789311423) − √(2 ln 2) + 0.14 = **6.1 squares** for k ≤ 1.27·10¹¹. **Exactly 6 are known.**

The odd-prime variant has the same density. Resta's 2016 search covered k ≤ 3.6·10¹², where the model predicts 6.3 squares (offset −0.11, measured the same way) and 8 are known. A Poisson count with mean 6.3 reaches at least 8 with probability 0.30, so this is ordinary scatter.

### 5. Forecast for the next square

Assume nothing beyond k₁ = 126,789,311,423 has been searched; the OEIS gives no bound. The number of new squares up to K is then Poisson with mean E = √(2 ln K) − √(2 ln k₁):

| K (number of primes) | E | P(at least one new square) | primes to sieve, up to p_K ≈ K(ln K + ln ln K − 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10¹² | 0.28 | 25 % | 3.0·10¹³ |
| 10¹³ | 0.59 | 44 % | 3.2·10¹⁴ |
| **2.3·10¹³** | **0.69** | **50 % (median)** | **7.6·10¹⁴** |
| 10¹⁴ | 0.88 | 59 % | 3.5·10¹⁵ |
| 10¹⁶ | 1.43 | 76 % | 3.9·10¹⁷ |
| 10¹⁸ | 1.95 | 86 % | 4.4·10¹⁹ |

Even at k = 10¹⁸ there is a 14 % chance that no new square has appeared yet.

### 6. Cubes and higher powers (heuristic)

Near X the m-th powers are spaced about m·X^{1−1/m} apart, so the chance is (1/m)·X^{1/m−1}. The expected number for k > K is

```
E_m(K, ∞) ≈ ∫_K^∞ (1/m) ((t²/2) ln t)^{−(m−1)/m} dt
          ≈ (1/m) · 2^{(m−1)/m} · (ln K)^{−(m−1)/m} · ∫_K^∞ t^{−2(m−1)/m} dt
          = 2^{(m−1)/m} · K^{−(m−2)/m} / ( (m−2) · (ln K)^{(m−1)/m} ),
```

because ∫_K^∞ t^{−2(m−1)/m} dt = K^{−(m−2)/m}/((m−2)/m), and that converges for every m ≥ 3. Treating ln t as constant on the range is the only approximation.

For m = 3 this is E₃(K, ∞) ≈ 2^{2/3}·K^{−1/3}/(ln K)^{2/3}. Unlike the squares, the cubes are expected to be **finite in number**.

| | expected for k ≤ 5,761,455 | expected beyond K = 5,761,455 | beyond our sweep (1.9·10⁸) | beyond 3.6·10¹² |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cubes, S | 0.84 | 1.4·10⁻³ | **3.9·10⁻⁴** | 1.1·10⁻⁵ |
| 5th powers, S | 0.32 | 5.7·10⁻⁶ | | |
| 7th powers, S | 0.20 | 5.1·10⁻⁷ | | |
| cubes, T | 0.69 | | | |

Almost all of the expected 0.84 cubes sits at the first few k, where S(k) is small: 2, 5, 10, 17, 28, 41, 58, 77, 100, 129, none of them a cube. The odd sums used their share early: T(3) = 3 + 5 = 8 = 2³. The sweep found no cube up to 1.9·10⁸. From there the chance of a cube anywhere is 4·10⁻⁴. If Resta's 2016 run also tested cubes (the OEIS does not say), it drops to 10⁻⁵.

So to Honaker's "Can k produce cubes, etc.?" the answer is: heuristically almost certainly not. A proof would need to rule out S(k) = n³ for every k. No algebraic structure is known that could do that, so a proof is out of reach.

### 7. Lucy's recursion, and why no shortcut finds the next square

Lucy's recursion gives π(x) and Σ_{p≤x} p together.
- Let V = {⌊x/i⌋} be the set of all distinct values x/i, about 2√x of them. Start with the "sieve nothing" values C(v) = v − 1 and Σ(v) = v(v+1)/2 − 1.
- For each prime p ≤ √x and each v ≥ p² in V, update

  C(v) −= C(v/p) − C(p−1),  Σ(v) −= p·(Σ(v/p) − Σ(p−1)).

  This removes the numbers whose smallest prime factor is p.
- The cost is O(x^{3/4}/ln x) and the memory O(√x). For x = 3.5·10¹² that is about 2·10⁶ values and 4 seconds in C with 128-bit sums.

The verification script runs it a second time in numpy: once mod 2⁶⁴ (exact) and once in float64 (approximate). If a value agrees with n² mod 2⁶⁴ and lies within 2⁶³ of n², it equals n².

Lucy lets a search start at any x: it computes the exact sum up to x, and then you sieve from x onwards. **It cannot skip k values.** Whether S(k) is a square depends on S(k) itself. By §2 no modulus q excludes a range of k, and knowing S(k) mod q already needs p_k. Any search is therefore linear in the number of primes. The 50 % point of §5 means sieving to 7.6·10¹⁴. Our sieve does 4·10⁹ in about 10 minutes on one core, so that is roughly 3.6 core-years, or weeks with a much faster sieve such as primesieve. That is far outside a bounded run.
