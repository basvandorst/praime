# Puzzle 456: Mersenne primes as a sum of consecutive primes

> **In short:** Asked: write Mersenne primes 2ᵉ − 1 as sums of consecutive primes. The page has examples for e = 5, 7, 13, 17, 61, 127. The method: (1) a window of consecutive primes is a difference of two prefix sums, so all windows with a given sum are found in one pass over the primes; (2) for large e, each number k of terms has at most one window, near (2ᵉ − 1)/k. Result: the page's lists for e ≤ 17 are complete; 2¹⁹ − 1 and 2³¹ − 1 are not a sum of two or more consecutive primes at all.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_456.htm)

**Status:** open · complete for e ≤ 31 (computed); for e = 61, 89, 107, 127 every k ≤ 3000

## The question

2⁵ − 1 = 7 + 11 + 13, 2¹⁷ − 1 = 43669 + 43691 + 43711, 2¹²⁷ − 1 is a sum of 3 consecutive primes. Express Mersenne primes as a sum of consecutive primes.

## How we found it

1. Let C(j) be the sum of the first j primes. A window of consecutive primes with sum M is a pair i < j with C(j) − C(i) = M. Both C(j) and C(j) − M increase with j, so two streams of primes, one ahead and one behind, find every pair in one pass.
2. For M = 2³¹ − 1 the pass runs over the primes below M/2 (20 seconds). For e ≥ 61 that is out of reach, but for a fixed number k of terms the window lies near M/k and its sum increases when the window moves up by one prime. So each k has at most one window, found by sliding.
3. How many to expect: for a given k the window sums near M are spaced about k·ln(M/k) apart, and only odd k give an odd sum, so the chance is about 2/(k·ln(M/k)). Summed over all odd k this is of order 1 for every Mersenne prime: some have a representation, some have none.

## The math

**Prefix sums (proved).** With C(0) = 0 and C(j) = p₁ + … + pⱼ, the primes pᵢ₊₁ … pⱼ sum to C(j) − C(i). For k ≥ 2 terms and odd M the largest prime is below M/2 + (one gap), because k = 2 would need 2 + (M − 2) and all other windows have at least 3 terms. The search streams the primes up to M/2 + 2000 for j and looks up C(j) − M among the prefix sums.

**One window per k (proved).** Moving a window of k consecutive primes up by one prime replaces its smallest prime by a larger one, so the sum strictly increases. Hence at most one window of k terms has sum M; the program starts near M/k and slides until the sum passes M.

**Parity (proved).** For odd M and a window without the prime 2, k is odd. A window with 2 has sum 2 + (k − 1 odd primes), odd only for even k; these start at 2 and are covered by the prefix-sum search for e ≤ 31. For e ≥ 61 a window from 2 would have about 10⁸ terms or more, outside k ≤ 3000.
