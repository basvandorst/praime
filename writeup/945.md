# Puzzle 945: Primes whose remainders modulo every place value are prime

> **In short:** Write a prime p = Σ dᵢ·10ⁱ and ask that p mod (dᵢ·10ⁱ) be prime for every nonzero digit dᵢ (353: 353 mod 3 = 2, mod 50 = 3, mod 300 = 53). The record zero-free solutions had 16 digits (smallest of each length up to 16 known), 12 digits for the largest. (1) The remainder at position i is 10ⁱ·((10Hᵢ) mod dᵢ) + Lᵢ, with Hᵢ the digits above i and Lᵢ the digits below, and (10Hᵢ) mod dᵢ depends only on Hᵢ modulo 1, 1, 3, 2, 1, 3, 7, 4, 9 for dᵢ = 1 … 9, all dividing 252. (2) A depth-first search from the units digit upward, carrying Hᵢ modulo the moduli still needed, tests every remainder as soon as it is determined and enumerates all zero-free solutions: 2862 of them, with lengths 2 to 23. (3) The longest, and the largest, is 61951261638886795435349 (23 digits, unique); the only 22-digit solution is 6437443768993674434843; there is no zero-free solution with 24 or more digits.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_945.htm)

**Status:** beaten · complete enumeration of the zero-free solutions (proven primes; the DFS is exhaustive given the reduction of Step 1)

## The question

J. M. Bergot's rule: for a prime p and each nonzero digit d at position i (units = position 0), compute p mod (d·10ⁱ); every such remainder must be prime. Zeros are skipped. The page asks for the largest such prime, "the fewer the 0's, the better". Vicente Felipe Izquierdo lists the smallest solution of each length up to 11 digits (12184279817); Giovanni Resta extends that list to 16 digits (1219213679413217); Pierandrea Formusa gives 49963422857 as the largest zero-free solution below 5·10¹⁰ and 10²⁰⁰⁰ + 50053 as a large solution with zeros; Paul Cleary gives 726862627433 as his largest zero-free solution.

## How we found it

The remainder modulo d·10ⁱ splits into a known part and an unknown part: the lower digits Lᵢ are known when building the number from the right, and the upper digits Hᵢ enter only through (10Hᵢ) mod d, that is, through Hᵢ modulo a divisor of 252. So we build the number from the units digit upward and guess the residue of Hᵢ modulo the moduli that the remainders below still need. Every guess is checked when the top is reached (Hᵢ = 0 there), and the recurrence Hᵢ = 10Hᵢ₊₁ + dᵢ₊₁ carries the guess upward. Each remainder is tested the moment it is determined, so the tree is pruned at every digit, and it dies out: partial strings survive to 28 digits, none to 29. The script is [`code/p945_dfs.py`](code/p945_dfs.py) (27 seconds, 1.37 million nodes).

## The math

**Step 1 (proved). The remainder formula.** Let p = Hᵢ·10ⁱ⁺¹ + dᵢ·10ⁱ + Lᵢ with 0 ≤ Lᵢ < 10ⁱ. Then p = 10ⁱ(10Hᵢ + dᵢ) + Lᵢ, and modulo dᵢ·10ⁱ the term 10ⁱ·dᵢ vanishes, so

  p mod (dᵢ·10ⁱ) = 10ⁱ·((10Hᵢ) mod dᵢ) + Lᵢ,

because the right side lies in [0, dᵢ·10ⁱ). The factor (10Hᵢ) mod dᵢ depends on Hᵢ mod m(dᵢ) with m(d) = d/gcd(10, d) adjusted for the shared factor 2: m = 1, 1, 3, 2, 1, 3, 7, 4, 9 for d = 1, …, 9 (for d = 4: 10H mod 4 = 2H mod 4 needs H mod 2; for d = 8: 2H mod 8 needs H mod 4; for d = 6: 4H mod 6 needs H mod 3). For d ∈ {1, 2, 5} the remainder is simply Lᵢ. For the top digit Hᵢ = 0 and the remainder is p with its leading digit removed. All m(d) divide 252 = 4·9·7.

**Step 2 (proved). The search state.** Build the digits d₀, d₁, … upward. At position i the state is (Lᵢ₊₁ = value of d₀ … dᵢ, M, h) with h ≡ Hᵢ (mod M), where M is the least common multiple of the moduli m(dⱼ), j ≤ i, that still matter. The recurrence Hᵢ = 10Hᵢ₊₁ + dᵢ₊₁ determines Hᵢ₊₁ modulo M/gcd(10, M) from h and dᵢ₊₁ (10 is invertible modulo the odd part of M, and the factor 2 of M is lost: this is why a 2-adic modulus from d = 4 or 8 fades after one or two steps). The new modulus is M' = lcm(M/gcd(10, M), m(dᵢ₊₁)) and the state branches over the lifts of Hᵢ₊₁ to Z/M'. The remainder at position i+1 is then determined and tested. The number terminates at position i when h ≡ 0 (mod M) (so Hᵢ = 0 is consistent) and Lᵢ₊₁ is prime. By induction from the top, where Hᵢ = 0 is exact, every state on an accepted path equals the true residue, and conversely every solution is found along the path of its true residues. So the enumeration is complete as long as the tree dies out, which it does: the number of surviving states per depth is 8, 97, 753, …, peaks at 170573 for 12 digits, and falls to 239, 90, 48, 3 for 24 to 27 digits; there is no state with 28 or more digits past its remainder check (the search allowed up to 40), and the last solutions (primes at the top) have 23 digits.

**Step 4 (heuristic). Why the set is finite.** An n-digit zero-free candidate has n − 1 remainders of about i digits (i = 1 … n−1) that must be prime, each with probability about 2.5/ln 10ⁱ, and p itself must be prime. The expected count is about 9ⁿ·(1/3)·(4/9)·Π_{i=1}^{n−1} 2.5/ln 10^{i+1/2}·2.5/ln 10ⁿ, a quantity that behaves like 9.8ⁿ/n! and peaks near 9 digits. The model gives the right shape (peak at 9 digits, decay by a factor of about 2 per digit beyond 18) but underestimates the counts by a factor 3 to 10, growing with n, because solutions favour the digits 1, 2 and 5, whose remainders are the shorter numbers Lᵢ. With solutions allowed to contain zeros the set is infinite (a zero skips a constraint), which is why the page's 10²⁰⁰⁰ + 50053 is not comparable.
