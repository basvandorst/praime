# Puzzle 115: Prime squares with at most two different digits are (almost surely) finite

> **In short:** The known primes whose square uses at most two different digits are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 109, 173 and 81619 (81619² = 6661661161). Mishima searched every such square up to 10⁴⁸ and found nothing more. The page asks whether the sequence is finite.
> - **A calibrated count says yes.** A digit DP gives the exact number of two-digit-alphabet strings in every class mod 83160. Combined with the density of prime squares in those classes, it predicts the counts of small solutions correctly.
> - **The expected number of further solutions above 10⁴⁸ is about 2·10⁻¹⁰.** Even Mishima's range from 15 to 48 digits expected only 0.002.
> - **Only 8 of the 45 digit pairs are possible at all:** 01, 12, 14, 16, 18, 29, 49 and 89.
>
> This is a heuristic, not a proof.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_115.htm)

**Status:** answered · Q2 argued: heuristically finite, the list of 8 very probably complete (calibrated model; not a proof)

## The question

Find primes p such that p² is written with at most two different digits (R. K. Guy, *Unsolved Problems*, F24). The known terms are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 109, 173 and 81619.

- Q1: extend the sequence.
- Q2: argue whether it is finite or infinite.

H. Mishima's method builds the upper half of each two-digit square and takes its square root. With it he searched all squares up to B = 10⁴⁸ and found no new example. The page ends with "Maybe is time to start arguing why no other example should come any more".

## The model

A k-digit number N built from two digits is a prime square with a chance that depends on N modulo small numbers:

- every p² with p > 11 is ≡ 1 (mod 24);
- every p² ends in 1 or 9;
- every p² is a square of a unit modulo 7, 9 and 11.

With M = 83160 = 2³·3³·5·7·11 and R the set of squares of units mod M:

P(N = p² | N ≡ r mod M) = [r ∈ R] · (M/|R|) · 1/(2√N · ln √N).

A digit DP counts exactly how many k-digit strings over each pair {a, b} fall in each class r. Summing gives the expected number E(k) of k-digit solutions (`p115_model.py`).

The same DP with "all squares" in place of "prime squares" tests the model. Squares of multiples of 10 are left out, since their trailing zeros make them trivial.

| lengths 5–14 | model | exact (roots below 10⁷) |
|---|---|---|
| squares with ≤ 2 digits, A not divisible by 10 | 5.97 | 7 |
| squares of primes with ≤ 2 digits | 1.10 | 3 (11881, 29929, 6661661161) |

## The answer

E(k) falls by a factor of about 2 for every two extra digits. Each extra digit multiplies the number of strings by 2 but the chance of being a square by 1/√10, and there is the extra factor 1/ln √N. For example, E(20) = 7.6·10⁻⁵, E(30) = 2.7·10⁻⁷ and E(48) = 4.8·10⁻¹¹.

- **Mishima's searched range** (15–48 digits): expected 0.0022 solutions, found 0.
- **Above 10⁴⁸:** expected about **1.8·10⁻¹⁰** solutions in total.

So the sum over all lengths converges: the sequence is heuristically finite, and the 8 known terms are almost certainly all of them. A proof would need to rule out squares in sparse digit sets, which is far out of reach.

**Which digit pairs can occur.** For p prime and p² with 15 to 40 digits, the residues mod 83160 leave only the pairs 01, 12, 16, 49 and 89 (all lengths), plus 14 (lengths ≡ 1 mod 3), and 18 and 29 (only some lengths). The reasons:
- p² ends in 1 or 9, so the pair contains 1 or 9;
- p² ≡ 1 (mod 8) restricts the last three digits;
- p² ≡ 1 (mod 3) restricts the digit counts;
- the conditions mod 7 and 11 do the rest.

The known 6661661161 uses the pair 16, and 11881 uses 18.
