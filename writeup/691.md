# Puzzle 691: Conjectures related to Gilbreath's scheme

> **In short:** Conjecture 2 said that for large k the k-th difference row D_k(p) of the primes has no two equal elements; it was open. The proof: (1) D_k(p) is a difference of prime gaps, so half of its first N terms are at most 2^(k+2)·ln(2N) in size; (2) those terms are even; (3) by pigeonhole, values repeat. This proves conjecture 2 false for every k ≥ 1.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_691.htm)

**Status:** answered · conjecture 2 disproved for every k (proof); conjectures 1 and 3 settled on the page (Vantieghem); conjecture 4 implies Gilbreath's conjecture and stays open

## The question

D_0(p) = (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …), and D_(k+1) is the sequence of differences of D_k. Mahdi Meisami conjectures:

1. no D_k(p) is constant;
2. there is an m such that for every k > m, D_k(p) has no two equal elements;
3. every natural number m occurs in some D_k(p);
4. with absolute differences (Gilbreath's scheme), from some row on only 0, 1 and 2 occur.

On the page:

- Jan van Delden and Emmanuel Vantieghem proved conjecture 1. A constant row would make p_n a polynomial in n; also, every row k ≥ 1 has an odd first term and even later terms.
- Vantieghem argued conjecture 3 is false for odd m (15 is in no D_k for k < 30000), and noted conjecture 4 implies Gilbreath's conjecture.
- On conjecture 2 van Delden wrote that "proving this in this form would be something". It stayed open.

## The math

The chain of claims:

| # | Claim | Why | Validated |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | D_k p(n) = Σ_(j=0)^(k−1) (−1)^(k−1−j) C(k−1, j)·g_(n+j) | D_k p = D_(k−1) g, with g the gap sequence | algebra |
| 2 | Σ_(n≤N) \|D_k p(n)\| ≤ 2^(k−1)·Σ_(m≤N+k−1) g_m < 2^(k−1)·p_(N+k) | triangle inequality; each g_m appears with total weight at most Σ_j C(k−1, j) = 2^(k−1) | k ≤ 24, N = 50000 (actual mean about ¼ of the bound) |
| 3 | for N ≥ k ≥ 1 the mean of \|D_k p(n)\| over n ≤ N is below 2^(k+1)·ln(2N) | p_m < 2m ln m for m ≥ 3 (Rosser), and N + k ≤ 2N | theorem |
| 4 | at least N/2 of the first N terms satisfy \|D_k p(n)\| ≤ 2^(k+2)·ln(2N) | Markov's inequality | — |
| 5 | those terms (n ≥ 2) are even, so they take at most 2^(k+2)·ln(2N) + 1 values | parity (Vantieghem) | k ≤ 24 |
| 6 | once N/2 exceeds that number of values, two terms are equal; there are at least about N/2^(k+5) equal pairs among the first N terms, hence infinitely many | pigeonhole; Cauchy–Schwarz on the value counts | — |

So for every k ≥ 1 the sequence D_k(p) has two equal elements, and conjecture 2 fails for every m. The proof uses only Chebyshev-size information about the primes. The same argument works for any increasing integer sequence a_n with a_N = o(N²), for every k.

**What the data show.** The first coincidence in D_k(p), as (positions, value):

| k | 1 | 2 | 9 | 12 | 20 | 24 | 33 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| positions | 2, 3 | 3, 5 | 2, 14 | 77, 132 | 279, 875 | 4973, 5384 | 54422, 139282 |
| value | 2 | 2 | 220 | 5848 | −468924 | −11153800 | 11608813414 |

For k ≥ 34 no coincidence occurs among the primes below 3·10⁶. The proof's threshold, N ≈ 2^(k+3) ln N, is then far beyond that range.

**Conjectures 3 and 4.** By the parity fact, an odd m can occur only as the first term of some row, so conjecture 3 for odd m depends on the first terms D_k p(1) alone. These grow roughly like 2^k, which supports Vantieghem's view that most odd m never occur. Conjecture 4 (only 0, 1, 2 from some row on) implies Gilbreath's conjecture and is far out of reach. Odlyzko's check of 3.4·10¹¹ terms is on the page through Wikipedia.
