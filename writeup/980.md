# Puzzle 980: The "Commas" sequence — why some differences never occur

> **In short:** Q1 asked why some differences never occur in A121805, and Q2 why 5 and 55 are the most common. The explanation: (1) in a block with first digit F the last digit steps by F (mod 10), so the block uses one orbit of differences; (2) a parity invariant fixes which orbit; (3) the six actual block entries decide the rest. This explains the missing set {4, 24, 44, 64, 84, 15, 65} and the counts.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_980.htm)

**Status:** answered · Q1 and Q2 explained; Q3 was answered on the page (Resta, Volpatti)

## The question

A121805 (Angelini) runs: a(1) = 1, and a(n+1) is the smallest number above a(n) with a(n+1) − a(n) = 10·(last digit of a(n)) + (first digit of a(n+1)). It has 2137453 terms and ends at 99999945.

- **Q1:** why do the differences 15, 65 and 4, 24, 44, 64, 84 never occur (besides the multiples of 10)?
- **Q2:** why are D = 5 and D = 55 the most common (166669 each)?

## The answer

**1. Orbits inside a block.** Take a run of terms with the same number of digits j and the same first digit F. Each step adds 10L + F, so the last digit goes L ↦ L + F (mod 10). The block therefore only uses D = 10L + F for L on one orbit:

- **F = 5:** the orbit is {L, L + 5}, so a block uses one pair: {05, 55}, {15, 65}, {25, 75}, {35, 85} or {45, 95}.
- **F = 4:** the orbit is the even digits or the odd digits, so a block uses {04, 24, 44, 64, 84} or {14, 34, 54, 74, 94}.
- **F = 2, 6, 8:** likewise one parity class. **F = 1, 3, 7, 9:** all ten last digits.

**2. A parity invariant.** a(n+1) ≡ a(n) + F (mod 2), because 10L is even.

- In blocks with even F, the parity of the terms never changes.
- A 4-block therefore keeps the parity of its first term.
- The first term of a 5-block is the last 4-term plus 10L + 5, so the parity flips at that step.

**3. The six actual block entries.**

| digits | 4-block entered at | last digit | 5-block entered at | last digit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 461 | 1 | 530 | 0 |
| 4 | 4093 | 3 | 5012 | 2 |
| 5 | 40017 | 7 | 50008 | 8 |
| 6 | 400093 | 3 | 500028 | 8 |
| 7 | 4000061 | 1 | 5000034 | 4 |
| 8 | 40000039 | 9 | 50000030 | 0 |

- **Set 2.** Every 4-block was entered with an odd last digit, so no 4-block ever uses {04, 24, 44, 64, 84}.
- **Set 1.** By the parity flip, every 5-block was entered with an even last digit. An even entry gives the pairs {0,5}, {2,7}, {4,9}, {6,1} or {8,3}. Only an entry ending in 6 would give {15, 65}, and none of the six entries ends in 6.

Why the entries fall this way is a property of this one finite orbit, not a theorem. The block transitions act as a finite-state machine. In an F = 3 block, the state returns every 10 steps after an increase of exactly 480, and 10^j ≡ 160 (mod 480) for j ≥ 5. The sequence stops at 99999945, so there are only six blocks of each kind.

**4. Q2: the counts.** A block of size S with orbit O has about S / mean(O) terms, spread evenly over the |O| values of D.

- The 5-block from 5·10⁷ uses the pair {05, 55}. That is the fewest D values of any block (2), with the smallest mean step of any block (30).
- It contributes 10⁷/60 = 166666.7 of each, and the small block 530–599 adds about 1.2. The prediction is 166668, against 166669 observed.

The same rule reproduces the whole table, for example:

| D | predicted | observed |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 47624 | 47624 |
| 1 | 24154 | 24154 |
| 45 | 7143 | 7143 |
| 35 | 916 | 918 |

The largest relative error over all D with more than 1000 occurrences is 0.03 %. The values of D the model never predicts are exactly 4, 24, 44, 64, 84, 15 and 65.
