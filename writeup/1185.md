# Puzzle 1185: Primes that divide their own look-and-say value

> **In short:** 2, 5, 108947 and 4940867 divide their look-and-say reading LS(p) (4940867 reads "one 4, one 9, one 4, one 0, one 8, one 6, one 7" = 14191410181617 = 3²·337·947·4940867); the page asks for a fifth such prime or an argument that none exists, and the OEIS search bound is 4·10¹². (1) LS(p) has at most twice as many digits as p and ends in the last digit of p; nothing else ties it to p, so p | LS(p) is a 1-in-p event. (2) The expected count below X is Σ 1/p = ln ln X + 0.26: 3.6 below 4·10¹² (4 found). (3) So a fifth prime is expected, infinitely many in fact, but the median of the fifth is near 10²⁵; it is below 10¹⁵ with probability 0.16. Our own search confirms that the four are the only ones below 10¹¹.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1185.htm)

**Status:** open · explained (the four known primes are the only solutions below 10¹¹, direct, and below 4·10¹² in the OEIS; heuristic: infinitely many, growing like ln ln X, the fifth expected near 10²⁵)

## The question

G. L. Honaker, Jr. asks about his Prime Curio 4940867 and the sequence A247148, the primes p that divide LS(p), where LS is the look-and-say function (A045918): 2, 5, 108947, 4940867. The fourth term was found by Alois P. Heinz in 2014. Q: find the fifth term, or give an argument that it cannot exist. The OEIS entry adds a(5) > 4·10¹² (Tyler Busby, 2023).

## How we found it

The same reasoning as for puzzles [1101](1101.md) and [1218](1218.md): a prime p is asked to divide a quantity built from its own digits whose size is a fixed power of p. The look-and-say value is a rearrangement of p's digits with run counts inserted, so LS(p) mod p has no reason to prefer or avoid 0, and p | LS(p) behaves like a coin with probability 1/p. The sum of 1/p over the primes grows like ln ln X, which says both that more solutions exist and that the next one is far out.

## The math

**Step 1 (proved). Form and size.** Write the decimal digits of p as runs: c₁ copies of d₁, then c₂ copies of d₂, and so on. LS(p) is the concatenation c₁ d₁ c₂ d₂ … . A run count is a single digit unless the run has 10 or more digits, so a d-digit prime has an LS value of at most 2d digits, hence LS(p) < 10^{2d} ≤ 100p². (Nine primes below 10¹¹ contain a run of ten or more equal digits, 11111111113 to 79999999999; their LS values have five digits, smaller than p, so they are not solutions. The code handles two-digit run counts anyway.) The last digit of LS(p) is the last digit of p. So when p | LS(p) and p is coprime to 10, the quotient m = LS(p)/p satisfies (m − 1)p ≡ 0 (mod 10), that is m ≡ 1 (mod 10): the quotients are 1019011 and 2872251 for the two large terms. There are no trivial solutions beyond 2 and 5: 3 and 7 give 13 and 17.

**Step 2 (computed). Direct check.** A segmented sieve to 10¹¹ with LS(p) computed as a 128-bit integer and reduced mod p ([`code/p1185_search.c`](code/p1185_search.c), two threads, a few minutes) finds exactly the four known primes among the 4118054813 primes below 10¹¹ (the prime count matches the known value of π(10¹¹)). A Python search below 10⁷ with a string-based LS agrees.

**Step 3 (heuristic). Expected count.** With probability 1/p per prime, the expected number of solutions with p ≤ X is Σ_{p≤X} 1/p = ln ln X + M, M = 0.2615 (Mertens; the exact sum below 10⁷ is 3.04 against the formula's 3.04).

| X | 10⁷ | 10¹¹ | 4·10¹² | 10¹⁵ | 10²⁰ | 1.6·10²⁵ | 10³⁰ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| expected count | 3.04 | 3.49 | 3.63 | 3.80 | 4.09 | 4.32 | 4.50 |

Four solutions below 4·10¹² against an expectation of 3.63 is ordinary (Poisson probability of at least 4: 0.49). Given that a(5) > 4·10¹², the probability that it lies below X is 1 − exp(−(ln ln X − ln ln 4·10¹²)): 0.03 for X = 10¹³, 0.16 for 10¹⁵, 0.37 for 10²⁰ and 0.50 at X = (4·10¹²)² = 1.6·10²⁵, the median. So the answer to the page's question is heuristically yes, a fifth term exists (and infinitely many more), but no search can be expected to find it: extending the search tenfold, from 4·10¹² to 4·10¹³, buys only a 3 % chance.
