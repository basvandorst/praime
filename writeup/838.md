# Puzzle 838: Prime Kaprekar numbers that are not repunits (attempt)

> **In short:** A prime p is a Kaprekar number exactly when p divides N = 10ⁿ − 1 for some n and the cofactor m = N/p divides p − 1. Repunit primes R_n with n ≡ 1 (mod 9) are the case m = 9. We prove three things:
> - no non-repunit example exists for even n;
> - for odd n the prime p must be a primitive factor of Φₙ(10), which rules out every n with φ(n)/n < 0.48;
> - a search of n ≤ 600 finds only R₁₉. It sees every case whose other prime factors are all below 2·10⁶, so it is not exhaustive.
>
> A heuristic count gives far less than one expected example. So such primes very probably do not exist, but we have no proof.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_838.htm)

**Status:** open · even n proved impossible; odd n reduced and searched to n ≤ 600 (not exhaustive)

## The question

Puzzle 837 asked for prime Kaprekar numbers. Jan van Delden and Emmanuel Vantieghem showed that the repunit primes ≡ 1 (mod 9) are Kaprekar numbers. They noted that "there might exist prime divisors d of N = 10ⁿ − 1 that are not repunits but that satisfy d ≡ 1 (mod N/d)". Q: find one, or show that none exist.

## Reduction

A number k is Kaprekar when k² = a·10ⁿ + b with 0 < b < 10ⁿ and a + b = k. By Iannucci's theorem, this happens exactly when k ≡ 0 (mod d) and k ≡ 1 (mod N/d) for a unitary divisor d of N = 10ⁿ − 1, with k ≤ N.

For a prime k:

- d = 1 gives k = 1, and d = N gives k = N, which is composite.
- So d = k = p, and the conditions become p | N, gcd(p, m) = 1 and m = N/p divides p − 1.
- Then m < p, so p > √N. So **p is the one large prime factor of N, and the product m of all the other factors has to divide p − 1.**

The brute-force check below confirms this criterion against the Kaprekar definition for every prime below 2·10⁵, none of which is a Kaprekar number.

## Even n: impossible

Write n = 2k. Then N = (10ᵏ − 1)(10ᵏ + 1), and the two factors are coprime.

1. If p | 10ᵏ − 1, then p < √N, which is not allowed.
2. So p | 10ᵏ + 1, and √N < p ≤ 10ᵏ + 1 forces p = 10ᵏ + 1 and m = 10ᵏ − 1.
3. But m would then have to divide p − 1 = 10ᵏ, while gcd(10ᵏ − 1, 10ᵏ) = 1 and m ≥ 9.

## Odd n: reduced

1. **p is primitive.** If p | 10ᵈ − 1 for a proper divisor d of n, then d ≤ n/3 and p < √N. So p divides Φₙ(10) and p ≡ 1 (mod n).
2. **φ(n) cannot be too small.** Since Φₙ(10) < 11^φ(n), we need 11^φ(n) > 10^(n/2). That excludes, for example, n = 105, 315, 525, 735, 945 and 1155 (ten odd n below 2000).
3. **The repunit case.** When R_n itself is prime, m = 9 and the condition is R_n ≡ 1 (mod 9), i.e. n ≡ 1 (mod 9). That is the known family.

## Search

For each n ≤ 600 we removed all prime factors below 2·10⁶ from N and kept the smooth part m. We then tested whether the cofactor is a probable prime with m | cofactor − 1.

- The only hit is n = 19, which is R₁₉ with m = 9: the repunit case.
- Cases where N has two or more prime factors above 2·10⁶ are not seen. Such a p would need m, which then contains a prime above 2·10⁶, to divide p − 1.

## Heuristic

Take the smallest m that is not a repunit case: R_n = ℓ·p with a single prime ℓ ≡ 1 (mod 2n), for prime n.

- The condition p ≡ 1 (mod 9ℓ) holds with probability about 1/(9ℓ).
- R_n = ℓ·(prime) holds with probability about 1/(ℓ·ln R_n).
- Summed over ℓ ≥ 2n + 1 and over n, a rough estimate of the expected total is well below 1 (order 10⁻²; not computed precisely).
- Larger m are rarer still.

So non-repunit prime Kaprekar numbers very probably do not exist. A proof would need to control the factorisation of 10ⁿ − 1, which is out of reach.
