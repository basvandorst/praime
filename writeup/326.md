# Puzzle 326: One more Firoozbakht curio sequence

> **In short:** The numbers n with n = π(product of the factorials of the digits of n) are 0, 3, 34, 52, 2800414; the page asks for the next term. (1) A k-digit term needs a digit multiset whose factorial product lies in a short window, so only a few hundred products per k need π. (2) All were tested for k ≤ 11: the five known terms are the only ones below 10¹¹. (3) About 0.06 further terms are expected in total, so the sequence is almost surely complete.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_326.htm)

**Status:** answered (no term with 8 to 11 digits, exhaustive; heuristically no term at all beyond 2800414, expected 0.06)

## The question

Firoozbakht asks for the next term after 0 = π(0!), 3 = π(3!), 34 = π(3!·4!), 52 = π(5!·2!), 2800414 = π(2!·8!·0!·0!·4!·1!·4!). No contribution is on the page.

## How we found it

The product of the factorials depends only on the multiset of digits, and π(P) must have exactly k digits, which pins P into a window of width a factor of about 10. For each k the multisets whose product lands in the window are few (72 for k = 7, 204 for k = 11), and the digits 0 and 1 contribute nothing to the product, so distinct products are fewer still. For each product, π(P) is computed exactly; a cheap pre-check with li(P) (whose error is far below √P·ln P in this range) discards multisets that cannot match the leading digits of π(P).

## The math

**Step 1 (proved): the window.** If n = π(P) has k digits then 10^(k−1) ≤ π(P) < 10^k, i.e. p_(10^(k−1)) ≤ P < p_(10^k), where p_N is the N-th prime. With the bounds N(ln N + ln ln N − 1) < p_N < N(ln N + ln ln N) for N ≥ 6 (Dusart), P lies in [10^(k−1)(ln 10^(k−1) + ln ln 10^(k−1) − 1), 10^k(ln 10^k + ln ln 10^k)]. The search uses exactly this window (and tests all P below 100 directly for k = 1).

**Step 2 (computed): the search.** For k = 1 … 11 every multiset of k digits with product in the window was examined; the number of distinct products is 4, 7, 11, 22, 35, 50, 72, 96, 125, 162, 204. The only matches are the five known terms. For k = 11 the largest P is about 2.8·10¹², where π(P) takes 15 s with sympy; k = 12 would need about 250 evaluations of π near 3·10¹³ and was not run.

**Step 3 (heuristic): how many terms to expect.** Treat π(P) as a random k-digit number. A multiset M of k digits gives a term iff the digits of π(P_M) are exactly M, with probability (number of k-digit numbers with digit multiset M)/(9·10^(k−1)). Summing over the multisets in the window gives the expected number of k-digit terms: 0.156, 0.104, 0.074, 0.052, 0.040, 0.032, 0.025, 0.019, 0.015, 0.012 for k = 2 … 11, then 0.009, 0.007, … for k = 12, 13, …, about 0.06 in total beyond 11 digits and converging. (The three known multi-digit terms against an expectation of 0.5 for k ≤ 11 are a mild coincidence; the expectation is what the digit statistics of a counting function allow.) A term with k digits needs a product of k factorials of size only 10^k·k·ln 10, while a random digit string has product about 10^(2.1k); so the admissible strings are those made almost entirely of 0, 1, 2 and 3 with a few larger digits, and their share keeps falling.
