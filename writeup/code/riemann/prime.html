With too few zeros each method stops at the last chosen zero and starts to fail. With no usable zero at all, 128 falls back on “keep Σ ln p close to x”, which alone gives the primes up to 31. The zeros beyond the first thirty are computed here with the Riemann–Siegel formula and its first three correction terms, from the integers alone; checked against mpmath they are good to 10⁻⁶.

The other way round works too: the 25 primes below 100 fix all 10,142 zeros below height 10,000 (on average to 7·10⁻⁵). The count θ(t)/π + 1 + S(t), with S(t) summed over the prime powers up to 100, is a sum of smoothed unit steps at the zeros, and a least-squares fit finds the steps. Fed back into 139, these zeros give every prime up to 70,000. In general the prime powers up to 1.6·√(T/2π) fix every zero up to height T, the same length as in the Riemann–Siegel formula.

Measured off this page with the same formula: all 32 characters mod 120 need zeros only up to 0.004·πn, and all 192 characters mod 840 up to 0.0008·πn (every prime in 30,000–30,500 from zeros below height 77). In each case that is about one unit 2π of height per prime and per class: about two real numbers per prime at these safe settings, and between one and one and a third at the point where the first error appears.

Without zeros: 129 finds a prime from one Kronecker symbol of 1 + 8k·p!, and 132 is Golomb’s formula, the next prime from ζ(s) and the primes so far.

Nr is the position of the prime among all primes on the circle, or the transformation number when the source is the start number. The table follows the line filter. “Expected by chance” uses the same part of the circle as the filter: for “higher” only the numbers above each prime, for “lower” only those below. Click a row to select that prime. “Matched on residue mod 30” compares each destination only with numbers in the same class mod 30 (same parity, same remainder mod 3 and mod 5), so a transformation that avoids multiples of 2, 3 or 5 gets a fair baseline.

Atlas: every transformation on this circle

Two numbers describe each transformation. Sieve score: how often its destinations avoid the factors 2, 3 and 5, relative to random (1 = like random numbers, 3.75 = every destination coprime to 30, the maximum a sieve on 2, 3, 5 can give). Mixing: distinct destinations compared with a random mapping (1 = random, below 1 = many sources pile onto the same destinations, above 1 = permutation-like). Bits: log₂(hits ÷ fair expected): information about primality beyond the sieve. The model predicts ≈ 0 bits for every transformation; points far from 0 should have low mixing (correlated hits), not real foresight.
