Prime squares

A grid of dots, numbered from point zero in the top left corner: the top row is 0 to 99, the next row 100 to 199, and so on. The primes are lit. For every prime p below 100 a square of side p is drawn from point zero, so its corner on the top row sits exactly on the prime p, and every dot on its outline is crossed. The crossed dots are prime about twice as often as the other dots. Is that the primes? Switch the squares to composite sizes such as 9, 21 or 91 and the effect stays: the right edge of the square of p is the column p, p + 100, p + 200, …, which never holds a multiple of 2 or 5. Tap or hover a dot to read it. Pair clocks: prime35, overview: all pages.

Why the crossed dots are richer in primes

In the row numbering every column is one residue class mod the grid size: the dot (x, y) is x + 100y. The right edge of the square of p is the column x = p, the numbers p, p + 100, …, p + 100(p − 1). For a prime p other than 2 and 5 that column shares no factor with 100, so it skips every multiple of 2 and of 5, and only 40 of the 100 columns are like that. Those 40 columns hold all primes except 2 and 5, so each is 2.5 times richer in primes than an average column. The top edge, row p, is an ordinary run of p + 1 consecutive numbers starting at 100p. Squares of composite odd sizes that do not end in 5 have the same kind of right edge, and on the 100 grid their crossed dots are prime 21.86 % of the time, against 21.91 % for the prime squares; squares of even sizes have right edges without a single prime except 2. In the shell numbering a square's outline holds the consecutive numbers p2 … p2 + 2p, an ordinary stretch, and the effect disappears; that every such stretch holds a prime is Legendre's conjecture (true for every square drawn here, unproved in general).
