Spot the primes

Two strips show the same stretch of numbers. In one, the marked numbers are the true primes. In the other, a forger has marked fakes: random numbers with exactly the right density, and the better forgers also avoid the multiples of small primes, as primes do. Which strip is real? Five detectives try too, each with a single statistic, and a rule fixed in advance: the primes are the more regular set. Trains: prime19, bricks: prime17, baskets: prime16, dance: prime13, turtle: prime8.

The detectives

Each detective computes one number for both strips and calls the smaller one real. Sieve: how many marked numbers have a prime factor up to 31. Gaps: the mean square of the gaps divided by the squared mean gap. Repeats: the share of consecutive marked numbers with the same last digit. Baskets of 100 and of 1,000: the variance of the counts minus h / ln n in baskets of that width, divided by the mean of h / ln n.

How often each detective is right (rounds played by a program)

The clumsy forger is caught by every detective. The careful and the skilled forger pass the gap and repeat tests in short strips but not the sieve test. The master forger passes the sieve test by construction and fools every detective but one most of the time; the one with the wide baskets catches it in more than 4 rounds of 5 with strips of 100,000: the primes' counts in wide baskets vary less than any fixed sieve can imitate, because the primes are sieved by every prime up to the square root, so their sieve grows with the numbers (prime16). The repeat detective is wrong more often than right against the deeper forgers: such fakes repeat their last digit slightly less than the primes do (prime13).
