Jumping champion

Hop from prime to prime: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, … Each hop has a length, the gap. Which length is the most common, the jumping champion? At 100 it is 2, the twin hop (and for a while 4); from 947 on, at every x up to 107 and at 108, it is 6. The reason is the prime 3: for a prime p > 3, one of p + 2 and p + 4 is a multiple of 3 for each residue of p, but p + 6 never is. In the limit this makes a gap of 6 twice as likely as a gap of 2 or 4 (Hardy–Littlewood); up to 108 it is 1.75 times as likely. Gaps of 30 = 2 · 3 · 5 avoid 5 as well, but long hops are rarer among small primes; Odlyzko, Rubinstein and Wolf (1999) estimate that 30 takes over only near 1035, then 210. Wieferich's needle: prime33, high jump (the record gaps): prime23, overview: all pages.

The race up to 108

Counted by a separate program. A simple model gives a gap d the weight S(d) · e−d / ln x, where S(d) = Π (p − 1)/(p − 2) over the odd primes p dividing d (the Hardy–Littlewood singular series; S(6) = 2, S(30) = 8/3) and the exponential approximates Cramér's chance that no prime falls in between. Our prediction was that 6 is the sole champion at every power of ten from 103 to 108 (it held) and that this model gets N(30)/N(6) within 20 % at 108 (it failed: 0.362 against the counted 0.290). Letting every prime t use its own ln t instead of ln x, after the run, gives 0.333, still 15 % high, and the model puts N(6)/N(2) at 1.59 against the counted 1.75: the simple exponential is too crude for the shape of the gap distribution at these sizes. The page and the program agree on the gap counts up to 103 to 107.
