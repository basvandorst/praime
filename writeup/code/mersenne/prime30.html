Möbius walker

A walker goes along the numbers 1, 2, 3, … At a square-free number with an even number of prime factors (1, 6, 10, 14, 15, …) it steps forward, at one with an odd number (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 30, …) it steps back, and at numbers divisible by a square (4, 8, 9, 12, …) it stands still. Its position after x numbers is the Mertens function M(x). Drunk walkers that toss a coin at the same square-free numbers stray about 0.78 √x away, and sooner or later beyond √x. The Möbius walker stays within about a fifth of √x: the zeros of the zeta function hold it on a short leash. Rabbit clocks: prime29, census (counting prime factors): prime14, overview: all pages.

How far they stray, counted up to 10,000,000

A separate program computed M(x) up to 107 and 200 drunk walkers on the same 6,079,291 square-free numbers. From x = 200 on, every one of the 200 drunk walkers passed √x somewhere (the median largest |W| / √x was 1.88, the smallest 1.14). The Möbius walker never came closer than 0.566 of √x (at x = 200), and from 106 to 107 no closer than 0.418. Averaged over ln x from 104 to 107, (M(x) / √x)2 is 0.0289, against 6/π2 = 0.608 for a drunk walker. Under the Riemann hypothesis and further assumptions, Ng (2004) showed that this average equals the sum of 1 / |ρ ζ′(ρ)|2 over the zeros ρ of the zeta function; the first 200 zeros give 0.0286. Mertens's conjecture |M(x)| < √x is false (Odlyzko and te Riele, 1985), but nobody has found an x where it fails: the first exception lies far beyond any count. The page and the program agree on M(10k) for k = 2 to 7.
