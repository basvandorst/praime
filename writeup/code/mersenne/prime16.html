Prime baskets

Drop a basket of width h somewhere between N/2 and N and count the primes that fall into it. Do it again and again. If the primes were scattered at random with the right density (Cramér's model), the counts would spread like a Poisson count: the variance would equal the mean. The primes spread much less, and the wider the basket, the less. Bricks: prime17, carousel: prime15, census: prime14, Goldbach comet: prime10, turtle: prime8.

How much less than chance, measured up to 1,000,000 and 10,000,000

For every start n between N/2 and N (not only the dropped baskets) the page computes the exact spread. Weighted by ln p (the sum of ln p over the primes and prime powers in the basket), Montgomery and Soundararajan (2004) derived from a uniform form of the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture that the variance is h (ln(n/h) + B) with B = 1 − γ − ln 2π = −1.415, for ln N ≤ h ≤ √N; Cramér's model gives h ln n. Unweighted, dividing by ln² n gives the heuristic variance / mean = 1 − (ln h − B) / ln n. The counts are compared with li(n + h) − li(n), so the slow thinning of the primes does not count as spread. Fake primes with a fixed sieve also spread less than Poisson, but by the same factor for every width; only the primes spread less and less as the basket grows. The page and a separate program agree on all these spreads for N = 100,000 and 1,000,000.
