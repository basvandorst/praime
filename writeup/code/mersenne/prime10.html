Goldbach comet

Goldbach guessed in 1742 that every even number from 4 on is a sum of two primes: 10 = 3 + 7 = 5 + 5. In how many ways? Plot that count for every even number and the points form a comet with bright bands. Click a point to see its sums. Waterfall: prime9, turtle: prime8, Galton board: prime7.

What the comet shows, measured up to 5,000 and up to 1,000,000

Hardy and Littlewood (1923) predicted the number of ordered sums n = p + q as 2 C2 S(n) ∫ dt / (ln t · ln(n − t)), with C2 = 0.6601… and S(n) the product of (p − 1)/(p − 2) over the odd primes p dividing n. So the bands are the small primes: a multiple of 3 has twice as many sums (factor 2/1), a multiple of 5 a third more (4/3), of 7 a fifth more (6/5). A random p is equally likely to leave any remainder, but when 3 divides n, n − p is a multiple of 3 only if p is, so no pair is lost to the factor 3. How wide is the comet once the bands are divided out? For the even numbers from 500,000 to 1,000,000 the count divided by the prediction (a slow drift removed) spreads by 0.0080. Fake primes, with the right density and the true primes up to 100, spread by 0.0124: the real comet is about 35 % narrower. Fake primes that avoid the multiples of the primes up to 13, 31 or 100 spread by 0.0129, 0.0118 and 0.0107 (4 runs per model): avoiding a few small primes does not help, only a deeper sieve brings the fakes closer, so the narrowness looks like the work of the sieve as a whole. Up to 5,000: 0.057 against 0.085 for the fakes. We found no source that compares the width of the comet with chance. Every even number from 4 to 1,000,000 has at least one sum; Goldbach's guess has been checked up to 4 · 1018 (Oliveira e Silva, Herzog and Pardi, 2014). The page and a separate program agree on the counts, the even numbers with a single sum (4, 6, 8, 12), the fewest sums in the upper half, the band ratios and the width, up to 5,000, 20,000 and 200,000.
