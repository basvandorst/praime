Prime doorman

At the door of a prime p stand the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, … The doorman lets in the squares: the numbers that leave the same remainder after division by p as some square. For p = 7 the squares 1, 4, 9 = 2, 16 = 2, … leave 1, 2, 4, so 1, 2, 4 go in and 3 is the first turned away. Half of the primes turn away 2 already, a quarter 3, an eighth 5: Erdős's law. Compass: prime20, carousel (the first number that visits every seat): prime15, Galton board: prime7.

Erdős's law, measured up to 100,000 and 1,000,000

The first number turned away is always a prime q, and it is q exactly when every smaller prime is a square mod p and q is not. By quadratic reciprocity each of these is a condition on the remainder of p, and by Dirichlet's theorem the primes satisfy each combination equally often, so the share with first refusal at the k-th prime tends to 2−k (Erdős, 1961), and the mean tends to Σ pk / 2k = 3.674644. The shares are on target within 0.002, but the mean creeps up from below: the conditions for a late refusal repeat only every 8 · 3 · 5 · 7 · … numbers, and up to a million the rare primes that turn away 31 or 43 (the pseudosquares of prime7) have not all appeared. The least primitive root, the first knocker who would visit every seat of the carousel, averages 4.73 and 4.88; Elliott and Murata conjectured that its mean tends to a constant. The page and a separate program agree on every count and both means, up to 100,000 and 1,000,000.
