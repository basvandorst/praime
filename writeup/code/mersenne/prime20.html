Prime compass

Fermat knew that every prime 1 more than a multiple of 4 is a sum of two squares: 13 = 3² + 2². Draw the point (3, 2): it is a needle at an angle. Every such prime gives two needles, (a, b) and (b, a), and the prime 2 one at 45°. Hecke proved in 1918 that the needles spread evenly over the quarter circle. Rudnick and Waxman (2019) looked closer: in narrow sectors the needles are spread far more evenly than random needles would be. Trains: prime19, baskets (the same effect on the number line): prime16.

Measured up to norm 108

The variance of the number of needles in a sector of a fraction 1/K of the quarter circle, averaged over 64 positions of the sectors, divided by the mean N/K (N needles in all; the inert primes 3, 7, 11 … with p² up to the bound sit at angle 0). Random needles give 1. Rudnick and Waxman conjecture min(1, 2 log K / log N) for large K and N. The measured curve rises with the same slope 2 in log K / log N, but lies below their line: by about 0.47 at norm 107 and 0.35 at 108, a gap that shrinks slowly. For very fine sectors it levels off near 0.93, close to 1 − 1 / ln N. Fake needles (lattice points (a, b) with a, b coprime and a² + b² odd, each kept with a chance that gives the right density) are also below 1, because keeping a point is a coin flip with a sizeable chance (their floor is 0.75), but averaged over 10 sets of fakes they stay well above the primes at every K from 64 on: the extra evenness of the primes is arithmetic, not a property of the lattice. The fake curve on the page is a single set and scatters at small K; for K below about 64 every estimate rests on too few sectors to mean much. The page and a separate program agree on the variances of the primes for every K, up to 1,000,000 and 10,000,000.
