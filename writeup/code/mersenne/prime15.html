Prime carousel

Take a prime, say 7, and put the seats 1 to 6 in a ring. Start at seat 1 and keep multiplying by 2, keeping only the remainder after division by 7: 1 → 2 → 4 → 1. That ride visits only three seats. With 3 instead: 1 → 3 → 2 → 6 → 4 → 5 → 1, every seat. When every seat is visited, the number is a primitive root of the prime. Artin guessed in 1927 that 2 is a primitive root for a fixed share of all primes, about 37.4 %. Census: prime14, dance: prime13, radio: prime11.

The carousel of the prime 101

Artin's guess, measured up to 5,000 and up to 1,000,000

Artin's constant A = ∏ (1 − 1/(q(q − 1))) over all primes q is 0.3739558… For a base that is not a square, not −1 and has no square factor, the predicted share is A, except when the base is 1 more than a multiple of 4: then A is multiplied by 1 − μ(|a|) ∏ 1/(q² − q − 1) over the primes q dividing a (Heilbronn's correction; μ is +1 or −1 as a has an even or odd number of prime factors). So 5 should be a primitive root more often (0.3936), −3 much more often (0.4488) and 21 a little less often (0.3721). Hooley proved all this in 1967, assuming the generalised Riemann hypothesis; without that assumption it is still open even for the base 2. The measured shares agree with every prediction to within about one binomial standard deviation, up to 1,000,000 and up to 10,000,000. The page and a separate program agree on every count, up to 5,000, 1,000,000 and 10,000,000.
