Prime rain

Every prime is a cloud. The cloud 2 rains on every second number, the cloud 3 on every third, and so on. After the clouds up to y, which numbers are still dry? The forecast treats the clouds as independent: each cloud p leaves a share 1 − 1/p dry, so x · (1 − 1/2)(1 − 1/3)(1 − 1/5)… numbers stay dry (Mertens). For small clouds the forecast is excellent. Near y = √x it fails: then the dry numbers are just the primes above y, and there are fewer of them than the forecast says. Dance: prime13, radio: prime11, Goldbach comet: prime10, turtle: prime8.

Where the forecast fails, measured up to 5,000 and up to 1,000,000

The forecast x ∏ (1 − 1/p) assumes that the clouds act independently. They do not quite, because a number up to x has room for only a few large prime factors. Buchstab's function ω(u) gives the true share: the dry count is about x ω(u) / ln y, while the forecast is about x e−γ / ln y (Mertens), so the ratio tends to eγ ω(u). It is 1 for small clouds, dips to eγ/2 = 0.8905 at y = √x, overshoots 1 slightly near u = 2.8 (ω has its first maximum 0.567 above e−γ = 0.561 there) and then settles. At finite x the dip is much shallower and comes before √x: at 1,000,000 it reaches 0.945 at y = 701, at 5,000 only 0.978 at y = 47, and at √x the ratio is back at 0.968 and 1.003. Buchstab's count in its integral form, ∫ ω(ln t / ln y) / ln y dt from y to x, follows the measured ratio closely at 1,000,000 (0.9689 against 0.9675 at √x) but is 2 to 4 % off at 5,000. The overshoot near u = 3 shows at 1,000,000 (1.0037 at y = 101) but not at 5,000 (0.997 at y = 17). The page and a separate program agree on the dry count and the forecast after every cloud, up to 5,000 and up to 1,000,000.
