Prime lighthouse

Put every prime p at distance p from the centre and at angle p radians. Close in you see spirals; further out, 20 spiral arms; far out, 280 straight rays. The reason: 2π is close to 44/7 and very close to 710/113, so numbers that differ by 44 (or 710) land at almost the same angle, and a prime can only sit on the arms whose remainder mod 44 (or 710) shares no factor with it: φ(44) = 20 arms and φ(710) = 280 rays. Fairer than fair, counted up to 10,000,000

If the primes picked their arm at random, the counts would scatter like a multinomial sample, and the chi-square sum would be about the number of arms minus one. The primes are far more even: up to 10,000,000 the chi-square is 0.8 for the 20 arms mod 44 (about 19 for random arms) and 60.8 for the 280 rays mod 710 (about 279). For a fixed number of arms this is expected: the unevenness of the primes over the arms is governed by the zeros of Dirichlet L-functions and grows like √x / ln x per arm, so the chi-square shrinks like 1 / ln x. The arms of the squares lag behind: Chebyshev's bias, of about c · π(√x) / (2 φ(q)) primes, where c = 4 is the number of square roots of each square. For mod 44 that is 44.6, measured 45.7; for mod 710 it is 3.2, measured 5.1. The lag is exactly a combination of three character sums, each of which carries this bias plus a fluctuation of about ±100, so the close match for mod 44 is partly luck. The page and a separate program agree on all arm counts.
