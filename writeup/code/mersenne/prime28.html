Brun's ladder

Every pair of twin primes adds a rung to a ladder: (3, 5) adds 1/3 + 1/5, (5, 7) adds 1/5 + 1/7, (11, 13) adds 1/11 + 1/13, and so on. Nobody knows whether there are infinitely many rungs, but Brun proved in 1919 that the ladder never climbs past a finite height. Where does it stop? The climb is so slow that the numbers alone hardly tell: a correction of 4C2 / ln x for the rungs still to come does. Rabbit clocks: prime29, lighthouse: prime27, star clusters (the twins are the smallest cluster): prime26, overview: all pages.

How high, counted up to 109

Counted by a separate program up to 109. The height itself creeps: 1.7748 at 109, still 0.127 below the top. The correction 4C2 / ln x estimates the rungs of all twins above x (twins have density 2C2 / ln² t, each rung is about 2/t, and the integral from x on is 4C2 / ln x); with it, the estimate is within 0.0025 of Brun's constant at every power of ten from 100 on, within 0.0022 everywhere from 104 on, and within about 10−5 from 108 on (the 3.4 · 10−7 at exactly 109 is a lucky crossing). Brun's constant 1.902160583… is the estimate of Sebah and Demichel from all twins below 1016 by the same extrapolation; it assumes the Hardy–Littlewood density of twins, and proved are only bounds such as 1.84 < B < 2.29. Brun proved that the height is finite; it is not known whether that is because the rungs stop (finitely many twins) or although they go on forever. The page and the program agree on the 58,980 twin pairs and the height up to 10,000,000.
