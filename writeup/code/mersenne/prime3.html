Prime gears

The numbers from 1 up as gears, as many as you set (up to 5,000). Gear k has k teeth, and one tick moves every gear one tooth, so gear k is back home every k ticks. At tick t the gears at home are exactly the divisors of t, and t is prime when only gear 1 and gear t are home. Press Start and watch. Which machine has the best results: the table at the bottom. Machines 10, 11 and 12 let one wheel decide whether a number is prime, and none of them has made a mistake in any test; machine 13 is proved and slow, machines 14 and 15 are proved and fast when n − 1 or n + 1 can be taken apart, and machine 16 chooses a wheel whose size can be taken apart: the contest and what each wheel can and cannot do.

How many: 1 to 30
Speed: 1 ticks per second

  • The wheels that decide win without a trick. On machines 10 to 16 each lamp is a decision: wheel n goes home at tick n only when n is prime, and stays there. At tick 241 all 53 prime lamps are on, and no other lamp.
  • Back and forth also has them all, for a simple reason. At tick 36 its wheels are exactly back where they started, so every wheel is home, prime or not.
  • Fibonacci and doubling come next. On these a wheel that is home at tick a is home at every multiple of a, so at a tick with many divisors (27,720, 55,440, 83,160) half of the prime wheels are home together.
  • On the plain wheels at most six primes are home at once. The tick must be a multiple of every wheel that is home, and 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 = 30,030 is the last such product below 100,000.
  • Two machines were removed. The tape and the square jumps gave the same results as machine 1.

One wheel decides: which wheel can be trusted

A harder contest. Wheel n must be home after n turns exactly when n is prime. On every machine below each prime comes home; that is proved. The mistakes are the numbers that are not prime and come home too. All machines were run against the odd numbers that are not prime.

  • The rule that wins: give the number a wheel it does not fit. A number n fits a ring of r cells when n × n leaves remainder 1 after division by r, and it fits a two-cell wheel with step c when c is a square in the arithmetic of remainders after division by n. Every mistake of Pascal's wheel that we found is a number that fits its ring: 42,120 on rings of 2 to 40 cells below 1010, not one that does not fit.
  • Checked as far as the lists go. A number that fools Pascal's wheel must fool the doubling wheel first (add up the cells: the sum doubles every turn). All 118,968,378 odd numbers below 264 that fool the doubling wheel are known, and we put every one on the smallest ring it does not fit and on its two-cell wheel: none came home. We also tried every one of them on each ring of up to 22 cells that it does not fit: none came home.
  • Is this the gear that amazes mathematicians? Not yet, and we say so plainly. The last three rows are open problems. Nobody has proved that they never make a mistake. For the pair of wheels there is a prize of 620 dollars, offered by Pomerance, Selfridge and Wagstaff; for Pascal's wheel the claim is a sibling of Agrawal's conjecture (his has x − 1 where the wheel has x + 1; on a ring with an even number of cells the two are the same); the two-cell wheel is a Frobenius test, for which no mistake is known either. There are reasons to expect a mistake at some very large number (Pomerance for the pair; Lenstra and Pomerance for Pascal's wheel). A proof for any of the three, or the first number that fools one, would be news.
  • Second round: fold the wheel. A ring has symmetries: renumber the cells and it is the same ring. Multiply the wheel by its mirror images and the product lives on a few cells: 5 cells fold onto 2, 7 cells onto 3. A number that fools the big ring must fool the folded wheel; that is proved.
  • Pascal's wheel on 5 cells contains the Fibonacci wheel. Folded onto 2 cells it turns by the square of the golden ratio. So every mistake of the 5 cells is a mistake of machine 6 and of machine 7 too (all 628 below 1010 are), and a number that does not fit 5 cells and still came home would win the 620 dollars.
  • The rule holds one level down. Folded wheels alone made 518 mistakes below 107, every one by a number that moves the cells round with order 2: it fits. Where a number must move three cells round, nothing came home. That is machine 12, the three-cell wheel.
  • On Mersenne numbers the two-cell wheel is proved. For 2p − 1 its step is always 3, and home is exactly the Lucas–Lehmer test.
  • Proved or fast, and both only for special numbers. Wilson's wheel (machine 13) never errs and that is proved, but it needs n − 1 turns and none can be skipped. Lucas's wheel (machine 14) is proved and fast, but only when the primes that divide n − 1 are known; that is how numbers such as 3 × 22208 + 1 are proved. Machine 15 does the same with n + 1 and proves every Mersenne prime: type 2^4423-1 there. The proved ring test of Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena needs more than 19,562,929 cells for 24423 − 1, where machine 10 uses 5. In one timing run on that number Lucas–Lehmer took 0.02 seconds, the two-cell wheel 0.06 and Pascal's wheel 0.33. For every number there is a method that is proved and works in practice, but it is not one wheel: a curve gives wheels whose size can be chosen, and one searches for a wheel whose size comes apart. Machine 16 is a small version of it; the largest such proof we found reported has 109,297 digits. One small wheel with few turns, for every number and with a proof, is what is missing.
  • What they can do today. Both wheels need a few cells and about as many steps as the number has binary digits, so they also run on numbers of a thousand digits: choose machine 10 or 11 and type one.
