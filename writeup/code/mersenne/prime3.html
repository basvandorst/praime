Prime gears

The numbers from 1 up as gears, as many as you set (up to 5,000). Gear k has k teeth, and one tick moves every gear one tooth, so gear k is back home every k ticks. At tick t the gears at home are exactly the divisors of t, and t is prime when only gear 1 and gear t are home. Press Start and watch. Which machine has the best results: the table at the bottom. Machines 10, 11 and 12 let one wheel decide whether a number is prime, and none of them has made a mistake in any test; machine 13 is proved and slow, machines 14 and 15 are proved and fast when n − 1 or n + 1 can be taken apart, machine 16 chooses a wheel whose size can be taken apart, machine 17 makes a chain of such wheels that proves a number of 100 digits in about a second, machine 18 writes a receipt, so that a test of 2^p − 1 can be checked without doing it again, and machine 19 is one small wheel that is settled for every number below 2^64: the contest, who wins where and what each wheel can and cannot do.

How many: 1 to 30
Speed: 1 ticks per second

  • The wheels that decide win without a trick. On machines 10 to 17 and 19 each lamp is a decision: wheel n goes home at tick n only when n is prime, and stays there. At tick 241 all 53 prime lamps are on, and no other lamp. Machine 18 asks another question (is 2p − 1 prime?), so only the 12 Mersenne exponents light up.
  • Back and forth also has them all, for a simple reason. At tick 36 its wheels are exactly back where they started, so every wheel is home, prime or not.
  • Fibonacci and doubling come next. On these a wheel that is home at tick a is home at every multiple of a, so at a tick with many divisors (27,720, 55,440, 83,160) half of the prime wheels are home together.
  • On the plain wheels at most six primes are home at once. The tick must be a multiple of every wheel that is home, and 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 = 30,030 is the last such product below 100,000.
  • Two machines were removed. The tape and the square jumps gave the same results as machine 1.

  • The rule that wins: give the number a wheel it does not fit. A number n fits a ring of r cells when n × n leaves remainder 1 after division by r, and it fits a two-cell wheel with step c when c is a square in the arithmetic of remainders after division by n. Every mistake of Pascal's wheel that we found is a number that fits its ring: 42,120 on rings of 2 to 40 cells below 1010, not one that does not fit. For rings the rule is not ours: it is the exception that Popovych wrote into his version of Agrawal's conjecture in 2009. Here it is carried over to the wheels of two and three cells.
  • Checked as far as the lists go. A number that fools Pascal's wheel must fool the doubling wheel first (add up the cells: the sum doubles every turn). All 118,968,378 odd numbers below 264 that fool the doubling wheel are known, and we put every one on the smallest ring it does not fit and on its two-cell wheel: none came home. We also tried every one of them on each ring of up to 42 cells that it does not fit: none came home.
  • Is this the gear that amazes mathematicians? Not yet, and we say so plainly. The rows that say “none” without “proved” are open problems: the pair of wheels and machines 10, 11, 12 and 19. Nobody has proved that they never make a mistake. For the pair of wheels there is a prize of 620 dollars, offered by Pomerance, Selfridge and Wagstaff; for Pascal's wheel the claim is a sibling of Agrawal's conjecture (his has x − 1 where the wheel has x + 1; on a ring with an even number of cells the two are the same); the two-cell wheel is a Frobenius test, for which no mistake is known either (Khashin reports that his own version of it makes none below 264). There are reasons to expect a mistake at some very large number (Pomerance for the pair; Lenstra and Pomerance for Pascal's wheel). A proof for any of them, or the first number that fools one, would be news.
  • Second round: fold the wheel. A ring has symmetries: renumber the cells and it is the same ring. Multiply the wheel by its mirror images and the product lives on a few cells: 5 cells fold onto 2, 7 cells onto 3. A number that fools the big ring must fool the folded wheel; that is proved. Below 264 the numbers that fool the doubling wheel were put on folded wheels 612,337,763 times (rings of 5, 7, 11, 13, 29, 31, 37, 41 cells): 1 passed, on a fold onto 2 cells that it fits one level down, and the full ring turned it away. So a large ring can be checked by turning a small wheel.
  • Pascal's wheel on 5 cells contains the Fibonacci wheel. Folded onto 2 cells it turns by the square of the golden ratio. So every mistake of the 5 cells is a mistake of machine 6 and of machine 7 too (all 628 below 1010 are), and a number that does not fit 5 cells and still came home would win the 620 dollars.
  • The rule holds one level down. Folded wheels alone made 518 mistakes below 107, every one by a number that moves the cells round with order 2: it fits. Where a number must move three cells round, nothing came home. That is machine 12, the three-cell wheel.
  • On Mersenne numbers the two-cell wheel is proved. For 2p − 1 its step is always 3, and home is exactly the Lucas–Lehmer test.
  • Proved or fast, and both only for special numbers. Wilson's wheel (machine 13) never errs and that is proved, but it needs n − 1 turns and none can be skipped. Lucas's wheel (machine 14) is proved and fast, but only when the primes that divide n − 1 are known; that is how numbers such as 3 × 22208 + 1 are proved. Machine 15 does the same with n + 1 and proves every Mersenne prime: type 2^4423-1 there. The proved ring test of Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena needs more than 19,562,929 cells for 24423 − 1, where machine 10 uses 5. In one timing run on that number Lucas–Lehmer took 0.02 seconds, the two-cell wheel 0.06 and Pascal's wheel 0.33. For every number there is a method that is proved and works in practice, but it is not one wheel: a curve gives wheels whose size can be chosen, and one searches for a wheel whose size comes apart. Machine 16 is a slow version of it and machine 17 a fast one; the largest such proof we found reported has 109,297 digits. One small wheel with few turns, for every number and with a proof, is what is missing.
  • Third round: a chain of wheels has all three. A number n has more wheels than one: one cell with n − 1 positions, two cells with a round trip of n + 1 turns, and curve wheels. For 97 families of curves the number of positions follows from n at once, without counting. Machine 17 takes a size that comes apart, checks that wheel, and goes on with the large prime part: a chain of wheels down to a small number. The page's code proved all 160 random primes of 20 to 150 digits that we gave it, in about 0.9 seconds for 100 digits, and a separate program accepted every chain. With n − 1 and n + 1 alone only 8 of 30 primes of 60 digits were proved, and with 9 families of curves 18 of 20 primes of 100 digits. It built no chain for any of the 438 numbers that are not prime and pass the doubling wheel which we tried. This is not our idea: it is elliptic curve primality proving (Atkin and Morain), cut down to fit a page. And it is a search, with no proof that it always ends soon.
  • Fourth round: a test with a receipt. A different way to win on the largest numbers: not a faster test, but a test that need not be done twice. Machine 18 runs the Lucas–Lehmer test in its two-cell form and writes down a few numbers, the receipt. With it a checker confirms the result by turning only a small piece of the strip of turns. In our runs the check was 6 times faster than the test at p = 9,689 and 45 times at p = 110,503 (table below). Every wrong result (51 of 51), every changed number in a receipt (12 of 12) and every bit flipped during a test (100 of 100) was rejected.
  • A receipt is not a proof of the result. We tried to cheat. A tester who has made the turns of a prime 2p − 1 can multiply the true result by a number that returns to 1 after a few multiplications, and the check still passes. On the one-cell form of the test the factor −1 was accepted in 360 of 360 attempts; on two cells a factor of that kind was accepted in 185 of 360. Each time the verdict changed from prime to not prime. So a receipt shows that the work was done, not that the reported result is the true one. This weakness is known (Hoffmann, Hubáček, Kamath and Pietrzak, 2023, who also repair it). A simple guard worked in our runs: compare a power of the result that wipes out such factors. All 24 forged results were then sent to a full test, and none of 288 numbers that are not prime was sent without need.
  • Fifth round: two cells, alone, settled below 264. Pascal's wheel is settled alone below 264, because the sum of its cells is the doubling wheel; but it needs 5 to 47 cells. Machine 11 has two cells, but was checked up to 264 only with the doubling wheel at its side. Machine 19 has both. It changes the start of the two cells to a and 1, with a × a two more or two less than the step. Then the two cells, multiplied into one number, give 2 or −2, and that number is doubled at every turn. So whatever fools this wheel fools the doubling wheel too (proved). All 118,968,378 numbers below 264 that fool the doubling wheel were put on it: none came home. As a check without the list, every odd number below 1010 went on the wheel: 455,052,510 came home, and a sieve counts 455,052,510 odd primes there. On primes of 19 digits it took 1758 billionths of a second, against 498 for the doubling wheel alone and 3470 for the seven tests that are known to settle every number below 264. Khashin reported in 2018 that his version of the two-cell test makes no mistake below 264, by a long computation; the short reason here is the 2. Above 264 nothing is proved.
  • What they can do today. Both wheels need a few cells and about as many steps as the number has binary digits, so they also run on numbers of a thousand digits: choose machine 10 or 11 and type one.
