Prime gears

The numbers from 1 up as gears, as many as you set (up to 5,000). Gear k has k teeth, and one tick moves every gear one tooth, so gear k is back home every k ticks. At tick t the gears at home are exactly the divisors of t, and t is prime when only gear 1 and gear t are home. Press Start and watch. Which machine has the best results: the table at the bottom. Machines 10 and 11 let one wheel decide whether a number is prime, and neither has made a mistake in any test: the contest.

How many: 1 to 30
Speed: 1 ticks per second

Which machine has the best results

Every machine was run for 100,000 ticks with 250 wheels. The table gives the most prime wheels, Mersenne-exponent wheels and twin-prime wheels that were home at once, and the tick. Among the numbers 1 to 250 there are 53 primes, 12 Mersenne exponents and 33 twin primes.

  • Pascal's wheel and the two-cell wheel win without a trick. Each of their lamps is a decision: wheel n goes home at tick n only when n is prime, and stays there. At tick 241 all 53 prime lamps are on, and no other lamp.
  • Back and forth also has them all, for a simple reason. At tick 36 its wheels are exactly back where they started, so every wheel is home, prime or not.
  • Fibonacci and doubling come next. On these a wheel that is home at tick a is home at every multiple of a, so at a tick with many divisors (27,720, 55,440, 83,160) half of the prime wheels are home together.
  • On the plain wheels at most six primes are home at once. The tick must be a multiple of every wheel that is home, and 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 = 30,030 is the last such product below 100,000.
  • Two machines were removed. The tape and the square jumps gave the same results as machine 1.

One wheel decides: which wheel can be trusted

A harder contest. Wheel n must be home after n turns exactly when n is prime. On every machine below each prime comes home; that is proved. The mistakes are the numbers that are not prime and come home too. All machines were run against the odd numbers that are not prime.

  • The rule that wins: give the number a wheel it does not fit. A number n fits a ring of r cells when n × n leaves remainder 1 after division by r, and it fits a two-cell wheel with step c when c is a square in the arithmetic of remainders after division by n. Every mistake of Pascal's wheel that we found is a number that fits its ring: 42,120 on rings of 2 to 40 cells below 1010, not one that does not fit.
  • Checked as far as the lists go. A number that fools Pascal's wheel must fool the doubling wheel first (add up the cells: the sum doubles every turn). All 118,968,378 odd numbers below 264 that fool the doubling wheel are known, and we put every one on the smallest ring it does not fit and on its two-cell wheel: none came home. We also tried every one of them on each ring of 5, 7, 9, 11 and 16 cells that it does not fit: none came home.
  • Is this the gear that amazes mathematicians? Not yet, and we say so plainly. The last three rows are open problems. Nobody has proved that they never make a mistake. For the pair of wheels there is a prize of 620 dollars, offered by Pomerance, Selfridge and Wagstaff; for Pascal's wheel the claim is Agrawal's conjecture; the two-cell wheel is a Frobenius test, for which no mistake is known either. There are reasons to expect a mistake at some very large number (Pomerance for the pair; Lenstra and Pomerance for Pascal's wheel). A proof for any of the three, or the first number that fools one, would be news.
  • What they can do today. Both wheels need a few cells and about as many steps as the number has binary digits, so they also run on numbers of a thousand digits: choose machine 10 or 11 and type one.
