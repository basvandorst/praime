Prime census

Hold a census of the numbers 1, 2, 3, … and ask each one: how many different primes divide you? 12 = 2 · 2 · 3 has two, 30 = 2 · 3 · 5 has three. Erdős and Kac proved in 1940 that the answers follow a bell curve, centred at ln ln x with spread √(ln ln x). That is a theorem about the limit. Here we watch real numbers, and see how far from the limit they still are. Dance: prime13, rain: prime12, Galton board: prime7.

Numbers per second: 1,000

Which prediction fits, measured up to 5,000, 1,000,000 and 10,000,000

Distance: the share of numbers that a prediction puts in the wrong bar (total variation; 0 is perfect). Erdős–Kac uses a bell with mean and variance ln ln x. Sathe and Selberg (1954) give a finer formula, (x / ln x) (ln ln x)k−1 / (k − 1)! · G((k − 1) / ln ln x). The third prediction is a bell with the mean of the census (exactly Σ ⌊x/p⌋ / x) and the variance Σ (1/p)(1 − 1/p) minus the sum of 1/(pq) over pairs of primes p ≠ q with pq > x: two such primes cannot both divide a number up to x. That subtracted sum is 1.29 at 5,000, 1.43 at 1,000,000 and 1.47 at 10,000,000, on its way to π²/6 = 1.645, while ln ln x itself is only 2.1 to 2.8. So the bell of Erdős and Kac is far too wide at every size a computer can count, and it will stay too wide for a long time, because ln ln x grows so slowly. The true spread was derived here as a heuristic and checked against the census; the page and a separate program agree on every bar, mean, variance and distance, up to 5,000, 1,000,000 and 10,000,000.
