Prime dance

The dance floor has a spot for every last digit a prime can have: 1, 3, 7 and 9. A dancer steps to the spot of each prime in turn: 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23 … is the dance 7 → 1 → 3 → 7 → 9 → 3. If the digits were independent, every step would be equally common, and a repeat (the same spot twice) would happen a quarter of the time. The primes repeat far less (Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan, 2016). Fake primes show where that comes from. Census: prime14, rain: prime12, radio: prime11, race: prime6, balance: prime5.

Where the dislike of repeats comes from, measured up to 5,000 and up to 1,000,000

Last digits, primes above 100. A repeat needs a gap that is a multiple of 10, and gaps are mostly short, so repeats are rare for any sequence that avoids the even numbers and the multiples of 5. Fake primes that avoid only 2 and 5 already show four fifths of the deficit. Fakes that also avoid 3 come close at 1,000,000 but not at 10,000,000; fakes that also avoid 7 and more primes match at 10,000,000 but repeat a little too little at 1,000,000. So the wheel that fits moves with the size: the primes' dislike of repeats fades more slowly than in any random model with a fixed wheel. Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan explained the bias with a modified Hardy–Littlewood conjecture; Holt (2024) traces it to how gaps evolve in the sieve of Eratosthenes.
