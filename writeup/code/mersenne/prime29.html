Rabbit clocks

Fibonacci's rabbits: 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, … each number the sum of the two before. Count them on a clock with p hours (only the remainder after division by p). For every prime p the hand comes back to 0 again and again, every z(p) generations: then p divides a Fibonacci number. The cousins of Fibonacci's numbers, the Lucas numbers 2, 1, 3, 4, 7, 11, 18, …, reach 0 on the clock of an odd prime exactly when z(p) is even (and 2 divides L3 = 4), and Lagarias proved that two thirds of all primes divide some Lucas number. Möbius walker: prime30, Brun's ladder: prime28, carousel (the same question with powers): prime15, overview: all pages.

Two thirds, counted up to 1,000,000

For p other than 2 and 5, z(p) divides p − 1 when p ends in 1 or 9 and p + 1 when it ends in 3 or 7. An odd prime divides some Lucas number exactly when z(p) is even (because Ln = F2n / Fn); 2 divides L3 = 4. Lagarias (1985) proved that these primes have density 2/3. Counted: 0.6667 of the primes up to 1,000, 0.6688 up to 10,000, 0.6655 up to 100,000 and 0.6668 up to 1,000,000. The share of primes (other than 2 and 5) with the longest possible clock, z(p) = p ∓ 1, is 0.3745 up to 1,000,000, close to Artin's constant 0.3740 of the carousel. That is no coincidence: z(p) is the order of a fixed number in a cyclic group of p ∓ 1 elements, and under the generalised Riemann hypothesis a Hooley-type argument gives exactly Artin's constant (0.1771 for z = p − 1, 0.1968 for z = p + 1; measured 0.1776 and 0.1969). The page and a separate program agree on these counts up to 100,000 and 1,000,000.
