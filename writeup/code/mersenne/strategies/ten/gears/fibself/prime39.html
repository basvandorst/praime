Fibonacci fools itself

The Fibonacci wheel of the prime gears page is a quick test for primes. Let ε = +1 when n leaves 1 or 4 after division by 5, and ε = −1 when it leaves 2 or 3. Every prime n other than 2 and 5 divides the Fibonacci number F(n − ε): turn the two cells of the wheel n − ε times and they come home. A number that is not prime and still comes home is a Fibonacci pseudoprime, a mistake of the wheel. The first is 323 = 17 · 19. This page counts the mistakes and shows a family hiding among them: the Fibonacci numbers themselves.

1. Put a number on the wheel

The page computes F(n − ε) modulo n by doubling (about two multiplications per binary digit of n), and decides whether n is prime with the strong test to the first twelve prime bases, which is exact below 3.3·1024; above that a "prime" verdict means probable prime, while "not prime" is always certain.

4. Why: on a Fibonacci number the wheel tests the index

Two facts about Fibonacci numbers do all the work.

(a)  F(a) divides F(b) exactly when a divides b          (for a ≥ 3)
(b)  for a prime p ≠ 5:  F(p) ≡ ε(p)  (mod p)            (Lucas)

Put n = F(k) on the wheel. By (a), n divides F(n − ε) exactly when k divides F(k) − ε. The wheel no longer looks at the big number n at all; it looks at the small number k. Now take k = p prime. Modulo 5, x² − x − 1 = (x − 3)², so F(m) ≡ m · 3m−1 (mod 5), which gives ε(F(p)) = ε(p). With (b), p divides F(p) − ε. So every F(p) that is not prime fools the wheel: F(19) = 37 · 113, F(31) = 557 · 2417, F(37) = 73 · 149 · 2221, and so on. For k = 2p the same works with F(2p) = F(p) · L(p) and L(p) ≡ 1 (mod p).

The recursion goes further. If k is itself a mistake of the right kind, F(k) is one too: 4181 = F(19) fools the wheel, and so does F(4181), a number of 874 digits (try it above). The indices k up to 100,000 whose F(k) fools the wheel are

None of this is new. Duparc (1955) and Emma Lehmer (1964) proved that F(2p) fools the wheel for every prime p > 5, which gives infinitely many mistakes. Parberry (1970, Theorem 2) proved the equivalence above for k prime to 30, and that composite F(p) generate further mistakes recursively; Bruckman (1994) showed that a composite F(p) fools the Lucas test as well. The mistakes are OEIS A081264; the extra indices are terms of A279072. Our part is the counting and the pictures. Whether infinitely many F(p) are composite is still unknown.
