Prime star clusters

Look at the numbers as a night sky in which the primes are stars. Apart from 2 and 3, primes can never be closer than 2, and three primes in a row can never fit in fewer than 6 numbers (one of p, p + 2, p + 4 is always a multiple of 3). The tightest clusters are twins (p, p + 2), triplets (p, p + 2, p + 6) and their mirror (p, p + 4, p + 6), quadruplets (p, p + 2, p + 6, p + 8), quintuplets and sextuplets. Hardy and Littlewood (1923) predicted how many of each there are. Orchard: prime25, race: prime6, overview: all pages.

Counted against Hardy and Littlewood

Up to 109

Counted by a separate program up to 109: every cluster except the sextuplet lies within 0.8 % of the prediction (twins 0.9998, triplets 0.9992 and 0.9999, quadruplets 0.9997, quintuplets 1.0049 and 0.9925). The sextuplets reach only 0.851, largely because 1/ln⁶ t is huge just above t = 2: started at 1,000 instead of 2, the integral predicts 319 sextuplets against the 317 found. The two triplet shapes race fairly: at 109 the shape (0, 2, 6) trails by 240 with about 379,500 of each, well inside the expected spread of the difference (about 870), after 449 changes of the lead. The page and the program agree on all seven counts up to 10,000,000.
