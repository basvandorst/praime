Wieferich's needle

Fermat's little theorem says that p divides 2p−1 − 1 for every odd prime p. Divide once and take the remainder of the quotient on division by p again: q(p) = (2p−1 − 1)/p mod p, a number from 0 to p − 1. Draw it as a needle on a dial at the angle q(p)/p of a full turn. For 1093 and 3511 the needle points straight up: p² divides 2p−1 − 1, and these are the only Wieferich primes known. If the needles point anywhere with equal chance, about Σp≤x 1/p of the needles of the primes up to x should point straight up, a sum that grows like ln ln x: forever, but extremely slowly. Jumping champion: prime34, two-square tiles: prime32, impostors (the other side of Fermat's theorem): prime24, overview: all pages.

All needles up to ten million

A separate program computed q(p) for all 664,578 odd primes below 107. In ten sectors of the dial the needles number 66,303 to 66,699, against 66,458 each for an even spread; the chi-square value is 2.42 on 9 degrees of freedom. That is unusually even (random needles give less about 2 % of the time), but we looked at six such values (10 and 20 sectors at three sizes), and the others are ordinary (for example 7.6 and 7.7 at 106 and 3 · 106), so one value in the 2 % tail is no evidence of a structure. Nothing is proved here: that the quotients of the base 2 spread evenly over the primes is a heuristic. The needles within a thousandth of a turn of straight up number 1,334 (including the two Wieferich primes), against 1,329 ± 36 for an even spread. The closest misses below 107 are 1,897,121 (q = −1), 2,152,849 (q = 5) and 1,437,049 (q = −5). The even spread predicts about Σ 1/p = 2.54 Wieferich primes below 107 (two found), and only about 0.8 more between 107 and 6.7 · 1015, where Dorais and Klyve (2011) searched and found none; later distributed searches report a far higher bound, which we have not checked. The page and the program agree on the needles of all primes up to 106.
