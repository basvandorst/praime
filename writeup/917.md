# Puzzle 917: Many primes P with the same prime Q = P + A·B

> **In short:** For a prime P with 2d digits and halves A, B, set Q = P + A·B, halved when even; the record was five primes with the same prime Q. (1) Q + 10^d = (A+1)(B+10^d) (or 2Q + 10^d when halved), so the preimages of Q are the divisors of Q + 10^d with cofactor in [10^d, 2·10^d) that give a prime P. (2) A smooth, odd, 5-free N₁ with many divisors and N₁ − 10^d prime therefore has many preimages: 86 primes of 18 digits for Q = 863105979646156247. (3) Exhaustive sieves give the maxima 4, 5, 10 at 6, 8, 10 digits.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_917.htm)

**Status:** beaten · proven primes (all below 2⁶⁴); the 6-, 8- and 10-digit maxima exhaustive

## The question

P is a prime with an even number of digits, A and B are its two halves, Q = P + A·B, and if Q is even it is replaced by Q/2. Vic Bold asks for pairs of primes with the same prime Q, for example 23 and 37 (Q = 29). Carlos Rivera found triples and quadruples, (102367, 237179, 261071) → 139801 and (197677, 233419, 257287, 285161) → 165523, and asks for k-tuples with k > 4. Flavio Torasso found three 5-tuples of 8-digit primes: (26083999, 37379539, 49134863, 69410521, 70130413) → 36513391 and two more. His first one uses 69410521 with B = 0521, so a half with a leading zero is allowed on the page.

## How we found it

Two angles. The direct one: sieve all primes with 2d digits, compute Q, and count how often each Q occurs, in passes over the Q range with one byte per odd Q ([`code/p917_search.c`](code/p917_search.c)). That is exhaustive and gives the maximum k for a given length, but stops at 10 digits (4·10⁸ primes, ten passes).

The structural one: solving Q = P + A·B for B gives (A+1)(B+10^d) = Q + 10^d, so P maps to Q exactly when A+1 divides Q + 10^d with a cofactor in [10^d, 2·10^d). A Q with many preimages is therefore a prime such that Q + 10^d has many divisors just above 10^d. Choose N₁ odd, not divisible by 5, built from small primes with a large divisor count; require Q = N₁ − 10^d prime; then list the divisors f of N₁ in the window and test P = (N₁/f − 1)·10^d + (f − 10^d) for primality ([`code/p917_construct.py`](code/p917_construct.py)). This reaches 18-digit primes in minutes.

## The math

**Step 1 (proved). The identity.** P = A·10^d + B, so P + A·B + 10^d = A·10^d + B + A·B + 10^d = (A+1)(B+10^d). If P + A·B is odd, Q = P + A·B and Q + 10^d = (A+1)(B+10^d). If it is even, Q = (P + A·B)/2 and 2Q + 10^d = (A+1)(B+10^d). Conversely, given N ∈ {Q + 10^d, 2Q + 10^d} and a factorisation N = e·f with 10^d ≤ f < 2·10^d and 10^(d−1) + 1 ≤ e ≤ 10^d, the number P = (e−1)·10^d + (f − 10^d) has 2d digits, A = e − 1, B = f − 10^d, and P + A·B = N − 10^d, which is Q or 2Q; so P maps to Q as soon as P is prime. The parity takes care of itself: N = Q + 10^d is odd for odd Q, so e and f are odd, A even, B odd and P + A·B odd; for N = 2Q + 10^d, P odd forces e even and f odd, so A·B is odd and P + A·B even. Hence

  k(Q) = #{(N, e, f) : N ∈ {Q + 10^d, 2Q + 10^d}, N = e·f, 10^d ≤ f < 2·10^d, 10^(d−1) < e ≤ 10^d, (e−1)·10^d + f − 10^d prime}.

**Step 2 (heuristic). Why large k exists.** For a random prime Q the expected k is Σ_e P(e | N)·P(P prime): the divisors e of N = Q + 10^d in the dyadic window contribute Σ 1/e ≈ ln 2 and P is coprime to 10, so the odd branch gives about ln 2·(10/4)/ln(10^{2d}) ≈ 0.09 at 8 digits and 0.075 at 10 digits, and the even branch (e even, f odd) a few hundredths more; the 10-digit histogram (152461 prime Q with 3 preimages, 18732 with 4, 3008 with 5, 544 with 6, 117 with 7, 17 with 8, 6 with 9, 1 with 10) has a heavier tail than a single Poisson law, because the rate depends on the divisor structure of Q + 10^d. Choosing N₁ = Q + 10^d with d(N₁) ≈ 2000 puts about 100 divisors in the window [10^d, 2·10^d) (divisors of a smooth number spread roughly log-uniformly, with the window at the square root the densest), and each gives a prime P with probability about 2.5/ln(10^{2d}) ≈ 0.08 at 14 digits, hence k around 8 for a typical such Q and much more for the luckiest among the ten thousand candidates. N₁ must be odd (Q odd) and prime to 5 (otherwise 5 | Q), which costs the primes 2 and 5 in the smooth part.

**Step 3 (computed). Exhaustive maxima.** The sieve over all primes with 6, 8 and 10 digits (68906, 5096876 and 404204977 primes; the counts agree with π(10^n)) gives the complete list of prime Q with large k: at 6 digits the maximum is k = 4 (Rivera's 165523, the only one); at 8 digits k = 5, attained by 17 values of Q (Torasso's three among them, [`data/p917_8digit.txt`](data/p917_8digit.txt)); at 10 digits k = 10, attained only by Q = 4898171713 with P = 5167789567, 5416180873, 5817368399, 6211757707, 6619747987, 7186136323, 7592129033, 9088107793, 9239306029, 9761300359 (the last three with B < 10⁴); six Q have k = 9 ([`data/p917_10digit.txt`](data/p917_10digit.txt) lists all 685 prime Q with k ≥ 6).

**Step 4 (computed). The construction.** Candidates N₁ are the odd 5-free numbers in [10^(2d−1) + 10^d, 10^(2d) + 10^d) composed of primes ≤ 61 with d(N₁) ≥ T (T = 500, 1000, 2500, 12000 for d = 6, 7, 8, 9, giving 102581, 512312, 944076 and 11945 candidates; a depth-first search with the bound d(N) ≤ d(current)·2^{log(remaining budget)/log(next prime)}). For each N₁ with N₁ − 10^d prime, both N = N₁ and N = 2Q + 10^d are factored and the preimages counted. Best results, all P and Q proven prime (below 2⁶⁴):
