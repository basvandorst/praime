# Puzzle 285: K(2ⁿ − 1) mod n

> **In short:** Kempner's function K(2ⁿ − 1) mod n is 1 for most n and n/2 + 1 for n = 28, 52, 68, 92, …; the page found no law beyond p mod 4. (1) K(2ⁿ − 1) is the largest prime factor p of 2ⁿ − 1 for every n checked. (2) With d = ord_p(2) and t = n/d, the residue is 1 + d·((p − 1)/d mod t). (3) For n ≡ 4 (mod 8) this gives n/2 + 1 exactly when p ≡ 3 (mod 4), which proves the observed rule. Checked for all n ≤ 200 and the multiples of 8 up to 400.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_285.htm)

**Status:** answered (Q2: the law is p ≡ 1 mod ord_p(2) with the quadratic character of 2 deciding the parity; the page's rule for n ≡ 4 mod 8 proved for t ≤ 4) · table extended to n = 200 and multiples of 8 to 400 (computed, PARI factorizations)

## The question

K(N) (Kempner, also Smarandache function) is the least m with N | m!. Martín Ruiz tabulated K(2ⁿ − 1) mod n for 2 ≤ n ≤ 97 and found the value 1 except at n = 28, 52, 68, 92, where it is n/2 + 1. Q1: extend the table. Q2: the general law.

The page: Firoozbakht extended to n < 254 (exceptions 28, 52, 68, 92, 124, 156, 172, 244, all ≡ 4 mod 8, value n/2 + 1), proved residue 1 when n or n/2 is prime, and thought there is no general law. Pebody noted that a primitive prime factor p of 2ⁿ − 1 is ≡ 1 (mod n). Rupinski observed empirically that for 4 | n the residue is 1 when p ≡ 1 (mod 4) and n/2 + 1 when p ≡ 3 (mod 4), and connected the exceptions with Aurifeuillian factors.

## The math

Throughout, p is the largest prime factor of 2ⁿ − 1, d = ord_p(2) its multiplicative order, t = n/d.

**Step 1 (checked): K(2ⁿ − 1) = p.** K is the maximum of K(qᵉ) over the prime powers qᵉ ∥ 2ⁿ − 1, and K(qᵉ) ≤ eq. The exponents are tiny (3ᵉ with e = 1 + v₃(n/2), 5² for 20 | n, 7² for 21 | n, 1093² only from n = 364 on), so the maximum is the largest prime, which occurs to the first power. The script computes K exactly from the prime powers and confirms K = p for every n in the table.

**Step 2 (proved): the residue.** p | 2ⁿ − 1 means d | n, and Fermat gives d | p − 1. Write p − 1 = d·k. Then p mod n = (1 + d·k) mod (d·t) = 1 + d·(k mod t). So the residue is 1 iff t | k. In particular t = 1 (p a primitive prime factor of 2ⁿ − 1) gives residue 1.

**Step 3 (proved): the parity of k.** 2 is a quadratic residue mod p iff p ≡ ±1 (mod 8) (second supplement). k = (p − 1)/d is even iff d | (p − 1)/2 iff 2^((p−1)/2) ≡ 1 (mod p) iff 2 is a quadratic residue. So k is odd iff p ≡ ±3 (mod 8). Also, if d is odd then 2d | p − 1 because p − 1 is even.

**Step 4 (proved): the small values of t.**

- t = 2: residue 1 if p ≡ ±1 (mod 8), n/2 + 1 if p ≡ ±3 (mod 8).
- t = 4, d odd: k is even by Step 3; residue 1 iff 4 | k iff p ≡ 1 (mod 4), else residue 1 + 2d = n/2 + 1.
- n ≡ 4 (mod 8), n = 4m with m odd: the orders that occur are d ∈ {m, 2m, 4m}. d = 4m gives residue 1 and p ≡ 1 (mod 4). d = 2m: p = 1 + 2mk, so p ≡ 3 (mod 4) iff k odd iff residue 2m + 1. d = m: the t = 4 case. In all three cases: **residue n/2 + 1 iff p ≡ 3 (mod 4)**, Rupinski's rule.
- n ≡ 2 (mod 4), n = 2m with m odd: d ∈ {m, 2m} gives 2m | p − 1 (Step 3 for d = m odd), residue 1. This contains Firoozbakht's cases n prime and n/2 prime.
- n ≡ 0 (mod 8): t = 1 gives 1; t = 2 means d = n/2 with 4 | d, so p ≡ 1 (mod 4), and the residue is n/2 + 1 iff p ≡ 5 (mod 8). Up to n = 400 the only t = 2 cases are n = 288 and 368, both with p ≡ 1 (mod 8), residue 1. So a residue n/2 + 1 with n ≡ 0 (mod 8) is allowed by the law but has not occurred.

Why the exceptional n are ≡ 4 (mod 8): for n = 4m with m odd, 2ⁿ − 1 = (2^m − 1)(2^m + 1)(2^{2m} + 1), and 2^{2m} + 1 splits further (Aurifeuillian factorization), so the largest prime factor often comes from 2^m ± 1 (t = 4 or 2) instead of from the primitive part (t = 1). In the table t = 4 occurs only for n ≡ 4 (mod 8), with p a Mersenne prime 2^m − 1 for n = 28, 52, 68, 124 (m = 7, 13, 17, 31) and p = (2^m + 1)/3 for n = 92, 156, 172. A Mersenne prime 2^m − 1 with m ≥ 3 is ≡ 7 (mod 8), hence ≡ 3 (mod 4), which gives the residue n/2 + 1 directly.

**Empirical (table n ≤ 200):** t = 1 for 165 of the 199 values of n; the others have t ∈ {2, 3, 4, 6}, and every residue is 1 or n/2 + 1.
