# Puzzle 362: A sequence of primes

> **In short:** The page lists nine primes (3, 5, 17, 257, 65537, 4294967311, …) and asks for the hidden property and the next three terms; no answer is on the page. The property: the primes are chosen greedily so that the product of (1 − 1/p) stays above 1/2 and tends to 1/2, i.e. the numbers divisible by none of them have density 1/2, like the odd numbers. (1) With r = ∏(1 − 1/a(i)) the next term is the least prime above r/(r − 1/2). (2) For the five Fermat primes this bound is exactly 2^(2^n), which is why the sequence starts as Fermat's. (3) The rule reproduces all nine terms and gives the next three: proved primes of 136, 270 and 536 digits. The sequence is OEIS A339344 (Eldar, 2020), which already holds these terms; the page has no answer.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_362.htm)

**Status:** answered · the property identified, the next three terms computed and proved prime (literature: OEIS A339344, 2020)

## How we found it

The first five terms are the Fermat primes, and their product is 2^32 − 1, with φ(2^32 − 1) = 2^31: the integers coprime to 3·5·17·257·65537 have density 2^31/(2^32 − 1), just above 1/2. The sixth term 4294967311 = 2^32 + 15 is the least prime above 2^32. That suggested "keep the density of the untouched integers above 1/2 with the smallest possible prime". A term lookup in the OEIS data (`references/oeis/lookup.py`) confirmed it: the nine terms are A339344, "lexicographically earliest sequence of odd primes such that the asymptotic density of the numbers divisible by at least one of them is 1/2" (A. Eldar, Nov 2020). Its b-file has a(1) … a(12). We recomputed all twelve terms from the rule, proved the three new ones prime with PARI, and compared with the b-file: equal.

## The math

Let r(n) = ∏_{i ≤ n} (1 − 1/a(i)). The density of the integers not divisible by any of a(1) … a(n) is r(n) (proved: inclusion–exclusion, the a(i) are distinct primes).

**The greedy rule.** a(1) = 3 and a(n+1) is the least prime q with r(n)(1 − 1/q) > 1/2, i.e.

```
q > r(n) / (r(n) − 1/2).
```

The product decreases and stays above 1/2, and its limit is exactly 1/2: with ε = r(n) − 1/2 the bound is B = r(n)/ε, Bertrand gives q < 2B, so r(n)/q > ε/2 and the new excess ε' = ε − r(n)/q is below ε/2. The excess at least halves at every step (proved).

**Why Fermat primes.** With a(1..k) = F_0 … F_{k−1} = 2^(2^j) + 1 (j < k), ∏ a(i) = 2^(2^k) − 1 and ∏ (a(i) − 1) = 2^(2^k − 1), so r = 2^(2^k − 1)/(2^(2^k) − 1) and

```
r − 1/2 = 1 / (2 (2^(2^k) − 1)),   r / (r − 1/2) = 2^(2^k).
```

So the bound is exactly 2^(2^k) and the next term is the least prime above it: 2^(2^k) + 1 when that is prime (k = 1 … 4), and 2^32 + 15 = 4294967311 at k = 5 since 2^32 + 1 = 641 · 6700417 (computed).

**Doubling of the digits.** Write r(n) = 1/2 + ε. The bound is ≈ 1/(2ε), the next prime is q = (1 + δ)/(2ε) with δ ≈ (ln q)/q the relative gap, and the new excess is ε' = ε − r/q ≈ ε(δ − 2ε) ≈ 2ε² ln q. So ε squares at every step and the terms, ≈ 1/(2ε), double in digits: 10, 19, 35, 69, 136, 270, 536 (computed; the exact excesses are in the output of the script).

## Validation

[code/p362_next_terms.py](code/p362_next_terms.py) computes a(1) … a(12) from the rule with exact fractions, checks that the prime below each term violates the rule, proves each term prime with PARI (`cypari2`, 14 s) and writes the data file. [code/p362_validate.py](code/p362_validate.py) is the independent check: plain integer arithmetic for the product, `sympy.isprime` for every term (a proof below 2^64, PRP above) and for every integer between the bound and the term, and the nine page terms compared with the file.
