# Puzzle 1267: How many emirps are there?

> **In short:** An emirp is a prime whose digit reversal is a different prime. The counts by number of digits were known to 12 digits; the page asked for a formula and compared with x/ln²x, guessing a limit like 1/(10 ln 10) for the share of emirps among primes. The model: (1) the reversal r of a prime p is prime with probability (33/8)/ln r, because r is automatically odd, prime to 3 (same digit sum), prime to 5 (its last digit is the first digit of p, which must be 1, 3, 7 or 9) and prime to 11 (alternating digit sum up to sign); (2) summing over the k-digit primes with first digit 1, 3, 7 or 9 gives the count of k-digit emirps within 0.3 % for k = 8 to 12; (3) asymptotically emirps(x) ~ (11/6)·x/ln²x, and the share of emirps among primes is ~ (11/6)/ln x, which tends to 0, not to a positive limit. Infinitely many emirps is open: it would follow from the model, but no proof is known.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1267.htm)

**Status:** answered (Q2: a formula that matches the counts to 0.3 % from 8 digits on; Q3: the heuristic, with the obstacle to a proof named) · counts to 8 digits recomputed

## The question

Q1: count the emirps up to the largest possible n. Q2: find a formula matching the data, compare with n/(ln n)², and say what the digit constraints contribute. Q3: a provable conjecture or a proof that there are infinitely many emirps.

The page has the counts of emirps with k digits for k ≤ 12 (OEIS A152014, quoted by Vantieghem), the ratio emirps/primes decreasing from 0.32 to 0.061, and the guess that the ratio tends to 1/(10 ln 10) ≈ 0.043. Rivera compared the cumulative counts with x/ln²x at x = 10^(k+1) and found ratios near 2.1.

## The math

**Step 1 (proved): what the reversal inherits.** Let p be a prime with k ≥ 2 digits and r its reversal. The digit sum is the same, so r ≡ p (mod 3) and (mod 9): 3 ∤ r. The alternating digit sum of r is ± that of p, so r ≡ ±p (mod 11): 11 ∤ r. The last digit of r is the first digit of p; r is prime only if that digit is 1, 3, 7 or 9, which excludes 5/9 of the primes (the first digits of primes with k digits are nearly uniform, with a slight preference for small digits that the logarithmic integral accounts for). No other small modulus is preserved by reversal.

**Step 2 (heuristic): the model.** Treat r as a random odd number of its size that is prime to 3, 5 and 11. Its chance to be prime is then (1/ln r)·2·(3/2)·(5/4)·(11/10) = (33/8)/ln r. Hence

```
E(k) = Σ_{p prime, k digits, first digit ∈ {1,3,7,9}, p ≠ r} (33/8) / ln r.
```

**Step 3 (computed): the model against the counts.** The exact sum E(k) for k ≤ 8 (sieve to 10⁸) and its closed form with li for k ≤ 12:

| k | emirps | model (exact sum) | closed form | count / closed form |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 204 | 235 | 238 | 0.856 |
| 6 | 9538 | 9843 | 9841 | 0.969 |
| 8 | 535578 | 534982 | 534777 | 1.0015 |
| 10 | 33619380 | — | 33548912 | 1.0021 |
| 12 | 2294771254 | — | 2299612641 | 0.9979 |

The closed form replaces the sum over primes with first digit d by li((d+1)·10^(k−1)) − li(d·10^(k−1)) and 1/ln r by its average over the four possible first digits d' of r. For small k the model overshoots because 1/ln r is not small and the independence assumption is crude; from k = 7 on the agreement is within 0.6 %, from k = 8 within 0.3 %.

**Step 4 (heuristic): asymptotics.** With the primes of size x having first digit in {1, 3, 7, 9} with frequency → 4/9 and 1/ln r ≈ 1/ln x, the number of emirps up to x is ~ (33/8)(4/9)·x/ln²x = (11/6)·x/ln²x ≈ 1.833·x/ln²x. The share of emirps among the primes is ~ (11/6)/ln x: 0.066 at 10¹² (page: 0.0668 measured, with the share still falling). So the share tends to 0, and the page's guess of a positive limit is not supported. Rivera's ratio 2.1 comes from using x = 10^(k+1) in x/ln²x; with x = 10^k the cumulative count 2608090124 to 10¹² is 1.086 times (11/6)·x/ln²x, the usual slow convergence of x/ln²x.

**Q3 (proved: the obstacle).** Infinitely many emirps would follow if the reversal of a prime were "random" as in Step 2. Nothing of that kind is provable today: no one can show that infinitely many primes have a prime reversal, since even the existence of infinitely many primes with a prescribed value of one digit in each half of the number is only known for special patterns (Maynard's primes with restricted digits give primes avoiding a digit, not primes whose reversal is prime). A plausible, still unproved, statement that would do: the primes p with first digit in {1, 3, 7, 9} and prime reversal have counting function ~ (11/6)x/ln²x.
