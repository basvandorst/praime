# Puzzle 823: Strings of the digits 1, 3, 7, 9 with the most prime substrings

> **In short:** For a string S of L digits from {1, 3, 7, 9}, K(S) counts the distinct primes among its substrings read left to right; the records are 74 (L = 20), 183 (L = 40) and 431 (L = 80), the last two from a 2017 search, and exact maxima are known only to L = 14. (1) A random string has about 1.09·Σ (L−ℓ+1)/ℓ ≈ 55, 160, 350 prime substrings for L = 20, 40, 80, so the records exceed random strings by 35 %, 15 % and 23 %. (2) Simulated annealing with a memo of substring primality ties the L = 20 record with a second string, 93719333139979337397, reaches 162 at L = 40 from random starts and does not improve 183 or 431 when started from them. (3) The L = 20 maximum stays open; an exact answer needs a search over 4²⁰ strings with pruning, which the page estimates at a year.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_823.htm)

**Status:** open · attempted (records not beaten; a second string with 74 for L = 20; all strings on the page re-evaluated)

## The question

Carlos Rivera asks for strings S of L digits from 1, 3, 7, 9 embedding a maximal number K of primes, read left to right only; his 19717393139337391999 gives 68 for L = 20. Emmanuel Vantieghem found 73 (L = 20) and 156 (L = 40), Dmitry Kamenetsky 74, 166 and 354 (L = 20, 40, 80), T. D. Noe solved L ≤ 14 exactly (the 14-digit case took 15 hours; 20 digits "about a year"), and Michael Hürter holds 183 (L = 40) and 431 (L = 80).

## How we found it

K(S) is the size of the set of prime substrings (equal substrings give the same prime; no leading zeros occur). A simulated annealing over single-digit changes, swaps and double changes, with a memo of substring primality (deterministic Miller–Rabin below 19 digits, GMP's probable-prime test above) and full re-evaluation of the 210, 820 or 3240 substrings per step, runs at a few thousand steps per second for L = 20 ([`code/p823_anneal.cpp`](code/p823_anneal.cpp), Python version [`code/p823_anneal.py`](code/p823_anneal.py)). Three random starts with 3·10⁶ steps for L = 20 reached 71, 72 and 74; a random start with 1.5·10⁶ steps for L = 40 reached 162; starts from Hürter's strings found no better neighbour in 1.5·10⁶ (L = 40) and 4·10⁵ (L = 80) steps.

## The math

**Step 1 (heuristic). The random baseline.** A substring of length ℓ ends in 1, 3, 7 or 9, so it is prime with probability about (10/4)/ln 10^ℓ = 1.09/ℓ; summing over the L − ℓ + 1 substrings of each length gives E[K] ≈ 1.09·Σ_{ℓ=1}^{L} (L − ℓ + 1)/ℓ = 1.09·((L+1)·H_L − L): 55 for L = 20, 160 for L = 40 and 350 for L = 80 (distinct primes slightly fewer). The records 74, 183 and 431 are 35 %, 15 % and 23 % above the mean; the L = 40 record looks the least optimised and is the natural target, but 1.5·10⁶ annealing steps from it found nothing better.

**Step 2 (computed). Our strings.** 93719333139979337397 has K = 74, like Kamenetsky's 39311171399719337397 (both end in 97337397 … the strings share the suffix 9337397). 9917331113193937393199733313939191719333 has K = 162 (L = 40).

**Step 3 (computed). Re-evaluation.** All strings on the page give the claimed values when re-evaluated independently (sympy and PARI).
