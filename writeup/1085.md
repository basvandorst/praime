# Puzzles 1079 and 1085: Forest of prime numbers (prime stepping stones)

> **In short:** On an infinite board a 1 and n stones of value 2 are placed. Then the primes 3, 5, 7, … are placed in order, each on an empty cell whose already placed king-neighbours add up to it. The goal is to get as far as possible. The page lists the best known last primes 13, 23, 31, 41, 53, 61, 73, 89, 97 for n = 1 … 9. It asks whether they are optimal (puzzle 1085), and in particular whether 13, 23, 31 are best for n = 1, 2, 3 (puzzle 1079, Q1). Optimality was claimed without published proof. (1) The stones can be placed lazily: a stone goes down when the first prime next to it is placed, and it may touch no earlier prime, because it would have changed that prime's sum. This makes the game a finite tree for each n. (2) An exhaustive depth-first search of this tree, with the first prime placed east or south-east of the 1 (symmetry), gives the maxima 13, 23, 31, 41, 53, 61, 73, 89 for n = 1 … 8. (3) A second search, written independently, agrees for n ≤ 5, and every configuration is replayed against the rules. So the table is optimal for n ≤ 8; n = 9 (97) and n = 10 (103) were not checked.

**Puzzles:** [1079](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1079.htm), [1085](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1085.htm)

**Status:** answered (computed) · the values for n = 1 … 8 are maxima (exhaustive search; a second search agrees for n ≤ 5); n = 9, 10 not checked

## The question

A 1 and n green stones worth 2 lie on an infinite square board. The primes 3, 5, 7, … are placed one at a time, in order, each equal to the sum of its already placed neighbours horizontally, vertically and diagonally. The score is the last prime placed before the next one cannot be placed. Puzzle 1079 (Kurchan) gives 13, 23, 31 for n = 1, 2, 3 and asks (Q1) whether these are best, and (Q2) for solutions with n > 3. Puzzle 1085 lists the best values for n = 2 … 9 and asks whether they are all optimal:

| n | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| best known | 23 | 31 | 41 | 53 | 61 | 73 | 89 | 97 |

The values for n = 4 and 9 are Kamenetsky's, the others Vecchi's (who reports n = 10: 103). Kamenetsky "believes" the first three are optimal "but cannot prove it". Vecchi "claims" optimality of his values, and the page shows no proof.

## The math

**Step 1 (proved): lazy stones.** In the original game the stones lie on the board from the start. A stone that touches no prime cell changes nothing, so it can be ignored. Every other stone is first needed when the first prime next to it is placed. So place each stone at that moment, on an empty cell next to the new prime. It must not touch an earlier prime cell, since in the original game it would have counted in that prime's sum. (Touching the 1 or another stone is harmless; those are not sums.) The two descriptions give the same games.

**Step 2 (proved): the moves.** Prime q can go on an empty cell c with an odd neighbour (q is odd and the stones are even). The placed neighbours of c sum to S₀ ≤ q, with q − S₀ = 2k, and the k new stones are placed on k of the admissible neighbours of c. If no cell admits a move, the game ends with the previous prime. For each n this is a finite tree, because each prime uses up at least one cell next to the bounded configuration.

**Step 3 (computed): the search.** Depth-first search over the whole tree ([code/p1079_stones.c](code/p1079_stones.c)), with the dihedral symmetry broken by putting 3 east or south-east of the 1. The program stops with an error if a configuration approaches the edge of its 80 × 80 board, which never happened.

**Step 4 (computed): checks.** [code/p1079_verify.py](code/p1079_verify.py) is written independently of the C program, in plain Python with its own neighbour logic:

- a second exhaustive search, which gives 13, 23, 31, 41, 53 for n = 1 … 5;
- a replay of our configurations for n = 4, 6, 7, 8, checking that every prime equals the sum of its smaller neighbours and that the primes run from 3 without a gap;
- the same replay for Kamenetsky's configurations for n = 4 … 9, parsed from the page text, which are all valid. Vecchi's configurations are images and could not be replayed.

**What is not settled.** n = 9 would need roughly 7 times the work of n = 8 (about three hours on two cores), so 97 (n = 9) and 103 (n = 10) remain best known only.
