# Puzzle 443: Sum of cubes of consecutive primes

> **In short:** The page asks whether a sum of cubes of consecutive primes can be a cube, or a prime cube; puzzle 393 asks the same and neither page has an answer. (1) Two primes never work, by Fermat's theorem for exponent 3. (2) All 2920799693 sums of 2 to 68042 consecutive prime cubes up to 10²² were tested: none is a cube. (3) A window sum N is a cube with probability N^(−2/3)/3; summed over the windows this predicts 0.064 solutions in the tested range and 5·10⁻⁶ beyond it. (4) The same count for squares predicts 39 solutions below 10¹⁴, and 30 exist.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_443.htm)

**Status:** answered (probably never: no solution with sum ≤ 10²², expected number beyond 5·10⁻⁶; heuristic, not a proof) · also puzzle 393 Q1

## The question

SCCP is any sum of the cubes of consecutive primes. Q1: can an SCCP be a cube? Q2: a prime cube? [Puzzle 393](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_393.htm) Q1 asks for "a sum of consecutive cubed primes equal to another cube (prime or not)", after puzzle 128 found sums of consecutive squared primes equal to a square, such as 355363² + … + 355951² = 2489647² (49 terms).

## How we found it

Write the sum of the cubes of L consecutive primes starting at pᵢ as S_L(i). Then S_L(i) = S_{L−1}(i) + p_{i+L−1}³, so all windows of length L come from those of length L − 1 by one array addition, and S_L(i) grows with i, so the windows with S ≤ X are a prefix. [`code/p443_search.py`](code/p443_search.py) keeps each sum twice, as a float (to get m = round(S^(1/3))) and modulo 2⁶⁴ (to test m³ = S), and recomputes any hit with exact integers. The largest array has 1.1·10⁶ entries; the run to X = 10²² takes 90 s and 190 MB.

## The math

**Step 1 (proved). Two terms.** p³ + q³ = m³ has no solution in positive integers (Euler, Fermat's theorem for exponent 3), so L ≥ 3.

**Step 2 (proved). The test is exact.** The float sum adds L positive terms, so its relative error is below L·2⁻⁵³ ≤ 68042·2⁻⁵³ = 7.6·10⁻¹². The root m = round(S^(1/3)) is exact when the relative error of S is below 3/(2m) ≥ 1.5·X^(−1/3) = 7·10⁻⁸; the script asserts a factor 10 of margin and also tries m ± 1. A true cube then satisfies m³ ≡ S (mod 2⁶⁴), so no solution is lost; a false match would be removed by the exact recomputation.

**Step 3 (computed). The search.** The primes below 17099859 = (X/2)^(1/3) + 100 give 2920799693 windows of 2 to 68042 consecutive primes with sum ≤ X = 10²². None of the sums is a cube. So Q1 and Q2 have no solution with sum up to 10²².

**Step 4 (heuristic). The expected number.** Near N the cubes have density N^(−2/3)/3. Summing this over all windows with L ≥ 3 and N ≤ 10²² gives 0.064 (0.120 with L = 2 included); nearly all of it comes from the smallest sums, and the decades 10¹⁹, 10²⁰, 10²¹ contribute 7.13·10⁻⁶, 4.51·10⁻⁶ and 2.87·10⁻⁶. The number of windows per decade grows by a factor 2.95 and the density falls by 10^(−2/3) = 0.215, so each decade contributes 0.633 times the one before; the geometric tail beyond 10²² is 2.87·10⁻⁶·0.633/0.367 = 4.9·10⁻⁶. With the range below 10²² empty, a solution is expected with probability about 5·10⁻⁶.

**Step 5 (empirical). The model on squares.** For squares the density is N^(−1/2)/2 and the sum over the windows diverges (each decade adds 1.34 times the one before), so infinitely many solutions are expected. Up to 10¹⁴ the model gives 39.2 and the search finds 30, from 41² + … + 173² = 586² (the example on page 128) to 9158347²; up to 10¹² it gives 22.2 against 11. The model is right within a factor 2, on the high side.

**Step 6 (proved). Why the square solutions with a prime root have 49, 6913, 10609 terms.** Every prime p > 3 has p² ≡ 1 (mod 24). A sum of L such squares is ≡ L (mod 24) and the squares mod 24 are 0, 1, 4, 9, 12, 16, so L must lie in one of these six classes; if the root is itself a prime > 3, then L ≡ 1 (mod 24). The three prime-root solutions on page 128 have L = 49, 6913 and 10609, all ≡ 1 (mod 24); L need not be a square.
