# Puzzle 157: There are exactly 20 perfect zip primes

> **In short:** "Zipping" a number into k parts deals its digits out like cards: part 1 gets digits 1, k + 1, 2k + 1, …, part 2 gets digits 2, k + 2, … A perfect zip prime with n digits stays prime, part by part, for every k from 1 to n. Sudipta Das listed 20 of them in 2002 (the largest is 5222333), but the puzzle's first question, to *show* that there are only finitely many, had no answer. We prove it with one lemma: the two halves of a perfect zip prime (its odd-place digits and its even-place digits) are again perfect zip primes. So every perfect zip prime is built from two shorter ones. The 18 numbers that could be built with 8 to 14 digits all fail, and by induction nothing longer exists. Das's list is complete: exactly 20 perfect zip primes.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_157.htm)

**Status:** answered (Q1) · proof (complete)

## The question

Walter Schneider defines zipping: a number N with digits d₁d₂…dₙ is split into k parts, where part j is the number with digits d_j, d_(j+k), d_(j+2k), … in that order. N is *k-zippable* if all k parts are prime, a *zip prime of order K* if it is k-zippable for k = 1, …, K, and a *perfect zip prime* if K = n. The page's example: 27239 gives 229 and 73 for k = 2, and 23, 79 and 2 for k = 3, so it is a zip prime of order 4 (the part 9 kills k = 5).

**Q1.** Show that there are only a finite quantity of perfect zip primes and obtain all of them. (Q2 asks for five zip primes of order 7 and Q3 for zip primes of order 8, 9 and 10.)

## How we found it

Das remarked that the parts of a zip prime "are zip primes themselves". We asked which parts keep the *full* zipping structure. Zipping into an even number 2m of parts treats the odd-place and the even-place digits separately, so the k = 2 parts carry all even zippings with them. That turns the question into a recursion on the length, and the recursion dies out after 7 digits.

## The math

Write N = d₁d₂…dₙ and let zip_k(N) be its k parts: part j (1 ≤ j ≤ k) has the digits d_j, d_(j+k), d_(j+2k), …

**Step 1 (proved). All digits are 2, 3, 5 or 7.** For k = n each part is a single digit, so a perfect zip prime has only prime digits. In particular it has no 0, so no part ever has a leading zero.

**Step 2 (proved). The halving lemma.** Let A = d₁d₃d₅… (⌈n/2⌉ digits) and B = d₂d₄d₆… (⌊n/2⌋ digits); these are the two parts of zip₂(N). The i-th digit of A is d_(2i−1), so part j of zip_m(A) has the digits

```
a_j, a_(j+m), a_(j+2m), …  =  d_(2j−1), d_(2j−1+2m), d_(2j−1+4m), …  =  part 2j−1 of zip_2m(N)
```

and in the same way part j of zip_m(B) is part 2j of zip_2m(N). So for every m with 2m ≤ n, the parts of zip_m(A) and zip_m(B) are parts of zip_2m(N).

If N is perfect, then:

- B is m-zippable for every m ≤ ⌊n/2⌋, since 2m ≤ n. So B is a perfect zip prime.
- A is m-zippable for every m ≤ ⌊n/2⌋. When n is odd, A has one more length to cover, m = ⌈n/2⌉. There its parts are single digits of N, which are prime by step 1. So A is a perfect zip prime as well.

**Step 3 (proved). The recursion.** Let Pₙ be the set of perfect zip primes with n digits. By step 2, every element of Pₙ (n ≥ 2) is the interleaving of some A ∈ P_⌈n/2⌉ and B ∈ P_⌊n/2⌋. So

```
Pₙ ⊆ { interleave(A, B) : A ∈ P_⌈n/2⌉, B ∈ P_⌊n/2⌋ }
```

and each Pₙ is found by testing finitely many candidates built from shorter ones.

**Step 4 (checked). Lengths 1 to 14.** Starting from P₁ = {2, 3, 5, 7}:

| n | candidates | perfect zip primes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | — | 2, 3, 5, 7 |
| 2 | 16 | 23, 37, 53, 73 |
| 3 | 16 | 223, 233, 337, 523, 733, 773 |
| 4 | 16 | 5233 |
| 5 | 24 | 33377, 72733 |
| 6 | 36 | 272333, 572333 |
| 7 | 6 | 5222333 |
| 8 | 1 | none |
| 9 | 2 | none |
| 10 | 4 | none |
| 11 | 4 | none |
| 12 | 4 | none |
| 13 | 2 | none |
| 14 | 1 | none |

All 18 candidates of lengths 8 to 14 fail. 17 of them are composite, e.g. 55223333 = 11·797·6299 (the only candidate of length 8, made from 5233 and 5233) and 55222222333333 = 11·449·11743·952129 (length 14). The candidate 257722333333 is prime but fails at k = 3.

**Step 5 (proved). No perfect zip prime has more than 14 digits.** Use strong induction on n ≥ 15. The half A has ⌈n/2⌉ digits, and 8 ≤ ⌈n/2⌉ < n. By step 4 or the induction hypothesis, P_⌈n/2⌉ is empty, so by step 3 Pₙ is empty too.

So there are finitely many perfect zip primes: exactly the 20 numbers of the table, the largest being 5222333. As an independent check, a brute force over all 4 + 4² + … + 4¹⁰ numbers with digits 2, 3, 5, 7 up to 10 digits finds the same 20.

**Note for Q3.** The same index argument shows that if N is a zip prime of order K, its halves A and B are zip primes of order ⌊K/2⌋. A zip prime of order 9 is therefore an interleaving of two zip primes of order 4, and one of order 10 an interleaving of two of order 5. That cuts the search space for Q3, but we did not run the search.
