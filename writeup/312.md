# Puzzle 312: Primes whose three-term sums are prime

> **In short:** The page asks (Q1) whether an infinite sequence of primes with every sum of three consecutive terms prime can use every odd prime exactly once, as a term or as a sum, and (Q2) whether the greedy rule "start 3, 5; next take the first prime not yet appeared whose sum with the previous two is prime" produces it. (1) Q2: no. Run exactly, the rule's 29th term is 157 and its sum 139 + 167 + 157 = 463 repeats the earlier sum 151 + 173 + 139; under the other reading of "not appeared" (terms only) the 49th term 193 repeats the sum 661. (2) Q1: with the repaired rule, where the new term and the new sum must both be unused primes, 50000 steps place every odd prime below 857897 exactly once, with only ten primes near the frontier still open; no prime is ever lost. A proof of Q1 would need infinitely many primes q with q + c prime for every even c, so it is out of reach; the construction is strong evidence.

## The question

Rubin: an infinite sequence of primes with every sum of three consecutive terms prime, e.g. 3, 5, 11, 7, 13, 17, 29, 43, 41, … with sums 19, 23, 31, 37, 59, 89, 113, …
Q1. Can every odd prime appear exactly once, in the sequence or in the sums?
Q2. Does the rule "P1 = 3, P2 = 5, then the first prime that has not appeared yet with P(i−2) + P(i−1) + P(i) prime" produce such a sequence? No contribution is on the page.

## How we found it

Running the rule exactly is all Q2 needs: it is deterministic, and the first repeated prime settles it. "Has not appeared yet" has two readings (not yet a term; not yet a term or a sum); both fail, at the 29th and at the 49th term. The failure is always a repeated **sum**: the rule only keeps the new term new, never the new sum. Adding that one condition gives a rule that, in 50000 steps, places every odd prime below 857897 exactly once.

## The math

**Q2 (computed, exact).** With P1 = 3, P2 = 5 and the rule applied with "not appeared in either sequence":

```
terms: 3, 5, 11, 7, 13, 17, 29, 43, 41, 47, 61, 71, 67, 53, 73, 97, 101, 79, 83, 107, 103, 127, 137, 109, 151, 173, 139, 167, 157
sums:  19, 23, 31, 37, 59, 89, 113, 131, 149, 179, 199, 191, 193, 223, 271, 277, 263, 269, 293, 337, 367, 373, 397, 433, 463, 479, 463
```

The 25th sum is 151 + 173 + 139 = 463, and the 29th term 157 (the smallest unused prime with 139 + 167 + 157 prime) gives 463 again. So the rule does not produce the desired sequence. With "not appeared in the term sequence" the terms differ from the 29th on, and the 49th term 193 gives the sum 661 a second time. Either way Q2 is answered: no.

**Why a sum repeats (explained).** At each step the rule chooses the term among the unused primes but never looks at the sum. The sums grow about three times faster than the terms, so a sum lands in a range the sums have already visited with probability about 1/ln s per step, and a collision within a few dozen steps is expected.

**Q1 (computed evidence).** Repaired rule: the next term is the smallest unused prime q such that the sum s is prime and unused; then both q and s are marked used. After 50000 steps the largest term is 858251 and every odd prime below 857897 has appeared exactly once (the ten unplaced primes all lie in the last 400 numbers before the frontier, where the process has not yet arrived). The same holds at every intermediate check (1000 steps: all primes below 11279). No prime is ever skipped for long: the smallest unused prime becomes the next term as soon as the sum with the two previous terms is an unused prime, which happens quickly because the sum ranges over the sparse region far above the terms.

**Why Q1 cannot be proved this way (explained).** For the construction to continue from terms a, b one needs an unused prime q with q + (a + b) prime and unused. That there are infinitely many primes q with q + c prime, for every even c, is de Polignac's conjecture, open for every c. So Q1 has the same status as that conjecture: true on the evidence, unprovable at present.
