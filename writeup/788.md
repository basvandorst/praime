# Puzzle 788: P&p(i)²&RP — the sequence is finite

> **In short:** The sequence is finite, with at most 168 terms. Modulo 7, whether 7 divides P&q²&RP depends only on P mod 7, RP·10^(−L) mod 7 and the number of digits of q² mod 6. That leaves 42 cases. Once q = 1009 is reached (k = 169), each case has a prime q ≤ 1009 that makes P&q²&RP divisible by 7. So no prime P works for k ≥ 169. At k = 168 one case mod 7 still survives, and no prime modulus below 170 blocks k = 168. So the heuristic expectation is that terms exist up to exactly k = 168.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_788.htm)

**Status:** answered · Q2 proved (finite, at most 168 terms)

## The question

Find the smallest prime P such that P&q²&RP is prime for all primes q ≤ p(k). Here RP is the reversal of P and & is concatenation. The known terms are 11, 17, 1097, 7949, 780587, 123638027, 3259714649 and 76526081651, for k = 1..8 (Izquierdo, Devesh, Vantieghem, Havermann). Q2 asks whether the sequence is finite or infinite, and "nobody posted anything about Q2".

## How we found it

Concatenation is a sum of shifted blocks, so N mod m depends only on a few residues. We tested every prime modulus m below 170, with RP left free except mod 3 and 11, where RP is tied to P. Moduli 3, 9 and 11 force classes, such as P ≡ 2 (mod 3), but they never block every P. Modulus 7 does, and nothing else does on its own.

## The math

Let L be the number of digits of P (RP also has L digits, since P does not end in 0), and let d be the number of digits of q². Then

N_q = P·10^(L+d) + q²·10^L + RP.

Since 7 ∤ 10, we have 7 | N_q ⟺ q² ≡ −(x·10^d + y) (mod 7), where x = P mod 7 and y = RP·10^(−L) mod 7. The order of 10 mod 7 is 6, so only d mod 6 matters. For a prime P ≠ 7, x ∈ {1..6} and y ∈ {0..6}, which gives 42 cases, whatever L is.

Let S_d be the set of residues q² mod 7 for primes q ≤ 1009 whose square has d (mod 6) digits:

| d mod 6 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S_d | 1, 2, 4 | 1, 2, 4 | 0, 4 | 1, 2, 4 | 1, 2, 4 | 1, 2, 4 |

1. For fixed x ≠ 0, the targets c_d = −(x·10^d + y) for d = 0..5 are six distinct residues: every residue except −y.
2. A direct check of the 42 cases shows that some d always has c_d ∈ S_d. So some q ≤ 1009 gives 7 | N_q, and N_q > 7.
3. The bottleneck is d ≡ 1. Below 1009 the only primes with a square of 1 or 7 digits are 2 and 3, with residues 4 and 2. The prime 1009 ≡ 1 (mod 7) is the first prime whose square has 7 digits, and it adds residue 1. Without it, the case x ≡ 1, y ≡ 3 survives.
4. P = 7 fails at once: 7&4&7 = 747 = 3²·83.

So a(k) does not exist for k ≥ 169 (p(169) = 1009).

**Is 168 sharp?** At k = 168 (q ≤ 997) exactly one case survives mod 7: P ≡ 1 and RP ≡ 3·10^L. We also checked every prime m < 170, with RP free mod m except for m = 3 and 11. Each of them leaves a surviving class at k = 168. Primes m ≥ 170 cannot block, because 168 values of q remove at most 168·m of the m(m−1) or more states (P mod m, RP mod m). So there is no local obstruction at k = 168. Under the usual prime-tuple heuristics, a(k) then exists for every k ≤ 168, but the terms grow very fast. This part is heuristic: it does not prove that a(9) exists.

## Verify it yourself

`writeup/code/p788_mod7.py` does the following:
