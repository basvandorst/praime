# Puzzle 1223: Primes of the form p_n^(p_k − 1) + p_k − 1

> **In short:** F(n, k) = p_n^(p_k − 1) + p_k − 1 was found prime only for (p_n, p_k) = (2, 2), (3, 3), (31, 31); the page asks for another prime or a proof that none exists. (1) If p_n ≠ p_k then p_k | F, so only G(p) = p^(p−1) + p − 1 matters. (2) G(p) is never divisible by 2 or 3, and a sieve-calibrated model expects 0.41 primes G(p) beyond the searched range, a finite total. So probably no further prime exists (67 %), but it cannot be excluded.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1223.htm)

**Status:** open · proved: only n = k can give a prime (on the page); heuristic (calibrated): 0.41 further primes expected in total, so probably none

## The question

F(n, k) = Prime(n)^(Prime(k) − 1) + (Prime(k) − 1) is prime for (2, 2), (3, 3) and (31, 31), where F = 3, 11 and a 45-digit prime; searched for k ≤ 1000, n ≤ 1000 and far beyond for small k, and for n = k up to the 3000th prime 27449.

**Q.** Find another prime F(n, k), or prove that none exists.

## What the page had vs ours

| | On the page | Ours |
|---|---|---|
| n ≠ k | "I think n must equal k because of Fermat's little theorem" | correct: p_k \| F(n, k) and F > p_k, so F is composite (for p_k = 2, F = p_n + 1 is prime only for p_n = 2) |
| n = k | three primes, "3 and 31 are Mersenne primes" | G(p) = p^(p−1) + p − 1 is never divisible by 2 or 3; expected number of primes G(p): 2.1 for p < 400 (found 1), 0.28 up to 27449 (found 0), 0.41 beyond; the Mersenne primes 7 and 127 give composites |

## How we found it

Once n = k is forced, the question is how often p^(p−1) + p − 1 is prime. For a prime q, divisibility depends on p mod q (the base) and on p − 1 mod (q − 1) (the exponent), and over the primes p both are uniform over the units and independent by the Chinese remainder theorem. That gives exact local densities, a Hardy–Littlewood-type constant, and an expected count whose tail Σ_{p > X} 1/((p−1) ln p) ≈ 1/ln X is finite: the question is of the "finitely many expected" type, unlike 1235.

## The math

**Step 1 (proved; on the page). n = k.** Let p = p_n, r = p_k, p ≠ r. Then p^(r−1) ≡ 1 (mod r) by Fermat, so F = p^(r−1) + r − 1 ≡ 0 (mod r); and F > r, so F is composite. For r = 2, F = p + 1, prime only for p = 2.

**Step 2 (proved). 2 and 3 never divide G(p) = p^(p−1) + p − 1 for p ≥ 5.** G(p) is odd (odd + even). Mod 3: p ≡ 1 gives 1 + 0 = 1; p ≡ 2 gives 2^(p−1) ≡ 1 (p − 1 even) plus 1, so 2.

**Step 3 (heuristic). The density for a prime q ≥ 5.** For a prime p ≠ q, p mod q is a unit and p mod (q − 1) is a unit (p is coprime to q − 1 unless p | q − 1, a negligible set), and the two are independent. With r = p mod q and e = p mod (q − 1): q | G(p) iff r^(e−1) ≡ 1 − r (mod q). So f_q = #{(r, e) : r^(e−1) ≡ 1 − r}/((q−1)·φ(q−1)). The formula is checked against the primes below 200000 for 5 ≤ q < 60. Then P(G(p) prime) ≈ C / ln G(p) with C = 2 · (3/2) · ∏_{q ≥ 5} (1 − f_q)/(1 − 1/q) = 4.16 (product to 1499; 4.07 at 97, 4.14 at 499).

**Step 4 (computed). Calibration.** Of the 2260 primes 5 ≤ p ≤ 20000, 878 have G(p) free of the primes 5 … 300; the model predicts 909 (ratio 0.965).

**Step 5 (heuristic). Expected counts.** Σ C/((p−1) ln p): 2.08 over 5 ≤ p < 400 (found: p = 31), 0.28 over 400 ≤ p ≤ 27449 (found none), and about C/ln 27449 = 0.41 over all p > 27449. The total expectation is finite, so only finitely many primes G(p) are expected; the chance that none remains is e^(−0.41) = 67 %.

**What this does not settle.** A further prime G(p) with p > 27449 (probability about 33 %); any such prime would have more than 120000 digits.
