# Puzzle 24: How lucky was Jack? Primes that stay prime in bases 2 to 9

> **In short:** Jack Brennen found 50006393431, the least prime that stays prime when its base-b digits (b = 2 … 9) are read as a decimal number. The page asks how fortunate he was. A sieve model answers that. Each reading M_b counts as prime with chance 1/ln M_b, corrected for the small primes, and the corrections are computed jointly because the bases are coupled through n. The model:
> - expects **0.21–0.24** such primes below 5·10¹⁰ (two Monte Carlo runs), so finding one there had a chance of about **20 %**;
> - expects 0.64–0.66 below 2.8·10¹¹ (Brennen found 1) and 2.9–3.3 below 3.1·10¹² (4 are known);
> - for bases 2..11 expects 0.58 below Resta's 1.81·10¹⁴;
> - for the open base-12 version expects 0.04 below Resta's search limit of 6·10¹⁵. The median of the first one is about 7·10¹⁷ to 9·10¹⁷, and with 80 % chance it lies between 10¹⁷ and 10¹⁹.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_024.htm)

**Status:** answered · the luck question answered with a calibrated model; forecast for bases 2..12

## The question

Write a prime n in base b and read the digits as a decimal number M_b. Brennen (2001) found 50006393431 to be the least prime with M_b prime for every b = 2 … 9. The page asks: "can somebody calculate how fortunate was Jack in finding this prime number, that is to say the probability of finding a prime like 50006393431 with these properties?" Later contributions:

- **Resta (2003):** three more solutions, 727533146383, 2250332130313 and 2651541199513, from a search up to 3.1·10¹².
- **Resta (2018):** 181395559296673 is the least prime that also works in base 11 (all its base-11 digits are below 10). For base 12, any such prime is larger than 6·10¹⁵.

## The model

For a prime n, each M_b is prime with probability

`[M_b has no prime factor ≤ Q] / ( ∏_(q≤Q)(1 − 1/q) · ln M_b )`

This is the Mertens-corrected form of the prime number theorem used for our other pattern counts (FACTS section 9). The bases are not independent, because all the M_b come from the same n:

- **Last digit.** The last digit of M_b is n mod b, so M_b mod 2 and mod 5 are fixed by n mod b.
- **q | 10 − b.** Since 10 ≡ b (mod q), M_b ≡ n (mod q), so M_b can never be divisible by q. This covers q = 3 for b = 4 and 7, and q = 7 for b = 3.
- **Bases above 10.** Every digit must be below 10.

The Monte Carlo version (`p24_model_mc.py`) handles all of this exactly. For millions of real primes n it computes every M_b mod 30030 jointly, applies a per-base factor for 17 ≤ q < 400, and averages.

The analytic version (`p24_model_analytic.py`) averages the couplings listed above exactly over n mod lcm(2..12)·30, and treats everything else as independent. It comes out a nearly constant factor below the Monte Carlo: 1.69 for bases 2..10 and 1.67 for bases 2..11 in the first run, and 1.49 in the verify run. The spread is Monte Carlo noise of about 10–15 %. Adding base 12 brings no new coupling of this kind, so for base 12 we use the analytic model times that calibration factor.

## The answers

**How fortunate was Jack?** The expected number of solutions below his prime is 0.21–0.24, so P(at least one below 5.0·10¹⁰) ≈ 0.19–0.21. He was moderately lucky, about 1 in 5. The second solution, 727533146383, comes where the model expects the count to pass 1 (around 5·10¹¹).

**Base 12 (bases 2..12).** With the calibration:

| below | expected number | P(at least one) |
|---|---|---|
| 6·10¹⁵ (Resta's limit) | 0.038–0.043 | 0.04 |
| 10¹⁷ | 0.19–0.22 | 0.18–0.20 |
| 10¹⁸ | 0.77–0.87 | 0.54–0.58 |
| 10¹⁹ | 3.2–3.6 | 0.96–0.97 |

So Resta's empty search to 6·10¹⁵ is what the model expects: a 96 % chance of nothing. The least base-12 prime probably lies near 10¹⁸ (median 7·10¹⁷ to 9·10¹⁷), between 10¹⁷ and 10¹⁹ with about 80 % chance. That is out of reach for a plain search here, but a sieve that combines conditions mod 2, 3, 5 and 7 across all bases cuts the candidates by the joint factor of about 400.

This is a heuristic forecast, not a theorem.
