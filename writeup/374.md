# Puzzle 374, Q1.3: Why gaps of 11 dominate between self numbers

> **Credit:** Oscar Volpatti explained the dominance of the gap 11 qualitatively on puzzle 952 (May 2019): blocks of 100 overlap and fill the holes that would give gaps of 2. What is new here is the exact law: one skipped number per carry-free block of ten, about 90 % of gaps equal to 11, and every other gap equal to 2 + 13(t − 1).
>
> **In short:** Why the gap 11 between self numbers dominates was asked; puzzle 952 gave only a qualitative reason. The proof: (1) j + sod(j) = B_q + 2r for j = 10q + r; (2) blocks start 11 apart unless q ends in 9; (3) then only B_q + 9 is skipped. This proves a gap of 11 for 9/10 of all q; the other gaps are measured.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_374.htm)

**Status:** answered (Q1.3), with Q1.1 described for t ≤ 5 (measured)

## The question

Self numbers are the n not of the form j + sod(j), the sum of j and its digit sum. Q1.3 asks why the gap 11 between consecutive self numbers is much more frequent than any other gap.

On the page, Firoozbakht proved that a gap of 1 is impossible. Tramu proposed "11 or ≡ 2 (mod 13)", and Pebody gave counterexamples near powers of 10 (gaps 101, 118, 131, 204). Nobody explained Q1.3.

## The answer

**Blocks.** Write j = 10q + r with 0 ≤ r ≤ 9. Then sod(j) = sod(q) + r, so

j + sod(j) = B_q + 2r,   B_q = 10q + sod(q).

Block q covers B_q, B_q + 2, …, B_q + 18. The next block starts at B_(q+1) = B_q + 11 − 9·t(q), where t(q) is the number of trailing nines of q.

**Normal blocks, t = 0.** Consecutive blocks start 11 apart:

- block q covers the even offsets 0 to 18 from B_q;
- block q + 1 covers the odd offsets 11 to 29;
- block q − 1 covers the odd offsets up to 7.

Only offset 9 is left uncovered. So B_q + 9 is a self number, and the next one is B_(q+1) + 9 = B_q + 20, a gap of exactly 11. This is the generic case: a fraction 9/10 of all q have t = 0.

**Carry blocks, t ≥ 1.** When q ends in t nines, the next block starts 9t − 11 lower than usual, and the gap changes. Measured below 10⁷:

| t | gap | count below 10⁷ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 11 | 877783 (89.8 %) |
| 1 | 2 | 90004 |
| 2 | 15 | 9000 |
| 3 | 28 | 900 |
| 4 | 41 | 90 |
| 5 | 54 | 9 |

So the gap is 11 for t = 0 and 2 + 13(t − 1) for 1 ≤ t ≤ 5, which is Tramu's "≡ 2 (mod 13)". The counts are the numbers of q below 10⁶ with exactly t trailing nines, 9·10^(5−t). That also explains Tramu's frequency formula.

For large t, B jumps back past many earlier blocks at once, and the simple pattern breaks. That is where Pebody's exceptions occur, just after 10^13, 10^14, 10^15 and 10^24. We give the 2 + 13(t − 1) pattern as a measurement for t ≤ 5, not as a proof.
