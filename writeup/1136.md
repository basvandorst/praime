# Puzzle 1136: Divisor functions and Fermat primes

> **In short:** F(p) = d(p − 1) and G(p) = σ(p − 1). Alessandro Casini (on the page, June 2023) proved that G(p) prime implies F(p) prime, and answered Q2–Q4. The converse is the missing half of Q1. F(p) prime forces p = 3 or p = F_(2^n) with F_n prime; then F(p) = F_n and G(p) is the Mersenne number M_(F_n). So Q1 holds unless some n has F_n and F_(2^n) both prime but M_(F_n) composite. Since F_5, …, F_32 are composite, Q1 is true for every prime p except possibly p = F_(2^n) with n ≥ 33, a number with more than 2^(2^33) digits. A full proof would settle an open question about Fermat and Mersenne primes.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1136.htm)

**Status:** answered · Q1 reduced to an open Fermat/Mersenne statement; true except possibly for p = F_(2^n), n ≥ 33

## The question

Q1 asks to prove that F(p) is prime exactly when G(p) is prime. Q2–Q4 ask about the sequence 3, 5, 17, 65537 and why 257 is missing.

Casini's note (references/2023_on-the-puzzle-1136-alessandro-casini.txt) proves the direction G prime ⇒ F prime, and explains Q2–Q4:

- the next terms would need F_8, F_16 and M_257 or M_65537 to be prime;
- 257 = F_3 fails because 3 is not a power of 2.

## The converse

1. d(p − 1) prime means p − 1 = q^(r−1) with q and r prime.
2. p is an odd prime, so p − 1 is even and q = 2. Then p = 2^(r−1) + 1 is prime, so r − 1 = 2^j and p = F_j.
3. r = 2^j + 1 must be prime as well, so j = 0 (p = 3, r = 2) or j = 2^n with r = F_n prime.
4. In that case G(p) = σ(2^(r−1)) = 2^r − 1 = M_(F_n).

So **F(p) prime ⇒ G(p) prime** fails exactly at a prime p = F_(2^n) with F_n prime and M_(F_n) composite.

| n | F_n | F_(2^n) | M_(F_n) | outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 3 | F_1 = 5 | M_3 = 7 | prime |
| 1 | 5 | F_2 = 17 | M_5 = 31 | prime |
| 2 | 17 | F_4 = 65537 | M_17 = 131071 | prime |
| 3 | 257 | F_8 | — | F_8 composite, so no case |
| 4 | 65537 | F_16 | — | F_16 composite, so no case |
| 5 to 32 | composite | — | — | no case |

F_n is composite for 5 ≤ n ≤ 32 (FACTS 6208). So the converse, and with it Q1, holds for every prime p other than possibly p = F_(2^n) with n ≥ 33. Deciding those cases needs the primality of F_n (n ≥ 33), which is open.
