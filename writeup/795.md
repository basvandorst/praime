# Puzzle 795: Primes whose substring reversals are prime for every digit

> **In short:** For a digit a of m, F(a, m) reverses the digit strings between successive occurrences of a. The page asked for the smallest prime p, using exactly D distinct digits, such that F(a, p) is a prime different from p for every digit a of p; the smallest ones were known for D ≤ 6, and candidates for D = 7, 8, 9, 10 (14, 16, 18 and 21 digits) were given without a minimality claim. (1) A digit used once is a fixed point of F, so every digit of p occurs at least twice: a D-digit solution has at least 2D digits, and the last digit is preserved by every transform. (2) An ordered depth-first search over all numbers with 2D digits and each of D digits exactly twice, testing p and its D transforms, settles the minimum: for D = 7 it is 10452765470621 and for D = 8 it is 1021579083257389, smaller than the page's candidate 1036482501452863; the search reproduces the known minima for D = 3 … 6. (3) A pandigital solution needs at least 21 digits, because twenty digits with each digit twice have digit sum 90, a multiple of 3.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_795.htm)

**Status:** beaten · answered (D = 7 confirmed and D = 8 improved, both minima proved by exhaustive ordered search, proven primes; D = 9, 10 verified, minimality open)

## The question

Emmanuel Vantieghem defines F(a, m) for a digit a: the strings between two successive appearances of a in m are reversed (m = 12346597804454672013254628, a = 2: F = 12764544087956432310264528). He asks for primes p such that, for every digit a used in p, F(a, p) is a prime different from p; the smallest ones using 3, 4, 5, 6 distinct digits are 1051051, 39703709, 1497294217 and 102830938219. Q: the smallest such prime using 7, 8, 9, 10, … digits. He answered himself: "could be" 10452765470621 (7 digits), "might be" 1036482501452863 (8), and 102756857398639201 (9) and the pandigital 102344856798765493201 (10), "very likely the smallest (but I'm not 100% sure)".

## How we found it

If a digit occurs once in p, there is no string between two of its occurrences, F(a, p) = p, and p fails. So each of the D digits occurs at least twice and p has at least 2D digits; a solution with exactly 2D digits has every digit exactly twice. The last digit of p is never inside a reversed string (a reversed string lies strictly between two occurrences), so all D transforms end in the same digit as p, and they all have the same digit multiset. That leaves an ordered enumeration: a depth-first search over the digits from the most significant one, keeping each digit's count at most 2 and the number of distinct digits at most D, visits the admissible numbers in increasing order; at each leaf, p and its D transforms are tested (deterministic Miller-Rabin for 64-bit numbers), and the first success is the minimum ([`code/p795_search.c`](code/p795_search.c)).

## The math

**Step 1 (proved). Structure of a solution.** (a) Every used digit occurs at least twice (a digit occurring once is a fixed point). (b) p and all F(a, p) share the last digit, hence all end in 1, 3, 7 or 9, and share the digit sum, hence none is divisible by 3 unless all are: candidates with digit sum divisible by 3 are skipped. (c) With D = 10 and 20 digits, each digit twice, the digit sum is 2·45 = 90, so a pandigital solution has at least 21 digits; Vantieghem's 102344856798765493201 has 21 digits (the digit 4 three times).

**Step 2 (computed). The minima.** The ordered search, run for (L, D) = (7, 3), (8, 4), (10, 5), (12, 6), returns exactly the four minima of the page (there is no 6-digit solution with 3 digits, so 1051051 with the digit 1 three times is the minimum). For (14, 7) the first solution is 10452765470621 after 91236524 candidates, Vantieghem's number, with F(0) = 10745672540621, F(1) = 12607456725401, F(2) = 10452607456721, F(4) = 10456725470621, F(5) = 10456725470621, F(6) = 10452760745621, F(7) = 10452745670621. For (16, 8) the first solution is 1021579083257389 after 1559866697 candidates (about three hours): it uses the digits 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9 twice each and beats the page's candidate 1036482501452863; its transforms are F(0)=1097512083257389 F(1)=1201579083257389 F(2)=1023809751257389 F(3)=1021579083752389 F(5)=1021523809757389 F(7)=1021575238097389 F(8)=1021579083752389 F(9)=1021579837523809.

**Step 3 (computed). D = 9 and 10.** Vantieghem's 102756857398639201 and 102344856798765493201 satisfy the rule, with p and all transforms proven prime (PARI). The ordered search would have to test about 10¹¹ 18-digit candidates below the first number (and far more 21-digit ones), so their minimality stays open.

**Step 4 (heuristic). Why the minima have exactly 2D digits.** A random candidate with 2D digits passes with probability about (2.5·1.5/ln 10^{2D})^{D+1} (the last-digit and digit-sum filters are shared by all D+1 numbers). For D = 7 this is 3.5·10⁻⁸ per candidate against 8·10¹⁰ candidates, so hundreds of 14-digit solutions exist and the least one is small among them, as found (1.045·10¹³, just above the first 14-digit number).
