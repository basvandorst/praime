# Puzzle 282: φ(p) = φ(p′) and σ(p) = σ(p′) for a prime and its reversal

> **In short:** Farideh Firoozbakht asked for non-palindromic primes p with φ(p) = φ(p′) or σ(p) = σ(p′), where p′ is p written backwards. Only one was known: 98639 for σ. Branicky (puzzle 1196) found nothing else up to 10¹⁰ in either case. If p′ = m·b with b prime, the condition becomes a linear equation between r = p′ and its reversal, m·rev(r) = φ(m)·r − m(φ(m) − 1). A carry automaton that reads r from both ends at once solves this for every length. Three congruences rule out every m divisible by 3, 11 or 10. In the m = 19 family the automaton gives **the first φ solutions** (smallest: 67 digits, then 85, 91, …; 20 found up to 115 digits) and **three new σ solutions** (63, 67 and 69 digits). Every p and b quoted below is proven prime (PARI APR-CL).

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_282.htm), follow-up [puzzle 1196](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1196.htm)

**Status:** answered · first φ solutions and three new σ solutions, all proven primes (PARI)

## The question

p is a prime and p′ = rev(p) is its decimal reversal, with p ≠ p′. Find solutions of φ(p) = φ(p′) and of σ(p) = σ(p′), argue that they are improbable, or show that they are impossible.

## How we found it

The old searches tried primes one at a time, and the chance that a random p′ fits is about 10⁻ᵏ for k digits. Instead we fixed the shape of p′, a small number m times one big prime b. Then "φ(p′) = p − 1" becomes a linear equation between p′ and its own reversal, the same kind as 4 · 2178 = 8712. Such equations are solved digit pair by digit pair from the outside in, with one carry coming up from the units digit and one coming down from the leading digit. The carries stay small, so a finite automaton lists every solution of every length. Only the few solutions it gives need primality tests.

## The math

**Step 1: p′ is composite and lies on a fixed side of p** (proved). If p′ were prime, φ(p′) = p′ − 1 = p − 1 would give p′ = p, a palindrome. So p′ is composite. For composite n, φ(n) ≤ n − √n and σ(n) ≥ n + √n + 1. Hence p′ > p in the φ case and p′ < p in the σ case. The last digit of p is 1, 3, 7 or 9 (p > 5), so p′ starts with one of those digits. p′ cannot end in 0, because p has no leading zero.

**Step 2: the linear equation** (proved). Let r = p′ = m·b with b prime and b ∤ m. Then φ(r) = φ(m)(b − 1) and σ(r) = σ(m)(b + 1). Substitute b = r/m and p = rev(r):

```
phi:    p − 1 = φ(m)(r/m − 1)   ⇔   m · rev(r) = φ(m) · r − m(φ(m) − 1)
sigma:  p + 1 = σ(m)(r/m + 1)   ⇔   m · rev(r) = σ(m) · r + m(σ(m) − 1)
```

Both have the form P·rev(r) = Q·r + C with P = m. Check on the known solution: r = 93689 = 19 · 4931, and 19 · 98639 = 1874141 = 20 · 93689 + 361 ✓. Reducing mod m gives Q·r ≡ 0, so m/gcd(m, Q) divides r automatically. For prime m that is m | r.

**Step 3: three obstructions** (proved). A number and its reversal have the same digit sum, so rev(r) ≡ r (mod 9). The alternating digit sum gives rev(r) ≡ (−1)^(k−1)·r (mod 11) for k digits. So:

- 3 | m ⇒ 3 | r ⇒ 3 | p, impossible.
- 11 | m ⇒ 11 | r ⇒ 11 | p, impossible.
- 10 | m ⇒ r ends in 0, impossible by step 1.

This matters: the m with by far the most automaton solutions are all dead. m = 729 has a Fibonacci number of solutions at every length (ratio φ(m)/m = 2/3), and m = 417, 4134, 2480 have hundreds of thousands to millions at 50–60 digits. All of them are divisible by 3 or by 10.

**Step 4: the two-ended carry automaton** (proved). Write r = Σ dⱼ10ʲ (j = 0 … k−1), so rev(r) = Σ d_(k−1−j)10ʲ. The equation says Σ eⱼ10ʲ = C with eⱼ = P·d_(k−1−j) − Q·dⱼ. Define carries c₀ = −C and c_(j+1) = (cⱼ + eⱼ)/10. Each division must be exact, and the equation holds iff c_k = 0. Two estimates bound the carries: |cⱼ| ≤ max(P, Q) + |C|/10ʲ, and reading downwards from c_k = 0, cⱼ = 10·c_(j+1) − eⱼ. Step t picks the digit pair (d_t, d_(k−1−t)). That moves the low carry c_t → c_(t+1) and the high carry c_(k−t) → c_(k−1−t) at the same time. The state is the pair of carries, and when the two ends meet the carries must agree (with a middle digit for odd k). One breadth-first pass gives all lengths at once. It is exact whenever 10^⌈k/2⌉ > |C|. Shorter k are skipped; for m ≤ 20000 that is always k ≤ 17, and for m ≤ 300 it is k ≤ 10, which Branicky's search already covers. Checked against a direct search of all r < 10⁶ for every m < 120: identical solution sets wherever that condition holds.

**Step 5: the search** (exhaustive in the stated class). For every m ≤ 20000 not divisible by 3, 11 or 10, every length 11 ≤ k ≤ 70, both cases: list every automaton solution r, keep those with m | r, b = r/m prime and b ∤ m, and p = rev(r) prime. The run takes about a minute. The four hits are the ones in the table (63, 67, 67, 69 digits). For the m = 19 φ family alone, lengths 71 to 120 give 19 more (85, 91, 3 × 101, 2 × 103, 5 × 113, 7 × 115 digits; PRP, the 85- and 91-digit ones proven).

So the 67-digit φ solution is the least one with p′ = m·b (b prime, b ∤ m, m ≤ 20000) among all lengths 18 … 67, and among all lengths 11 … 67 for m ≤ 300. Not covered: p′ with two or more large prime factors or with b² | p′, and large m at 11–17 digits. Whether a smaller φ solution of such a shape exists is still open.

**Step 6: why m = 19, and how many to expect** (heuristic, calibrated). The number N_k of k-digit automaton solutions for (σ, m = 19) grows about 1.127× per digit: 8913 at k = 100, 11.3 million at k = 160. A solution is a hit when b and p are both prime, with chance about 1/(ln b · ln p) for random numbers. Summed over k ≤ 70 this gives 0.20. The candidates are not random, though. p ends in 1, 3, 7 or 9 by construction (factor 2.5). Steps 2–3 force b ≡ 2 and p ≡ 2 (mod 3) (factor 1.5 each), b is odd (factor 2), and 11 divides neither (factor 1.1 each). That is about 13.6 in all, so 2.8 hits are expected against 3 found. The same count predicts about 18000 σ hits between 71 and 160 digits. Since N_k grows exponentially and the prime chance only falls like 1/k², the expected total diverges: **both equations should have infinitely many solutions**, almost all of them in a few rich automata such as m = 19. A simple reason why these automata are rich is not known to us. The repeated blocks in the hits (…5498641604588641…) are cycles of the carry graph.

## Verify it yourself

[code/p282_verify.py](code/p282_verify.py) checks every claim from scratch: the reversal, p′ = m·b, PARI primality proofs of p and b, φ or σ computed directly with sympy, the linear equation, and the mod 9 / mod 11 reversal identities on random numbers. [code/p282_search.py](code/p282_search.py) is the automaton search; `p282_search.py phi 20000 11 70` and `p282_search.py sigma 20000 5 70` reproduce the table (about a minute each).

```python
import cypari2
from sympy import totient, divisor_sigma

pari = cypari2.Pari()
p = 7198858264383196858264206778858264384978840444384977076264384978957
r = int(str(p)[::-1])
b = r // 19
assert r == 19 * b and pari.isprime(p, 2) and pari.isprime(b, 2)
assert totient(p) == totient(r) == 18 * (b - 1)
q = 986146894593861385310540531145946885405310540613861468945938639
s = int(str(q)[::-1])
assert s % 19 == 0 and pari.isprime(q, 2) and pari.isprime(s // 19, 2)
assert divisor_sigma(q) == divisor_sigma(s)
print("phi(p) = phi(rev p) and sigma(q) = sigma(rev q), both proven")
```
