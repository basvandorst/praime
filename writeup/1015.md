# Puzzle 1015: Sequence of semiprimes (and puzzle 640)

> **In short:** From a semiprime p·q the next term is the concatenation p&q or q&p, if it is a semiprime; the record is 23 terms, and the page's table of smallest starts below 10⁸ has no entry for 22 terms. A tree search over all 143 million semiprimes from 10⁸ to 10⁹ gives: the smallest start for 22 terms is 414428717; a second one is 978652727; no sequence of 24 or more starts below 10⁹. For the stricter rule of puzzle 640 (only p&q with p ≤ q, record 16), a 17th term needs about 10¹¹ starts: not attempted.

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net 1015](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1015.htm) · [640](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_640.htm)

**Status:** open · the table entry for 22 terms filled (computed); the record of 23 terms stands below 10⁹

## The question

15 = 3·5 → 35 = 5·7 → 57 = 3·19 → 319 = 11·29 → 2911 = 41·71 → … : each term is a semiprime, and the next term is made by writing its two prime factors side by side, in either order. Q1: the longest sequence. Puzzle 640 asks the same with the smaller factor always first.

## How we found it

1. Every concatenation is larger than the product it comes from, so the terms increase and the possible continuations form a finite tree. The longest sequence from a start is the depth of its tree.
2. PARI's `forfactored` hands out the factorisation of every integer in a range; for each semiprime the tree is searched recursively. 143 million starts took 30 minutes on each core.
3. Chain statistics decide what is reachable. With both orders allowed, the number of starts with at least L terms drops by a factor of about 2.7 per extra term (91, 30, 13, 5 starts for L = 17 … 20 between 10⁸ and 5.3·10⁸).
4. For the rule of puzzle 640 the drop is a factor 4.3 per term ([`code/p640_stats.py`](code/p640_stats.py) `8e9 8.004e9 12`: 582505 starts, 139890 with 2 terms, …, 3 with 9 terms). A 17th term then needs about 1.7·10¹¹ starts, some 80 hours here. No congruence helps, because each term depends on the factorisation of the one before.

## The math

**Increasing (proved).** For primes p, q with d digits in q: p&q = p·10ᵈ + q > p·q, since 10ᵈ > q. So a sequence never returns to a smaller number and the search from n ends.

**Starts (definition used by the page's table).** The table lists, for each length, the smallest start that is not itself a concatenation a&b of two primes: such a number would only continue the sequence of a·b. With this convention the script reproduces the table's starts for lengths 1 to 12.

**Smallest start for 22 (computed).** The page's table covers all starts below 10⁸ and has none for 22. Our search covers every semiprime from 10⁸ to 10⁹; the starts with longest sequence at least 21 are 414428717 (22), 974272461 (21; it is the second term of the former) and 978652727 (22). So 414428717 is the smallest start for 22 terms, given the page's search below 10⁸.
