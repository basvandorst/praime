# Puzzle 1165: Two disjoint distance sets with equal products of prime pairs

> **In short:** P_n(x) is the product of p(p + x) over the pairs of odd primes p < p + x ≤ p_n. The page asks for disjoint sets d, k of even numbers with ∏_(x∈d) P_n(x) = ∏_(x∈k) P_n(x) = (p_n#/2)^m. Its example is n = 14, m = 6; one contribution lists pairs for n = 7 … 19. (1) As in puzzle 1166, a prime q appears in ∏_(x∈X) P_n(x) once for each partner q ± x (x ∈ X) among the odd primes ≤ p_n. So the equation says that every odd prime ≤ p_n has exactly m partners at distances in d, and also exactly m at distances in k. (2) None of the listed pairs n = 7 … 19 meets this. Their d and k give different exponents (for n = 7, every prime has 2 partners through d but 3 through k). For n = 11 and 19, d even leaves a prime without partners. (3) For every n ≤ 30 and every m, an exact 0/1 search (CP-SAT, all cases decided; HiGHS agrees for n ≤ 22) finds a solution only for n = 14, m = 6. There it is unique: the page's own pair, which splits all even distances 2 … 40 between d and k.

## The question

P_n(x) is the product of all p(p + x) with p, p + x odd primes and p + x ≤ p_n. For n = 14 the page gives d = {6, 8, 12, 14, 16, 28, 30, 34, 36, 38} and k = {2, 4, 10, 18, 20, 22, 24, 26, 32, 40}, with ∏_(x∈d) P_14(x) = ∏_(x∈k) P_14(x) = (p_14#/2)⁶. Question: find disjoint sets d, k of even numbers with

```
∏_(x∈d) P_n(x) = ∏_(x∈k) P_n(x) = (A002110(n)/2)^m.
```

One contribution (Rebert) lists pairs for n = 7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 14, 15, 17, 19, for example n = 7, d = {4, 8, 12}, k = {2, 6, 10, 14}.

## The math

**Step 1 (proved): the degree form.** Let S be the odd primes ≤ p_n. Each P_n(x) is a product of distinct primes from S, and q ∈ S occurs once for each of q − x, q + x that lies in S. So

```
v_q( ∏_(x∈X) P_n(x) ) = Σ_(x∈X) ( [q − x ∈ S] + [q + x ∈ S] ) = deg_X(q),
```

and the equation holds iff deg_d(q) = deg_k(q) = m for every q ∈ S, with d ∩ k = ∅. Distances without any prime pair (P_n(x) = 1) are left out; they could be added to either set without effect.

**Step 2 (computed): the listed pairs.** The degrees of the nine listed pairs:

| n | 7 | 8 | 9 | 11 | 13 | 14 | 15 | 17 | 19 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| deg_d | 2 | 2 | 3 | 0 or 2 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 7 | 0 or 2 |
| deg_k | 3 | 4 | 4 | 5 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 8 | 7 |

So the two products differ in every case: for n = 7 they are (p₇#/2)² and (p₇#/2)³. For n = 11 and 19 the d-product is not even a power of p_n#/2. (The d sets for n = 13, 14, 15 are the degree-4 sets of [puzzle 1166](1166.md).) The page's own example has deg_d = deg_k = 6.

**Step 3 (computed): the exact search.** For each n = 3 … 30 and each m = 1 … |S| − 1, the 0/1 system "deg_d(q) = deg_k(q) = m for all q, u_x + v_x ≤ 1" was given to CP-SAT. Every one of these problems was decided (no time-outs at 20 s each). A solution exists only for n = 14, m = 6. Enumeration there gives exactly two ordered solutions: the page's (d, k) and the same pair swapped. HiGHS (scipy `milp`) solves the same programs for n ≤ 22 and agrees on every (n, m). For n = 31 … 34 some middle values of m were not decided within 20 s, and we stopped there.

**Why the example is special (computed).** At n = 14, d ∪ k is the full set {2, 4, …, 40} of possible distances (p₁₄ = 43). So the 20 distances split into two halves that each give every odd prime below 43 exactly six partners. Among all 3²⁰ ways to choose two disjoint subsets of these distances, only this split and its mirror image work.
