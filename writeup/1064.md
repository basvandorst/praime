# Puzzle 1064: GCD(2^p + 1, 3^p + 1)

> **In short:** For G(p) = gcd(2^p + 1, 3^p + 1), prime factors q ≡ 1 (mod 2p) were proved and q ≡ 1 (mod 6p) only conjectured. The proof: (1) (−3)^p ≡ 1 (mod q); (2) so −3 is a quadratic residue mod q; (3) so q ≡ 1 (mod 3). This proves q ≡ 1 (mod 6p); a heuristic model also expects infinitely many composite G(p).

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1064.htm)

**Status:** answered · q ≡ 1 (mod 6p) proved (conjecture of Kalogeropoulos, Cavegn and van Delden); composite G(p) explained, heuristically infinite

## The question

Sebastián Martín Ruiz checked that G(p) = gcd(2^p + 1, 3^p + 1) is 1 or a prime for the first 60,000 primes. Q1 asks to extend the check, Q2 for the first composite, Q3 whether "1 or prime" can be proved, and Q4 to prove G(p) ≡ 1 (mod p).

On the page:

- **Q2:** Giorgos Kalogeropoulos found the first composites, at p = 2243399, 2334547 and 2743723. Shyam Sunder Gupta extended the search to the 480,000th prime (7052113) and found nine composite values in all.
- **Q4:** Adam Stinchcombe, Emmanuel Vantieghem, Jan van Delden and Oscar Volpatti proved that every prime factor is ≡ 1 (mod p), and Volpatti and van Delden that it is ≡ 1 (mod 2p).
- **Open:** Kalogeropoulos ("all primes Pn are of the form 6kp+1 … I don't have a proof"), Simon Cavegn ("Conjecture: … only prime factors of the form 6*p*n+1") and van Delden ("I strongly feel that one should have q=1 mod 6p, for which one needs to prove that 3|q-1 as well") left the factor 3 open. Vantieghem: "I think there may be infinitely many" composites.

## How we found it

We broke the question into single claims and checked each one on exact data before using it. We computed G(p) by a full gcd and factored it for every prime p < 2·10⁵: 267 prime factors in all. Each step below holds for all of them. The model of the last section uses only these proved rules, and we calibrated it before it predicts anything.

## The math

The chain of claims, each validated before the next is used:

| # | Claim | Why | Validated |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2p \| q − 1 | order of 2 mod q is 2p (page) | proof; all 267 factors, p < 2·10⁵ |
| 2 | 3 \| q − 1, so q ≡ 1 (mod 6p) | (−3)^p ≡ 1 makes −3 a quadratic residue | proof; all 267 factors |
| 3 | q \| G(p) ⇔ q = 1 + dp with −2, −3 both d-th powers | x^p = x^((q−1)/d) | proof; both directions on exact data |
| 4 | q ≡ 1, 19 (mod 24); d ≢ 4 (mod 8); d ≡ 2 (mod 4) ⇒ p ≡ d/2 (mod 4) | −2 is a quadratic residue, q = 1 + dp mod 8 | proof; all 267 factors and the 18 page factors |
| 5 | P(1 + dp \| G(p)) = rule · 4/d² · C(d)/ln(1 + dp) | 2/d per base once claims 2 and 4 fix the quadratic characters; Hardy–Littlewood | heuristic; 1127 observed vs 1118 predicted, p < 10⁶ |
| 6 | factors for different d occur independently on the same p | different primes q | heuristic; single-base pairs 1294 vs 1267 |
| 7 | composite G(p) infinitely often, ~X/(ln X)³; 88 % with p ≡ 3 (mod 4) | claims 4–6 | heuristic; 9 found vs 14.5 expected to 7052113; all 9 have p ≡ 3 (mod 4) |

Claims 1–4 are theorems. Claims 5–7 are a model, and the table shows how well it fits.

Let p be an odd prime and q a prime dividing G(p). Then q ≠ 2 (2^p + 1 is odd) and q ≠ 3 (3 ∤ 3^p + 1).

**Step 1 (on the page): 2p | q − 1.** From 2^p ≡ −1 (mod q), the order of 2 divides 2p but not p, so it is 2 or 2p. Order 2 would mean q | 3. So the order is 2p, and Fermat gives 2p | q − 1.

**Step 2 (new): 3 | q − 1.** Since p is odd, (−3)^p = −3^p ≡ 1 (mod q). Write q − 1 = 2p·m. Then

(−3)^((q−1)/2) = ((−3)^p)^m ≡ 1 (mod q),

so −3 is a quadratic residue mod q by Euler's criterion. That holds exactly when q ≡ 1 (mod 3). Together with Step 1, **q ≡ 1 (mod 6p)**, the page's conjecture. (For p = 2, G(2) = 5 and 5 ≢ 1 (mod 12). The proof needs p odd.)

**Step 3 (new): the exact criterion.** Write q = 1 + dp. Then

q | G(p)  ⇔  (−2)^p ≡ 1 and (−3)^p ≡ 1 (mod q)  ⇔  −2 and −3 are both d-th power residues mod q,

because x^p = x^((q−1)/d) ≡ 1 says exactly that x is a d-th power. By Step 2, 6 | d.

**Step 4 (new): mod 8.** In the same way (−2)^((q−1)/2) ≡ 1, so −2 is a quadratic residue and q ≡ 1 or 3 (mod 8). With q ≡ 1 (mod 3) that gives **q ≡ 1 or 19 (mod 24)**. Since q = 1 + dp with p odd:

- d ≡ 4 (mod 8) would give q ≡ 5 (mod 8), which is impossible. So d = 12, 36, 60, … never occur.
- d ≡ 2 (mod 4) gives q ≡ 1 + 2p·(d/2) (mod 8). This is 3 only if p·(d/2) ≡ 1 (mod 4), that is **p ≡ d/2 (mod 4)**.
- 8 | d puts no condition on p.

So p ≡ 3 (mod 4) allows d ∈ {6, 30, 54, 78, …} ∪ {24, 48, 72, …}, and p ≡ 1 (mod 4) allows d ∈ {18, 42, 66, …} ∪ {24, 48, 72, …}. The most common factor, q = 6p + 1, only occurs for p ≡ 3 (mod 4).

Validation: all 267 factors for p < 2·10⁵ obey Steps 1–4, with no exception. So do all eighteen factors of the nine composite values on the page.

**Step 5: Q3, is G(p) always 1 or prime?** No: the page lists nine composite values, and we recomputed all of them in full. Why they keep appearing comes from Step 3. Given that q = 1 + dp is prime, −2 and −3 are each a d-th power with chance 2/d, because their quadratic characters are already forced to be +1. With the Hardy–Littlewood factor C(d) for 1 + dp being prime, the chance that 1 + dp divides G(p) is

P_d(p) = [local rule of Step 4] · (4/d²) · C(d) / ln(1 + dp),   C(6) = C(24) = 2.641.

Calibration on every factor with d ≤ 300 and p < 10⁶ (exact):

| d | 6 | 18 | 24 | 30 | 42 | 48 | all d ≤ 300 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| observed | 839 | 66 | 92 | 35 | 15 | 16 | **1127** |
| model | 800.9 | 82.3 | 91.1 | 38.4 | 17.2 | 21.8 | **1118.2** |

A composite G(p) needs two allowed values d < e for the same p. We tested whether different d act independently on the same p with the single-base events (q | 2^p + 1), which are 15 times more common. All pairs with d < e ≤ 60 for p < 10⁶ give 1294 observed against 1267 expected. Summing P_d·P_e over allowed pairs, with the joint Hardy–Littlewood correction for 1 + dp and 1 + ep both prime, gives the expected number of composite G(p) with p ≤ X:

| X | 2243399 | 7052113 | 10⁷ | 10⁸ | 10⁹ | 10¹⁰ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| expected | 5.7 | 14.5 | 19 | 130 | 924 | 6800 |
| on the page | 1 (the first) | 9 | | | | |

The count grows like X/(ln X)³, so the model predicts infinitely many composite G(p). It is not a proof: it assumes Hardy–Littlewood and independent power-residue characters. Up to 7052113 it expected 14.5 composites against 9 found, a chance of about 8 % for 9 or fewer. The first composite comes late: 5.7 were expected before 2243399.

The pair weight is 0.0163 for p ≡ 3 (mod 4) and 0.0022 for p ≡ 1 (mod 4). The reason is that d = 6, by far the most common factor, pairs only with p ≡ 3 (mod 4). So 88 % of composite G(p) should have p ≡ 3 (mod 4). All nine on the page do (chance 0.88⁹ = 0.31), and their factor pairs are (6, 24) twice, (6, 30) three times, (6, 54) twice, (24, 78) and (6, 270). The model ranks (6, 24) and (6, 30) as the two most likely pairs.
