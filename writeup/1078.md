# Puzzle 1078: p + (q − p)·2^(p−2) for consecutive primes

> **In short:** For consecutive primes p < q the page asks for a proof that N = p + (q − p)·2^(p−2) is composite for all p > 3, or a counterexample. The page proves 3 | N unless 6 | q − p, and finds no prime for p < 75000. (1) In the remaining case N is odd, N ≡ p (mod 3) and N ≡ (q − p)/2 (mod p), so no fixed small prime is forced. (2) About 15 % of these N have no prime factor below 10⁴, and that share is the same in every range up to 3·10⁵, so no finite covering set exists. A proof of "always composite" is therefore not available by the usual means. (3) A sieve-calibrated count gives an expected 1.56 primes for p < 75000, so seeing none has a 21 % chance. Each doubling of p adds only about 0.085, because the expected number grows like 1.4·ln ln p. (4) So a prime N should exist: the claim is heuristically false. But the first prime is expected only near p ≈ 10⁸ (median), with N of about 3·10⁷ digits; the chance of one with p < 1.2·10⁶ is about 27 %.

## The question

Let p < q be consecutive primes. Prove that p + 2^(p−2)·(q − p) is composite for all p > 3, or find a counterexample. On the page:

- the remark that such forms are composite only through covering congruences or algebraic factorisations (R. Chen);
- the proof that 3 | N whenever q − p ≡ 2 or 4 (mod 6), and a PARI search with no prime for 6 | q − p and p < 75000 (A. Rocchelli).

## The math

**Step 1 (proved): the factor 3.** p is odd, so 2^(p−2) ≡ 2 (mod 3) and N ≡ p + 2g (mod 3) with g = q − p. Since q = p + g is not divisible by 3: g ≡ 2 (mod 6) forces p ≡ 2 and N ≡ 0; g ≡ 4 (mod 6) forces p ≡ 1 and N ≡ 0; for 6 | g, N ≡ p ≢ 0 (mod 3).

**Step 2 (proved): no forced factor when 6 | g.** N is odd. By Fermat, 2^(p−2) ≡ 1/2 (mod p), so N ≡ g/2 ≢ 0 (mod p). For a prime r ∤ g, r ≠ p, the residue N mod r depends on p mod r and on p mod ord_r(2). Exactly one class of p makes r | N, and since q ≢ 0 (mod r) removes another class, r divides N about 1/(r − 2) of the time. For r | g, N ≡ p (mod r) is never 0.

**Step 3 (computed): no covering set.** Among the p with 6 | g, the share of N with no prime factor in 5 … 10⁴ is 15.1 % (p < 75000), 14.9 % (75000 … 150000) and 16.5 % (150000 … 300000). A finite covering set would drive this share to 0. Its constancy (close to ∏(1 − 1/r), adjusted for Step 2) says that the residues behave randomly.

**Step 4 (heuristic, sieve-calibrated): how many primes to expect.** For each N with no prime factor below R = 10⁴, the chance of being prime is ∏_(r<R)(1 − 1/r)⁻¹/ln N, and it is 0 for the others. Summed:

| p range | N with 6 \| g | free of primes < 10⁴ | expected primes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 … 75000 | 2981 | 450 | 1.560 |
| 75000 … 150000 | 2632 | 391 | 0.086 |
| 150000 … 300000 | 5003 | 826 | 0.090 |

So P(no prime below 75000) = e^(−1.56) = 21 %, consistent with the search. Since ln N ≈ p ln 2, the expected count grows like κ·ln ln p with κ ≈ 1.4 (0.086 per doubling at p ≈ 10⁵ gives κ = 0.086·ln p/ln 2). It diverges, so primes should appear. Given none below 75000, the expected number below P is 1.4·ln(ln P/ln 75000). That reaches ln 2 (median of the first prime) at P ≈ 1.0·10⁸, and is 0.31 at P = 1.2·10⁶ (a 27 % chance of a prime there).

**Answer.** The statement cannot be proved by a covering argument, and it is almost surely false. The counterexample is probably far beyond reach: near p = 10⁸, N has about 3·10⁷ digits.
