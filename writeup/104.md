# Puzzle 104: Why the "squares of the halves" solutions come in a Mersenne number of pairs

> **In short:** Numbers of length L that equal the sum of the squares of their two halves come in pairs, and the number of pairs (0, 1, 1, 1, 3, 3, 7, 15 for L = 2, 4, …, 16) was conjectured to be 2^(F−1) − 1, F the number of prime factors of 10ᴸ + 1, and later claimed to be an upper bound. The proof: (1) each pair corresponds to one way of writing 10ᴸ + 1 = u² + v² with u odd, except one trivial way; (2) every prime factor of 10ᴸ + 1 is 1 mod 4, so by Jacobi's two-square theorem there are exactly d(10ᴸ + 1)/2 such ways, d the number of divisors. This proves that the number of pairs is exactly d(10ᴸ + 1)/2 − 1: it equals 2^(F−1) − 1 when 10ᴸ + 1 is squarefree and is larger, not smaller, otherwise (e.g. L = 136, where 17² divides 10¹³⁶ + 1).

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_104.htm)

**Status:** answered (sub-question 3a, De Geest's observations) · proof

## The question

The page's main sequence consists of numbers equal to the sum of the cubes of their three thirds. Generalisation (a) asks for numbers of even length L = 2m equal to the sum of the squares of their two halves, written with leading zeros allowed: N = x·10ᵐ + y = x² + y² with 0 ≤ x, y < 10ᵐ. De Geest listed every solution up to L = 18 and noticed two things. The solutions come in pairs with the same right half and left halves summing to 10ᵐ. The number of pairs is 0, 1, 1, 1, 3, 3, 7, 15, 3 for L = 2, 4, …, 18. Rivera asked: "Can someone explain the Patrick's observations about this kind of numbers?"

## How we found it

Chilali reduced the problem to writing b² + 1 (b = 10ᵐ) as a sum of two squares, and Nash saw Mersenne numbers in the counts. The bridge between the two is the exact count of two-square representations (Jacobi). For numbers whose prime factors are all 1 mod 4, that count is just 4 times the number of divisors. The rest is bookkeeping: which representations give solutions, and which one is trivial.

## The math

Let L = 2m, b = 10ᵐ and M = b² + 1 = 10ᴸ + 1.

**Step 1 (proved). The equation.** x·b + y = x² + y² is the same as (2x − b)² + (2y − 1)² = b² + 1 (multiply by 4 and complete both squares).

**Step 2 (proved). Solutions are representations.** Put v = |2x − b| (even, since b is even) and u = |2y − 1| (odd). Every solution gives M = u² + v² with u odd and v even. Conversely, take a representation M = u² + v² with u, v > 0, u odd and v even:

- since M is not a square, v ≠ 0, so v ≥ 2 and u < b;
- u = 1 forces v = b, which gives x ∈ {0, b} and the trivial N ∈ {0, 1};
- every other representation has 1 < u < b and 0 < v < b, and gives y = (u + 1)/2 and the two solutions x = (b ± v)/2, both strictly between 0 and b.

These two x form De Geest's pair: the same right half, and left halves summing to b.

So the number of pairs equals the number of representations M = u² + v² with u, v > 0, u odd, minus the trivial one (u, v) = (1, b).

**Step 3 (proved). Every prime factor of M is 1 (mod 4).** M is odd, and a prime p | b² + 1 has −1 ≡ b² a square mod p, so p ≡ 1 (mod 4).

**Step 4 (theorem: Jacobi). The count.** The number of ordered integer pairs (U, V) with U² + V² = M is r₂(M) = 4·Σ_(d | M) χ(d), where χ is the non-trivial character mod 4. By step 3, χ(d) = 1 for every divisor, so r₂(M) = 4·d(M). M is not a square, so no pair has a zero coordinate. Hence there are d(M) pairs with U, V > 0. Exactly one coordinate of each is odd (M is odd), and swapping the coordinates gives a bijection, so exactly d(M)/2 of these pairs have U odd. Removing the trivial one:

```
number of pairs of length L  =  d(10ᴸ + 1)/2 − 1
```

**Step 5 (proved). Consequences.**

- If 10ᴸ + 1 = p₁p₂…p_F is squarefree, then d = 2^F and the count is 2^(F−1) − 1, a Mersenne number. This is Nash's formula and De Geest's pattern. It matches all nine counts on the page (L = 2 to 18), and our brute force for L ≤ 12.
- There are solutions iff d(M) > 2, i.e. iff 10ᴸ + 1 is composite (Chilali's statement). 10ᴸ + 1 can be prime only when L is a power of 2, and only L = 2 (101) is known; so every other length has solutions.
- If 10ᴸ + 1 is not squarefree, then d(M) > 2^F and the count exceeds 2^(F−1) − 1. Nash's "at most 2^(F−1) − 1" should read "at least". Example: 17 ∥ 10⁸ + 1 (10⁸ + 1 = 17·5882353), so lifting the exponent in 10¹³⁶ + 1 = (10⁸)¹⁷ + 1 gives 17² ∥ 10¹³⁶ + 1. For L = 136 the count is 3·2^(F−2) − 1, not 2^(F−1) − 1. (We checked squarefreeness only for L ≤ 30, so we do not claim that 136 is the first such length.)

The counts continue 7, 7, 3, 3, 7, 63 for L = 20, 22, 24, 26, 28, 30 (10³⁰ + 1 has 7 prime factors).
