# Puzzle 1276: The most primes from k copies of each digit, proved optimal

> **In short:** Given k copies of each digit 0–9, how many distinct primes can be formed? The page lists values for 41 values of k, from k = 1 to k = 100000; none was proved best above the simple bound 4k + 2. The proof: (1) for any digit weights y_d ≥ 0, the number of primes is at most k·Σy_d plus a correction from the primes whose digits weigh less than 1; (2) with suitable weights every prime of 8 or more digits weighs at least 1, so a finite count gives a bound valid for all primes; (3) an integer program over the primes below 10⁷ reaches the floor of that bound with every digit used. This proves all 41 values optimal, of which three were too low: k = 20000, 30000, 40000 give 35169, 51519 and 67254 primes (page: 35168, 51515, 67251).

**Puzzle:** [primepuzzles.net](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_1276.htm) (a new visit to [puzzle 49](https://www.primepuzzles.net/puzzles/puzz_049.htm))

## The question

Given k copies of each decimal digit, form as many distinct primes as possible, each digit used at most once (puzzle 49 also asks to leave as few digits unused as possible).

- **Q1.** Confirm or improve the best quantities found.
- **Q2.** Does the fraction of the upper bound 4k + 2 tend to 0 or to a positive limit?

Every optimum is reached with all 10k digits used, which also settles puzzle 49's second request: zero unused digits for every k listed. Q2 was answered on the page: the fraction tends to 0 like a/ln(a·k), a = 10·ln 10. For comparison, a·k/ln(a·k) is 35315 at k = 20000 and 157178 at k = 100000, against the optima 35169 and 155645.

## How we found it

One contributor on the page already used integer programming (primes below 10⁵, grouped by their digit counts) for k ≤ 200. An integer program is only optimal among the primes it is given. To prove more we need a bound that covers every prime, of any length, and that comes from LP duality. Give each digit d a weight y_d. A prime "costs" the sum of the weights of its digits, and the total weight available is k·Σy_d. Primes that cost less than 1 are few, and they are counted one by one.

## The math

Let c(p) = (c₀, …, c₉) be the digit counts of p, and for a vector c let t_c be the number of primes with c(p) = c.

**Step 1 (proved). The bound.** Let S be any set of distinct primes using each digit at most k times, and let y_d ≥ 0. For each p, 1 ≤ y·c(p) + max(0, 1 − y·c(p)). Summing over S:

  |S| ≤ y·Σ_{p∈S} c(p) + Σ_{p∈S} max(0, 1 − y·c(p)) ≤ k·Σ_d y_d + Σ_c t_c · max(0, 1 − y·c).

The last step uses Σ_{p∈S} c(p) ≤ (k, …, k) and that S has at most t_c primes with vector c.

**Step 2 (proved). Long primes cost nothing.** A prime with at least D + 1 digits (D ≥ 1) ends in 1, 3, 7 or 9, starts with a nonzero digit and has at least D − 1 further digits. So y·c(p) ≥ 1 for all of them if

  min(y₁, y₃, y₇, y₉) + min(y₁, …, y₉) + (D − 1)·min(y₀, …, y₉) ≥ 1,

and then the correction term only involves the primes below 10^D, a finite count. (For k ≤ 6, y = 1 on the digits 1, 3, 7, 9 and 0 elsewhere gives the classical bound 4k + 2: only 2 and 5 cost less than 1.)

**Step 3 (computed). The weights.** For each k we take y from the dual linear program with the condition of step 2 as an extra constraint (D = 5 for k ≤ 200, 6 up to k = 1000, 7 beyond), rounded to simple fractions. Examples: y = ¼ on 0, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8 and ½ on 1, 3, 7, 9 for k = 7, which gives the bound 29.75; for k = 30000 the weights are 5/42 on 0, 6/42 on 2, 4, 5, 6, 8 and 8/42 on 1, 3, 7, 9, which gives 51519.62. Every bound is recomputed in exact fractions.

**Step 4 (computed). The solutions.** An integer program over the primes below 10^D, grouped by digit vector, with each digit used exactly k times (HiGHS, zero gap), finds a set of ⌊bound⌋ primes for all 41 values of k. Since the bound covers all primes, these are the maxima.

What the optimal sets look like: at k = 20000 they use every prime of up to 4 digits, 8359 of the 8363 five-digit primes and 25581 six-digit primes; at k = 30000 all primes of up to 5 digits, 40023 six-digit and 1904 seven-digit primes. The last few hundred digits must be shared out so that every digit count comes out at exactly k, and that is where the earlier solutions lost 1 to 4 primes.
