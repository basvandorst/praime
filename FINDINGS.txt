FINDINGS - results that surprised us
=====================================

Old records fell to old ideas
- Many records broke by plugging in a known object, without a new search. The largest known Mersenne prime gave the largest self prime (373) and a larger Smith number (307). Old twin-prime records gave 1,290,006 prime factors (450). The largest palindromic prime, raised to the 7th power, has 13 million digits and not one 6 (607).
- Moving a known polynomial so its middle sits in the middle of the range beat a record (597). Smaller values are prime more often.

One remainder does all the work
- Primes that are 17 mod 40 always have an even period of 1/p. One formula built on that beat 1230. The same formula then beat 1135 too, because every value ends in two odd digits.
- A prime pine with 10 rows was open since 2012. Make every entry 1 mod 3, 5, 7 and 11, and the small primes can no longer interfere (77).

The rules were looser than intended
- Floor and mod formulas can glue together blocks of primes in arithmetic progression. That took 120 primes to 570 (1099) and 65 to 130 (1097). With such rules, a formula can reproduce any list you choose.
- Half the prime gap stays small for a very long time. Euler's polynomial with that input stays prime for 1,319,943 consecutive n (1217).

Some questions answered themselves with a proof
- A prime is never followed by a base-2 pseudoprime: n + 1 even hands n a divisor (170).
- A palindrome with 2000 digits is always divisible by 11, so three "2000" questions have no answer (75).
- 2^n - 2^k + 1 is a square only for k = 3: Ramanujan's equation and a theorem of Beukers (490).

Some page claims needed a second look
- A listed run of 45 unlucky primes contained five lucky numbers. The real run was 26 (1209).
- A page claim of 51.6% is not a coincidence. 30030 = 2·3·5·7·11·13, and Hardy-Littlewood predicts 51.7% (1204).

New this session
- Rotondo's 109-prime "irregular" polynomial (1139) is really two quadratic forms of one discriminant, interleaved. We searched every monic case with D up to 2*10^9, and filtered cases up to 10^13: nothing beats it.
- His odd-looking "unless the result is 2" clause is essential. Without it the run stops after 34 values.
- "All known n with 2^n - 7 prime are composite" looks like a law but is a bias (1257). Small primes leave 8 classes mod 60, and 5 of them are multiples of 3 or 5. A heuristic expects 2 to 3 prime n by now, so finding none has a 6-13% chance.

Conjectures pages
- A conjectured primality test failed at a prime. Rotondo's triangle test (conjecture 115) is passed by every odd prime up to 3000, but the prime 3917 fails it. For a prime p the test only needs G(k) mod p^2, and a random model predicts about 2.5/p failures per prime. That is infinitely many, but very sparse.
- Every prime p = 1 mod 4 divides G((p-1)/2) = sum of j^((p-1)/2): the Legendre symbols cancel in pairs.
- Rotondo's "geometric accident" for 9 is a theorem: v_p(G(p^e)) = 3e - 1, and no other odd prime power passes.
- The shielded-primes sieve (conjecture 111) is Flory's 1939 dimer model in disguise. The 1/e^2 is a theorem for random gaps, and on 10^7 consecutive primes near 10^15 the density matches e^-2 within noise.
- Rochelli's ln 3 (conjecture 99) is the integral of du/(u(1-u)) over [1/4, 1/2]. His low value at 10^9 is Mertens' remainder at p ~ n^(1/4), predicted to 0.0006.
- A claim "checked until 10^6" (conjecture 55, Sand's p^2 + q + d) fails at 305. Almost every n = 5 mod 6 is a counterexample.
- "Why 25?" (conjecture 80) is a prime race: primes ending in 3 or 7 add factors 5, primes ending in 1 remove them, net +5/16 per prime. An explicit 2018 bound covers x >= 8e9, and a 12-second sieve covers the rest: proved for all n.
- The Lucas-Lehmer pattern for 2^k + 3 (conjecture 112) is Fermat's little theorem in Z[sqrt 3]. The parity of k only decides whether sqrt 3 exists mod N.

What we learned about the method
- Asking "why does the old record work?" helped more than computing power. Most wins came from a short argument and a small search.
- Many results were a trick from another puzzle, reused. The same prime blocks served 1097, 1099 and 1028.
- Several claimed records did not survive an independent re-check. Re-checking is not optional.

From the facts pass (FACTS.md)
- Forward and mirrored Euler-Rodriguez chains (gaps 2, 4, 6, ... versus ..., 6, 4, 2) are equally common: below 10^10 there are 43,938 and 44,418 of length 5, and 109 and 118 of length 8. Yet the first mirrored 12-chain (27797667517) comes 170 times before the first forward one. First occurrences are luck; counts are not.
- Below 1.4e11 the longest run of consecutive primes on a polynomial of degree <= 4 is 12, on a quadratic: 27797667649 - x(x+1), x = 0..11. Cubics and quartics never fit a longer run than quadratics.
- The small primes are unusually dense: 17..73 (15 primes) and 37..113 (19 primes) have the least possible width of an admissible tuple, while for 11 to 14 primes no cluster below 1.4e11 reaches the minimum.
- Dusart's interval (x, x(1 + 1/(2 ln^2 x))] is sharp at its threshold: it fails at x = 3274 and holds from 3275 on.
From the second facts pass (October 2026)
- Below 2e8 the class of 1 never leads a prime race mod 8 (from x = 23) or mod 12 (from x = 463): squares of primes all land in that class. For last digits, primes ending in 1 or 9 are ahead of those ending in 3 or 7 only 1 to 3 % of the time.
- Cunningham chains need p = 29 (mod 30) (first kind) or p = 1 (mod 30) (second kind) once they have 4 primes above 5: x -> 2x +- 1 has one fixed class mod 3 and mod 5, and every other class reaches 0. All least starts from 89 to 85864769 obey this.
- The least prime in a Goldbach partition of n <= 2e8 never exceeds 1321 (n = 187852862); it grows like a constant times ln^2 n.
- Robin's inequality sigma(n) < e^gamma n ln ln n fails for exactly 26 values n >= 3, the last 5040, and holds on (5040, 1e6].

From the puzzle pass (30 October 2026)
- sigma(2^m - 1) = 3*2^(m-1) only for m = 4 and 10. Zsigmondy forces m = p^2 or pq, and a congruence mod 2^(p+1) kills all but two. This ends Firoozbakht's family in puzzle 489 (she had "none below 1279"), and with one inequality it gives every solution of sigma(sigma(phi(n))) = n with n = 2^a 3^b.
- "All n up to X" can be cheap when the condition is multiplicative: for sigma(n) = q^k every prime-power part has sigma a power of q, and odd parts need even exponents, so only p <= sqrt(X) matter. That took puzzle 542 from 10^10 to 10^18 in 3 minutes: only 81 and 400.
- Puzzle 882: below 2^64 there is exactly one run of 11 primes A(Z)_iB with one-digit Z and B of at most 4 digits, 95773717(6)_i1. The next decade of A has four runs of 10 and none of 11. Primes with small ord_q(10) (11, 37, 101, 41, 271) make these digit chains far more prime-rich than random tuples.
- Rotondo's "reverse rules" for 1/p (1238) are Marsaglia-Zaman add-with-carry generators: a(n-J) + a(n-N) with carry gives the reversed period exactly when p | 10^N + 10^J - 1, with no carry condition at all. Forward and reverse lags are negatives of each other mod ord_p(10). Below 1000, only 11 primes (11, 37, 41, 73, 101, 137, 239, 271, 281, 641, 859) have no two-lag rule in either direction.
- Puzzle 868: 3x3 magic squares of palprimes went from 31 to 101 digits with no new idea beyond Rivera's own structure. Making each digit position a magic square of digits avoids carries, and a 10^8-pattern bitset plus a center search found 5 squares in 20 minutes. The expected count, 10^m 25^m / 8 times (2.5 / ln 10^L)^9, matched the runs.
From the shared-component pass (30 October 2026; scratch code outside the repo, not yet in writeup/code)
- The missing link between Mertens (local, e^-gamma) and the prime number theorem (global) is Buchstab's omega: sieving by q <= y = x^(1/v) leaves e^gamma*omega(v) times the Mertens product. A model "exact sieve to y, random above" has count ratio e^gamma*omega(v) to the real primes, matched to 3-4 digits at 1e10 (y = 1e5: 0.8905 vs 0.8910, the Mertens paradox). FACTS.md has no Buchstab or Dickman entry yet.
- That hybrid model with y = x^0.405 (where e^gamma*omega(v) = 1) reproduces the real gap tail at 1e8, 1e10, 1e12, and the maximal gap below 1e9: 290 +- 9 over 8 seeds against the real 282 (Cramer: 359). Cramer overestimates gaps >= 8 ln x by a factor 2.25 at 1e10.
- The prime density C of n^2 + n + A is the class number formula in disguise: corr(log C, log(sqrt|D|/h(D))) = 0.988 over 486 odd A. Our puzzle-597 polynomial (A = 247757) is the C record below |D| = 1e6.
- Euler-Rodriguez chains: a parameter-free Hardy-Littlewood count (singular series, exp(-sum of S-ratios/ln t) for the empty slots, non-extension) matches the counts of fact 4010 (k = 5: 362270 vs 358682) and gives expected counts 1.04 and 0.28 at Andersen's first 13- and 14-chains.
- Fortunate numbers: (F_n - p_n)/theta(p_n) looks exponential with mean e^-gamma: 0.587 (n <= 300), 0.571 (n <= 400).
- Strings of consecutive primes in one class are anti-persistent: below 1e9 runs >= 16 mod 3 occur 18 times, 75 expected from the measured repeat rate 0.445.
- Covering windows of 20 consecutive primes with quadratics (m^2 - D)/4, m^2 - D (|D| <= 1e6): for 53..149, 138 forms hit >= 4, the best 11 (D = 437), and 3 forms cover all 20. But 20 random odd numbers from the same window coprime to 3*5*7 do as well (best 9-11, 3-7 forms). The good forms are exactly those with D a non-residue mod 3, 5, 7, 11, 13: combining them gives back the sieve, not a new prime formula.
- Predicting the 21st prime from 20 consecutive primes (20000 windows each near 1e4, 1e6, 1e9, 1e12, no primality test): trend formulas (mean gap, p + ln p, last gap, line, parabola, cubic) score 4-19 %, worse than "smallest step allowed mod 6" (9-27 %); a vote of the trend formulas is worse than its best member. The best predictor is the sieve, "first number after p with no factor <= y": success = e^gamma ln y / ln x (Mertens), e.g. y = 1000 at 1e9: 58.2 % predicted, 58.2 % measured. The previous 20 primes carry almost no information beyond the last prime's residues.
- Logic inside the sequence (predict the next gap from earlier gaps only; learn on primes in [1e9, 1.1e9), test on [1.1e9, 1.2e9)): the last 3 gaps raise exact hits from 11.3 % to 14.8 %. But p mod 30 alone gives 16.9 %, p mod 210 20.2 %, p mod 2310 22.5 %, and adding past gaps to p mod 210 adds nothing (20.2 -> 20.2 / 19.8 %). All predictive content of the past gaps is the position of p on the wheel of the small primes.
- A "dynamic wheel" (one counter per prime, no division; a counter becomes active when n reaches p^2; n is prime when no active counter is at 0) lists every prime: 664579 below 1e7 with 446 counters, sum checked against sympy. With only the counters up to y = x^(1/v), the first number after p with no active counter at 0 is the next prime with chance 1/(v*omega(v)) (Buchstab): near 1e9, v = 2.25 / 3 / 4 / 6 measured 81.5 / 59.3 / 45.3 / 30.6 %, predicted 81.8 / 59.1 / 44.5 / 29.7 %.
- Backtest of 100 next-prime rules (20 gap rules x sight 0/7/13/100/1000; 20000 predictions each from 1e6, 1e9, 1e12; learned tables trained only on the 1e5+ primes before the start): at every effective sight the winner is "first number after p with no factor <= y" (1e9: y = 7/13/100/1000 give 20.4/24.5/39.7/59.1 %). Learned "most frequent gap per p mod 30/210/2310" reproduces the wheel exactly (16.8 = 16.8 %, 20.4 = 20.4 %) or does slightly worse; rules from past gaps (last gap, mean, median, mode, last gap + 2) lose 30-60 % of the hits. Own-power runs near 1e9 average 1.1-2.8 primes, max 16. Code: writeup/code/facts_gaprules.c (usage: X T N).
- Look-back gap formulas near 1e9 (1715 linear rules a1 g1 + a2 g2 + a3 g3 + c with a in {-2,-1,-1/2,0,1/2,1,2}, c in {-4..4}, plus 28 exotic ones: 2g2 - g1, parabola, Fibonacci g1 + g2, |g1 - g2|, g1 g2 mod 30, periods, ...; train below 1e9, test above): with a sieve to y = 7/13/100/1000 none beats "first survivor", and the training winner is a rule that always goes negative, i.e. clamps to the first survivor itself. 2g2 - g1 keeps 57 % of the sieve hits (33.6 vs 59.2 % at y = 1000). With no sieve the best is 6 - (g1 mod 6) at 11.5 %, below the plain wheel of 6 (12.7 %): past gaps only leak part of p mod 6. Code: writeup/code/facts_gaprules_exotic.py.
- One explicit law for the next prime (31 Oct 2026 night run): the next prime after p is the J-th survivor of the sieve by q <= y, with J geometric, P(J = j) = rho (1 - rho)^(j-1), rho = 1/(ln x prod_{q<=y}(1 - 1/q) e^gamma omega(ln x/ln y)). Near 1e9, 1e12 and 1e15 (y = 100..10000) every P(J = j), j <= 6, is within 0.3-0.6 % and 1/E[J] equals rho to 3-4 digits; sampled runs of probable primes near 1e18, 1e24 and 1e30 agree within noise (e.g. 1e30, y = 1000: rho 0.1788, 1/E[J] 0.1790). The same "exact sieve + coin" model reproduces, within noise: the gap histogram (chi2/bin ~1 at y ~ x^(1/4)), the whole Lemke Oliver-Soundararajan 10x10 digit table (16 cells within 0.6 %, including 1->1 and 9->9 more common than 3->3 and 7->7), gap memory (corr(g1,g2) -0.029, equal consecutive gaps half as common as for shuffled gaps, 8 rising gaps 2.4x more common), counts of gaps >= G (below 4e9 up to G = 200, Cramer overcounting 1.8x-13x; on [1e10, 1e11) up to G = 400 within ~1 %), Maier's oscillation after r * 23# (0.30 / 1.17 measured, 0.31 / 1.16 predicted) and the sub-Poisson law of short intervals (P(no prime in 4 ln x) 0.0089 vs Poisson 0.0184 at 1e9).
- The past is the sieve: the last K gaps fix p mod q (p - p_(-k) is never 0 mod q), so K gaps of memory act as a sieve to y with y ln y ~ K (K = 100 -> 31, 1e3 -> ~200, 1e4 -> ~1400, 5e4 -> ~6000, the same at 1e9 and 1e12). Primes cover the classes mod q faster than random draws (q = 101, K = 500: 0.78 vs 0.51) because consecutive primes almost never share a class mod q (needs q | gap).
- Hardy-Littlewood explains the middle scales: var/mean of prime counts in windows H = 10..1e6 near 1e9 follows 1 - 1/ln x + (2/(H ln x)) sum_{h<H} (H - h)(S(h) - 1) within noise (aligned windows of 10 differ: 0.857 vs 0.823 over all shifts). The Goldbach comet r(N), N <= 4e6, follows 2 C2 prod (p-1)/(p-2) int dt/(ln t ln(N-t)) with sd 0.7 % (noise 0.9 %; 58 % without the factor).
- The zeros explain the global layer: Chebyshev's bias E(x) = (ln x/sqrt x)(pi(x;4,3) - pi(x;4,1)) up to 2e9 follows (ln x/sqrt x)(sum_{k>=2} pi(x^(1/k), chi^k)/k - log L(0, chi) + 2 Re sum li(x^rho)) with corr 0.98 using the 289 zeros of L(s, chi_4) below 400 (computed with mpmath); the rms error 0.076 is the truncation error (theory 0.066), and the mean residual is +0.0015 (mod 4) and +0.0045 (mod 3), within noise. Without the prime powers k >= 4 and the constant -log L(0, chi) = log 2 (mod 4), log 3 (mod 3) a residual of +0.023 remains. From 5000 zeta zeros, Montgomery's form factor follows alpha for alpha < 1 and averages 1.01 for 1.2 <= alpha <= 2.5, the value Hardy-Littlewood's pair conjecture gives. The same calibrated wheel model puts the jumping-champion switches 6 -> 30 at 10^35.6 and 30 -> 210 at 10^427 (Odlyzko-Rubinstein-Wolf: 1.7e35 and ~1e425).
- Bits: near 1e9 the next gap carries H = 4.664 bits; knowing p mod 6 / 30 / 210 / 2310 / 30030 leaves 4.112 / 3.769 / 3.524 / 3.369 / 3.232 bits, and the geometric law predicts h(rho)/rho = 4.125 / 3.774 / 3.526 / 3.371 / 3.239 (h = binary entropy). A sieve to 1000 leaves 1.65 bits, to sqrt x none: the randomness of the next prime is exactly what the primes between y and sqrt x still have to decide.

- What is proved and what is not, for the gap rule "J-th survivor of the sieve to y = x^0.405, J geometric":
  - The sieve layer is plain arithmetic (proved).
  - The coin's mean rho is a theorem. Buchstab: Phi(x, y) ~ x*omega(u)/ln y. With pi(x) ~ x/ln x this makes primes/survivors equal to rho on average, and c = 0.405 is where e^gamma*omega(1/c) = 1 (FACTS 2014-2016 and the PNT).
  - The independence of the coins is the Hardy-Littlewood conjecture. It gives Poisson statistics by Gallagher (FACTS 3016).
  - That independence is exactly the information the parity problem (FACTS 2017) keeps out of reach of every sieve. The coin decides "prime" against "product of two primes above y", and a sieve cannot see that difference.
  So the explanation splits cleanly: everything the sieve can see is proved and gives the whole structure of the gaps, while the unproved remainder is a fair coin, measured as independent to 1e-3 from 1e9 to 1e18. Proving it means breaking the parity barrier: Friedlander-Iwaniec and Maynard-Tao do that only in special settings (FACTS 5006, 3008).

  (1) c = 0.5615 is trivial: y = x^0.56 > sqrt x, so the survivors are exactly the primes and rho = 1. The crossings that carry information lie below 1/2, and c = 0.4027 is the largest of them, the deepest sieve whose coin is unbiased. That rule selects c from theory, without fitting.

That is the Hardy-Littlewood / Montgomery-Soundararajan layer (sum over pairs of the singular series), which takes over exactly where the sieve's reach y ends. Gaps live at scale ln x << y, which is why the local rule is exact for gaps and record gaps.
- Layer 2 as periodicity (writeup/code/facts_periodic_model.py). Every prime q <= sqrt x deletes one random residue class, and a coin rho_C = 1/(ln x * prod_{q<=sqrt x}(1-1/q)) -> e^gamma/2 = 0.8905 corrects Mertens' paradox (the sieve to sqrt x leaves 2e^-gamma = 1.12 times too many survivors). This model has no choice of c at all.
  - Gap tails, model/real on [1e8, 4e9]: 0.976, 0.979, 1.085 at G = 100, 200, 240.
  - Maximal gap below 4e18: P(max < 1476) = 0.43, against 0.45 for the c-model. P(gap >= 1854 below 1.1e20) = 0.05.
  - Var/mean of window counts near 1e9 at H = 1e2-1e5: 0.711, 0.598, 0.492, 0.379 against real 0.714, 0.600, 0.492, 0.401 (the coin model had 0.426 at 1e5).
  - At H = 1e6 (100 windows, about +-15 % noise) the model gives 0.26-0.32 over three seeds, against real 0.226. Its sieve part alone is 0.215 (seed 7), and the excess is exactly the coin's binomial term 1 - rho_C = 0.105.
  So the rigidity of prime counts at long range is periodic deletion: a prime q removes every q-th number, not a binomial sample. The one remaining non-random piece is the Mertens/Buchstab thinning by 0.89, which real primes do rigidly (it is the parity/"products of two large primes" correction) and no independent coin can mimic.
- The Mertens thinning: random locally, rigid only in a logarithmic way (writeup/code/facts_rigid_thinning.py, near 1e9). The 0.89 coin of the periodic model was replaced by an exact quota per block of B numbers (keep round(rho*n) of the n survivors, chosen at random).
  - The gap law does not change: chi2 is 126-139 on 59 bins (g = 2..118) for B = 0 (independent coin), 1e3, 1e4 and 1e5, with P(g >= 100) between 0.00408 and 0.00428 against real 0.00427.
  - The variance does change, as a step at scale B. With B = 1e5, var/mean is 0.476 / 0.293 / 0.208 at H = 1e4 / 1e5 / 1e6. Real primes decline smoothly and logarithmically instead: 0.492 / 0.401 / 0.226, the Montgomery-Soundararajan form 1 - ln H/ln x - const, i.e. the zeros' pair correlation.
  Conclusion: the gaps (all scales up to record gaps ~ ln^2 x) are fixed by periodic deletion up to sqrt x plus a locally independent thinning by e^gamma/2. What is still not random-model-like acts only on counts over long windows, and its form is logarithmic, the GUE/zeros layer. It does not affect gap statistics.
- Puzzle 1064: every prime factor of gcd(2^p+1, 3^p+1) is = 1 (mod 6p) (proved; conjectured by three contributors). The most common factor 6p+1 needs p = 3 (mod 4), so 88 % of composite gcds should have p = 3 (mod 4): all 9 known do.
- Puzzle 9: the model 'S(k) is a square with chance 1/(2 sqrt S(k))' with S(k) ~ (k^2/2) ln k gives sqrt(2 ln K) squares up to K. It predicts 6.1 for k <= 1.27e11, and 6 are known (Resta's included). Residues of S(k) mod 8, 3, 5, 7 carry no bias (E[rho_q] = 1.000 +- 0.003). Cubes converge: about 0.84 expected over all k, almost all at tiny k, 4e-4 beyond k = 1.9e8.
- Puzzle 1022: x(n) = (n-1)^n + n. Modulo n + 1 it is (-2)^n - 1, and modulo 2n - 1, 2^n x(n) is the Cullen number C_n (even n) or the Woodall number W_n (odd n). So (n+1) | x(n) iff (-2)^n = 1 and (2n-1) | x(n) iff (-2)^(n-1) = 1: base -2 probable-prime tests, including the pseudoprimes 341, 561, ... These are the only linear divisor families (proof via r | D(j,i,k)), and n = 1 (mod r) gives x = 1 (mod r), so no covering set exists. With n^2 - n + 1 (n = 2 mod 3) the three families explain 54.5 % of n <= 13389. A sieve model calibrated on OEIS A160360 and A108879 (4.1 expected, 6 observed) expects 1.5 primes for 30 <= n <= 13389 (none: 22 %), and the next one near n ~ 6e5. n = 3 (mod 4) is the richest class: no family and not 5 can divide there.
