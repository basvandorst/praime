Puzzle 1251
GPD(P+Nextprime(P)+1) A. On Dec.15, 2025 Alain Rochelli sent the following curio, similar to the posted in the Puzzles 1249 & 1250:

While searching the internet for Puzzle 1249 and 1250 I had the following idea :

Now, the P+Nextprime(P)+1 problem is as follows: start with any prime P (>=5) and consider the greatest prime divisor of P+Nextprime(P)+1, denoted A(P) = GPD(P+Nextprime(P)+1).

Then, repeat the operation: A(A(P)), A(A(A(P))) … enough to fall into a cycle.

After computations carried out with the first primes up to 10^7, it is conjectured that all primes (>= 5) fall into a cycle included 13 as smallest term or a cycle included 17 as smallest term *:

a) Cycle “13”: 31, 23, 53, 113, 241, 29, 61, 43, 13 (9 terms)

b) Cycle “17”: 37, 79, 163, 331, 223, 41, 17 (7 terms)

Q1. Are there more cycles other than the two mentioned above (13 & 17)?

* This conjecture is false, as Oscar Volpatti demonstrated in his contributions. See below.

Q2. Do you devise a way to produce a formal proof that there are only these two cycles, or this purpose is imposible to get?

** Maybe it’s interesting to see Back and Caragiu’s paper)? See https://www.fq.math.ca/Papers1/48-4/Back_Caragiu.pdf

B. On Dec. 19, 2025, Emmanuel Vantieghem wrote:

Maybe we should search for iterations of GPD(a*p + b) that have at least three cycles (in a "reasonable" range, say below 10^9).

Q3. What a, b can give 4 cycles, 5, 6 ... ?

From 19-26 Dec., 2025, contributions came from Gennady Gusev, Emmanuel Vantieghem, Simon Cavegn, Oscar Volpatti

For compactness, given a prime P, define the following numbers:

Q = Nextprime(P);

X = P+Q+1;

Y = GPD(X) = A(P).

Let's check the claimed exception:

Y = 3.

Hence, from starting prime P = 102945566047297, the cycle "3" mentioned by Emmanuel Vantieghem is reached:

102945566047297, 3, 3, 3...

I found such exception not by exaustive search, but by solving equation A(P) = 3.

Assuming P > 2, then P and Q must be odd and X must be an odd composite whose largest prime factor is 3.

Therefore X = 3^K and P+Q = 3^K-1, for some positive exponent K.

But Q > P, so Q-P = 2*S for some positive integer S.

Solving for P and Q:

P = (3^K-1)/2 - S,

Q = (3^K-1)/2 + S.

Search strategy.

Consider each positive exponent K in ascending order.

For given K, verify that integer M = (3^K-1)/2 is composite.

If so, consider each positive integer S in ascending order and check the pair (M-S,M+S); stop as soon as current pair contains at least one prime; if it actually contains two primes, we have a solution P = M-S, Q = M+S = Nextprime(P).
