# Mersenne exponents: a rule that lands only on them

> **In short:** The Mersenne exponents are the primes p with 2^p − 1 prime: 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, … Asked: a rule that steps from one to the next and touches nothing else. 1. Trial factors 2kq + 1 remove about half of the primes, but among the rest the share of exponents grows only like the logarithm of the factor bound: 1.8% → 4.1% below 10⁴, as the Wagstaff heuristic predicts (23.7 expected, 22 found). 2. In 110 tests, no arithmetic feature of the 51 exponents below 83,880,077 beats that heuristic; taken as plain integers, no step rule, formula, recurrence or digit pattern beats random sequences of the same growth, and the one integer congruence that hits them all (A366029) is a probable-prime test of 2^k − 1. About 2,000 more ideas from outside the known formulas (section 7), each chosen on the small exponents and tested on the large ones and on 401 exponents of 79 related families, find nothing either. 3. So each step must prove its prime: the next q whose 2^q − 1 survives trial factoring and passes Lucas–Lehmer. The page has it as transformation 142, as a list without a circle, and as a test of the known exponents alone.

**Page:** [prime.html](writeup/code/riemann/prime.html): transformation 142 "Next Mersenne exponent" (indicator "Mersenne exponents" counts the hits), and the panel "Mersenne exponents, one after another" at the bottom (Start, and "Only the known exponents"). [prime2.html](writeup/code/mersenne/prime2.html) shows the exponents as plain numbers in 54 layouts (section 8 explains every pattern in the first 43; the 11 wave layouts carry their own checked notes) (square, spirals, trees, triangles, space-filling curves, rings, walks), each with a note on why its empty lines are empty, with twin primes, chains of primes in order, a line test that counts the exponents on any lattice line against the heuristic, and the forecasts of section 7.

**Code:** [code/mersenne/](writeup/code/mersenne/), rerun by [run_all.sh](writeup/code/mersenne/run_all.sh).

**Status:** the algorithm is proved (Lucas–Lehmer is a theorem; trial factors are exact). Hit rates, feature tests and timings are computed. The probability model is heuristic.

## 1. The question

132 (Golomb's formula) gives the next prime, and from 1 its chain hits the exponents 2, 3, 5, 7 and then misses 11, 23 and 29: 7 of its first 10 steps are exponents. The aim is 100%: every step a Mersenne exponent, none skipped.

## 2. A filter is not enough (computed; heuristic model)

Every factor of 2^q − 1 has the form 2kq + 1 with 2kq + 1 ≡ ±1 (mod 8) (theorem). Removing the primes with such a factor for k ≤ K ([tf.py](writeup/code/mersenne/tf.py), [rate.py](writeup/code/mersenne/rate.py), output [rate.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/rate.txt); deeper K from [touch/tftable.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/tftable.out)):

| K | primes < 10⁴ left | share that are exponents (22) | primes < 10⁵ left | share (28) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1,229 | 1.8% | 9,592 | 0.29% |
| 100 | 805 | 2.7% | 6,781 | 0.41% |
| 10⁴ | 596 | 3.7% | 5,211 | 0.54% |
| 10⁵ | 532 | 4.1% | 4,665 | 0.60% |
| 10⁷ | 428 | 5.1% | | |

The heuristic P(2^q − 1 prime | no factor below Q) ≈ e^γ·log₂(Q)/q predicts 23.7 exponents below 10⁴ (true 22) and 30.0 below 10⁵ (true 28). The share grows like log Q: reaching 50% needs no factor below about 2^(0.28·q), which is the work of a primality proof ([touch/bound.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/bound.out)).

## 3. No hidden structure (computed)

All 51 exponents, against all 4,882,467 primes below 83,880,077 (GIMPS: every exponent below it tested and verified, milestone page read on 15 November 2026). The null is P = e^γ·log₂(a·p)/p, a = 2 for p ≡ 3 and a = 6 for p ≡ 1 (mod 4). Output in [structure/results.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/structure/results.txt), from [analysis.py](writeup/code/mersenne/structure/analysis.py); an independent recheck (gmpy2 primes, plain integers, its own Poisson-binomial code) in [check.py](writeup/code/mersenne/structure/check.py) → [check.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/structure/check.txt) reproduces every count, expectation and p-value.

- Calibration: 51 observed, 50.3 expected (a = 2/6), 47.6 (a = 2). By mod 4: 31 with p ≡ 1 (25.2 expected), 19 with p ≡ 3 (24.1). The data lean towards a = 6 (Bayes factor 1.9) without deciding it.
- 110 tests: p mod 3, 5, 7, 8, 12, 16, 24, 30, 60, 120; the forms 2^k ± 1 and 4^k ± 3; closeness to a power of 2; binary weight and runs; 2p + 1, p + 2, p − 2 prime; the spacing of log log p. Bonferroni threshold 4.5·10⁻⁴; the smallest p-value is 0.023, and 49% of simulated null sets have a smaller one.
- That smallest p-value belongs to a control that is a theorem: p ≡ 3 (mod 4) with 2p + 1 prime (p > 3) never gives an exponent (Euler: 2p + 1 divides 2^p − 1); 0 found, 3.74 expected. A class with no exponents at all becomes visible only once it would hold more than about 8 of them.
- The one feature found in the exponents below 10⁵ (2^k ± 1 or 4^k ± 3: 10 against 7.0) lies entirely at p ≤ 127 and has nothing to test above 10⁵.

## 4. How few tests per exponent (computed)

[touch/](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/): trial factoring to 10⁷ ([tfdeep.py](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/tfdeep.py)), P−1 run for real with gmpy2 ([pm1.py](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/pm1.py), tables [pm1table_1e4.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/pm1table_1e4.out), [pm1table_1e5.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/pm1table_1e5.out)), orderings ([order.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/order.out)).

- Best share of exponents among the Lucas–Lehmer tests: 22 of 350 (6.3%) below 10⁴ (trial factoring to 10⁷ plus P−1 to B1 = 10⁵), 28 of 3,931 (0.71%) below 10⁵.
- P−1 (B1 = 1000, B2 = 3000) costs more than the tests it saves below about 4·10⁴, and 0.42 and 0.30 Lucas–Lehmer tests per removal in [4·10⁴, 7·10⁴) and [7·10⁴, 10⁵).
- Ordering the candidates by their posterior probability is the ascending order (0 positions differ); ordering by the prior with the mod-4 factor finds the first 25 exponents in 895 tests instead of 946 but needs 3,557 instead of 3,392 for all 28.
- The only proofs that 2^q − 1 is composite are a factor or a full Lucas–Lehmer (or PRP) test of about q squarings. A base-2 Fermat test never rejects 2^q − 1 (theorem: 2^q ≡ 1 mod 2^q − 1 and q divides 2^q − 2). So a rule that touches only exponents would need a test nobody has; a complete search tests every prime that survives the factors.

## 5. The algorithm on the page (proved; times measured)

Next exponent after p: the next prime q with no factor 2kq + 1 for k ≤ 100,000·(q/7000)² (at least 1,000; sieved by the primes below 2^14, exact double arithmetic below 2^38), and Lucas–Lehmer s₀ = 4, s ← s² − 2 mod 2^q − 1, s_(q−2) = 0. Below q = 4,200 in BigInt; above, the squaring is an irrational-base FFT (Crandall–Fagin, as in GIMPS) in Float64Array, with the largest rounding error checked at every step (a step above 0.35 is redone at twice the length). Every zero found up to 50,000 (all of them in transformation 142 and in the list) is recomputed in exact BigInt. The readable source is [engine/best.js](writeup/code/mersenne/engine/best.js); the page holds the same functions without comments.

## 6. The exponents as plain numbers (computed; A366029 proved)

The same question with the exponents as integers only: 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, … as a sequence, without "prime" and without 2^p − 1. Two searches, each run identically on 250 to 500 random sequences that grow the same way (null A: points spread at random in log a; null B: random primes taken with the heuristic probability), with a family-wise correction and with every rule chosen on a₁ … a₂₅ and then counted on a₂₆ … a₅₂:

- **Step rules aₙ₊₁ = f(aₙ)** ([numbers/](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/), [report.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/report.txt)): 745,984 maps, compositions to depth 3 of 1,187 primitives (x + c, cx + d, ⌊x·r⌋, powers, 2^x ± c, bit and decimal operations, "the next y > x" in a residue class, palindromic, of a given binary weight or of the form 2^k ± c); the same grammar backwards; 16,884 two-term rules. The best, "(the second number of the form 2^k ± 1 above x) − 2", reproduces 7 of the 51 steps (2→3, 3→5, 5→7, 7→13, 19→31, 31→61, 107→127; all below 128); the null medians are 6 (p = 0.48 and 0.43). No rule chosen on the first 25 terms predicts any later term. Closed forms ([formulas.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/formulas.txt)): ⌊1.45717·1.50872ⁿ⌋ hits 8 terms (p = 0.31); a formula that reproduces all 52 needs at least the 804 bits of the list itself (a Mills-type constant restates the list).
- **Relations and patterns** ([relations/](writeup/code/mersenne/relations/), all 252 statistics in [combined.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/relations/combined.txt)): linear recurrences (Berlekamp–Massey over Q: the generic order ⌈m/2⌉ for every m from 8 to 52; integer recurrences with |c| ≤ 10 run for at most 4 terms), relations a_k = c₁aᵢ + c₂aⱼ + d with small coefficients (225 at d = 0, e.g. 19937 = 5·127 + 6·3217, against a null median of 274), the binary strings (Lempel–Ziv complexity, minimal automata, common substrings), the digits in bases 2 to 36, the growth (0.531 bits per term; the gaps behave like those of a Poisson process). The smallest raw p-value is 0.004 (base 23: two equal neighbouring digits in 12 of the 45 terms with at least two digits), 0.19 after the family-wise correction, and it fails out of sample.
- **The OEIS** ([oeis.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/oeis.txt)): the longest agreement is A366029, defined on the integer k alone: k > 1 with (k − 1)^(2^k − 2) ≡ 1 (mod (2^k − 1)·k²). It is 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 61, 89, 107, 127, then 257 instead of 521.
  - It contains every Mersenne exponent (proved): if 2^p − 1 is prime, Fermat gives (p − 1)^(2^p − 2) ≡ 1 mod 2^p − 1; mod p², (p − 1)^N ≡ 1 − N·p for even N, which is 1 because p divides N = 2^p − 2; and gcd(2^p − 1, p²) = 1.
  - The same expansion shows that the congruence is two Fermat tests (proved): 2^k ≡ 2 (mod k) and (k − 1)^(2^k − 2) ≡ 1 (mod 2^k − 1). [a366029.py](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/a366029.py) → [a366029.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/a366029.txt) checks both forms against each other for every k ≤ 4,500: they agree, and the terms are the 20 exponents up to 4,423 and 257.
  - The extras (proved): when k − 1 = 2^m the base is a power of 2, and since 2 has order k modulo 2^k − 1, (2^m)^(2^k − 2) ≡ 1 holds whenever k divides 2^k − 2. So every k = 2^m + 1 with 2^k ≡ 2 (mod k) is a term: all Fermat numbers 2^(2^n) + 1, prime or not (257, 65537, 4294967297, …), of which 3, 5 and 17 happen to be exponents.
  - So the one integer rule that lands on every exponent is a probable-prime test of 2^k − 1 under another name: k squarings of a k-bit number, the cost of Lucas–Lehmer.
  - On the page it is transformation 143 (next k > p in A366029, up to 150,000), with the source mode "every number on the circle": the chain from 1 runs 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 61, 89, 107, 127, 257, 521, 607, … (14 of its first 15 steps land on exponents; the miss is 127 → 257). The page decides each k exactly: 2^k ≡ 2 (mod k) first; then the congruence modulo a small factor of 2^k − 1 (for composite k, modulo 2^d − 1 with d the smallest factor of k), which refutes most k; k = 2^m + 1 by the proof above; the rest by the FFT of section 5 with an exact digit multiplication by k − 1, and every term found again in BigInt. Checked in Node on the page's own functions: equal to the definition for every k from 2 to 2,000, no base-2 pseudoprime among 25 (341 … 11305) passes, the FFT path equals exact BigInt on 18 values of k from 4,201 to 11,939, and the chain from 1 to 12,000 is the 23 exponents and 257; 65537 is a term, 65539 is not.

In the integer view as well, nothing beyond the growth law: whatever lands on every exponent tests 2^k − 1.

## 7. Outside the known formulas: rounds 3 to 5 (computed; the null is heuristic)

Ideas from anywhere, judged by one fixed referee. Background: all 4,882,467 primes below B = 83,880,077 with the heuristic rate w(p) = e^γ·log₂(a·p)/p (a = 6 for p ≡ 1, a = 2 for p ≡ 3 mod 4). - The most persistent near-miss, k ≡ 1 (mod 7) (13 of 51 against 7.4 expected; 4 of the 13 in the final range against 0.8), shows no effect at all in the 79 other families, where the holdout alone could detect a rate of 1.4 to 1.6. The 52nd exponent, 136,279,841 (beyond the verified bound, so used by no round), is ≡ 5 (mod 7).
- Inside a Lucas–Lehmer run nothing is recognisable before s_(k−3) ≡ ±2^((k+1)/2) (proved; checked on every exponent from 3 to 19,937). For a prime 2^k − 1, s_i is the trace of an element of order 2^(k−i); only orders up to 8 have closed-form traces, and going back one step costs a square root mod 2^k − 1, itself about k squarings. The Jacobi symbols (s_i ± 2 | 2^k − 1) are the same for prime and composite 2^k − 1 (from i = 1 on; checked on 1,184 checkpoints).
- The final range [10⁷, B) holds 13 exponents against 5.86 expected (round 1: p 0.015 for that range alone, 0.12 after its 8 ranges), so the fair final-exam test is conditional on the 13; both rounds 3b and 3c report it that way.
- The forecast (round 6b; heuristic): with w as above, the expected number of exponents between x and y is e^γ·[log₂(y/x) + log₂√12 · ln(ln y / ln x)] (the integral over the primes, within 1% of the exact prime sums from 10³ on; checked up to 10⁹). From no. 51, 82,589,933, the median forecast for the next exponent was 106.3 million (50%: 91.7–136.9 million); no. 52, 136,279,841, came at u = 0.75. GIMPS has tested every exponent below 142,869,329 at least once (milestones page, 15 November 2026), so no. 53 is forecast at a median of 184.0 million (50%: 158.7–237.1 million; 90%: 145.6–427.2 million). The 50 PIT values are uniform (KS p 0.36; recomputed independently: 46 of 50 inside the 90% interval, the step sizes sum to 50.56 against 50). prime2.html shows these forecasts.
- Power: with 10 validation exponents only strong effects show (a class holding nearly all exponents and at most a fifth of the null weight); round 5's 401 exponents raise the power to effects of about 1.5 times the rate.

## 8. Patterns in the pictures (computed; Bateman–Horn and the Hardy–Littlewood constants are conjectures, Dirichlet's theorem is proved)

prime2.html draws 1 … 500,000 in 43 layouts. Every layout was photographed (whole and zoomed four times, the exponent links off, the brightest line drawn by the page's own line test) and its exact dots read from the page ([patterns/extract.js](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/extract.js), [shots2.js](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/shots2.js)); the report with all screenshots is the page prime2-patterns.html beside prime2.html, built by [gen_report.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/gen_report.py) from [texts.json](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/texts.json).

- Grid layouts (23; [lines.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/lines.py), [lines.log](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/lines.log), [lines_out/](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/lines_out/)): every lattice line in 24 directions cut into stretches where the numbers follow one polynomial; 1.6 million stretches of 30 or more numbers. The prime count of every stretch is predicted by Bateman–Horn, C(f)·Σ 1/ln f(t) with C(f) from the densities of the small primes dividing f: in every layout a random 150 stretches differ from the prediction by 0.0 ± 0.5–0.7 standard deviations, and at most 1 in 150 by more than 3. The brightest lines are Euler's m² + m + 41 (spirals, shells, Klauber, between squares: 394 primes against 393 predicted), 2s² − 199 (tree, Floyd, diagonals, zigzag, diamond: no prime up to 31 divides it, constant 7.32 above Euler's 6.65), 3s² + 3s + 23 (hexagonal spiral) and Dirichlet's columns (rows: constant 708/φ(708) = 3.05; bands of 512: 510/φ(510) = 3.98); the dark lines are polynomials that factor or that a small prime always divides (constant 0). The six space-filling and bit-order layouts have no polynomial stretch of 30 numbers at all.
- Round layouts (12; [round/](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/round/)): the arms are remainder classes (polar: 20 of 44 and 280 of 710 arms hold primes; wheel: 232 spokes; sunflower: classes mod Fibonacci numbers), with arm strengths |μ(q)|/φ(q) as measured to about 0.01; Sacks' streaks are quadratics, 15,388 primes on 242 curves against 15,431 predicted.
- Walks and rows (7; [walks/](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/walks/)): the Mertens walk stays far tighter than a random walk and is reproduced by 150 zeta zeros (correlation 0.99); the Liouville walk touches 0 only at 2, 4, 6, 10, 16, 26, 40, 96, 586 and stays below up to 10⁶ (rechecked); the prime race mod 4 is below zero only at 26,861 and 26,862 up to 500,000 (rechecked); the turtle closes 255 rectangles (five primes with gaps a, b, a, b; recounted) against 362 ± 21 for shuffled gaps, because patterns impossible mod 3 or 5 never occur and the allowed ones are about three times as common (the Hardy–Littlewood constants); the row layouts match Legendre's sieve counts exactly.
- Cosine rows with period 12 ([patterns/cos12/analyse.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/analyse.py) → [analyse.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/analyse.txt)): every prime from 5 sits up (p ≡ ±1 mod 12, 3 a square mod p) or down (p ≡ ±5). The gap to the next prime decides the lane exactly (gaps ≡ 2, 10, 12 mod 12 keep it, 4, 6, 8 switch it), so consecutive primes switch lanes 54.6% of the time up to 500,000 (53.7% up to 10⁷), and repeat their remainder mod 12 only 57% as often as independence gives (66% up to 10⁷; Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan, 2016). Twin pairs always share a lane, up and down equally often (2,318 and 2,246). The down lane leads slightly (Chebyshev's bias; the up lane first leads at 61,463). The exponents lean down: 16 of the 49 from 5 to 82,589,933 are up, about 23 expected (P = 0.023 one-sided; both halves lean the same way, and no. 52 is down), but this is one split among the many residue groupings tested since round 1 (all classes mod 12 together: 0.095), so it is not significant; future exponents are a clean test (about half up if the heuristic holds).
- The cosine rows zoomed out ([patterns/cos12/bigdata.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigdata.py) → [bigdata.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigdata.txt), [race.svg](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/race.svg), [rails.svg](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/rails.svg); pictures by [bigpic2.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigpic2.py); every number rechecked by [bigcheck.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigcheck.py) → [bigcheck.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigcheck.txt), 27 checks): at under one pixel per number the picture is an even speckle; with a row width that is a multiple of 12 (240, numbers to 100,000) the waves line up into stripes: 64 of the 240 columns hold primes, each one lane only, the colour pattern repeating every 60 columns, the stripes equally full (137 to 164 primes, mean 149.8). Twin pairs run on 24 double tracks (12 per lane); all 1,222 from (11, 13) to 100,000 are on them. Width 277 (≡ 1 mod 12) tilts the stripes into diagonals, width 100 (≡ 4) changes each column's lane in a cycle of three rows. To 10⁷ the down lane leads, 332,352 primes against 332,225, by ½ Li(√x) on average: every prime square p² (p ≥ 5) is ≡ 1 (mod 12), and the primes in class 1 fall short by about half their number (Chebyshev's bias; Rubinstein–Sarnak 1994). Measured, the lead averaged over ln x from 10³ to 10⁷ is 0.986 times ½ Li(√x) (1.26 times √x / ln x); the up lane is ahead in 102 stretches (first at 61,463, longest 1,172,807 to 1,179,403, last ending at 2,233,787), after 1,476 of the 664,577 primes.
