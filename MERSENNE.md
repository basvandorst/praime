# Mersenne exponents: a rule that lands only on them

> **In short:** The Mersenne exponents are the primes p with 2^p − 1 prime: 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, … Asked: a rule that steps from one to the next and touches nothing else. 1. Trial factors 2kq + 1 remove about half of the primes, but among the rest the share of exponents grows only like the logarithm of the factor bound: 1.8% → 4.1% below 10⁴, as the Wagstaff heuristic predicts (23.7 expected, 22 found). 2. In 110 tests, no arithmetic feature of the 51 exponents below 83,880,077 beats that heuristic; taken as plain integers, no step rule, formula, recurrence or digit pattern beats random sequences of the same growth, and the one integer congruence that hits them all (A366029) is a probable-prime test of 2^k − 1. About 2,000 more ideas from outside the known formulas (section 7), each chosen on the small exponents and tested on the large ones and on 401 exponents of 79 related families, find nothing either. 3. So each step must prove its prime: the next q whose 2^q − 1 survives trial factoring and passes Lucas–Lehmer. The page has it as transformation 142, as a list without a circle, and as a test of the known exponents alone.

**Page:** [prime.html](writeup/code/riemann/prime.html): transformation 142 "Next Mersenne exponent" (indicator "Mersenne exponents" counts the hits), and the panel "Mersenne exponents, one after another" at the bottom (Start, and "Only the known exponents"). [prime2.html](writeup/code/mersenne/prime2.html) shows the exponents as plain numbers in 54 layouts (section 8 explains every pattern in the first 43; the 11 wave layouts carry their own checked notes) (square, spirals, trees, triangles, space-filling curves, rings, walks), each with a note on why its empty lines are empty, with twin primes, chains of primes in order, a line test that counts the exponents on any lattice line against the heuristic, and the forecasts of section 7.

**Code:** [code/mersenne/](writeup/code/mersenne/), rerun by [run_all.sh](writeup/code/mersenne/run_all.sh).

**Status:** the algorithm is proved (Lucas–Lehmer is a theorem; trial factors are exact). Hit rates, feature tests and timings are computed. The probability model is heuristic.

## 1. The question

132 (Golomb's formula) gives the next prime, and from 1 its chain hits the exponents 2, 3, 5, 7 and then misses 11, 23 and 29: 7 of its first 10 steps are exponents. The aim is 100%: every step a Mersenne exponent, none skipped.

## 2. A filter is not enough (computed; heuristic model)

Every factor of 2^q − 1 has the form 2kq + 1 with 2kq + 1 ≡ ±1 (mod 8) (theorem). Removing the primes with such a factor for k ≤ K ([tf.py](writeup/code/mersenne/tf.py), [rate.py](writeup/code/mersenne/rate.py), output [rate.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/rate.txt); deeper K from [touch/tftable.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/tftable.out)):

| K | primes < 10⁴ left | share that are exponents (22) | primes < 10⁵ left | share (28) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1,229 | 1.8% | 9,592 | 0.29% |
| 100 | 805 | 2.7% | 6,781 | 0.41% |
| 10⁴ | 596 | 3.7% | 5,211 | 0.54% |
| 10⁵ | 532 | 4.1% | 4,665 | 0.60% |
| 10⁷ | 428 | 5.1% | | |

The heuristic P(2^q − 1 prime | no factor below Q) ≈ e^γ·log₂(Q)/q predicts 23.7 exponents below 10⁴ (true 22) and 30.0 below 10⁵ (true 28). The share grows like log Q: reaching 50% needs no factor below about 2^(0.28·q), which is the work of a primality proof ([touch/bound.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/bound.out)).

## 3. No hidden structure (computed)

All 51 exponents, against all 4,882,467 primes below 83,880,077 (GIMPS: every exponent below it tested and verified, milestone page read on 15 November 2026). The null is P = e^γ·log₂(a·p)/p, a = 2 for p ≡ 3 and a = 6 for p ≡ 1 (mod 4). Output in [structure/results.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/structure/results.txt), from [analysis.py](writeup/code/mersenne/structure/analysis.py); an independent recheck (gmpy2 primes, plain integers, its own Poisson-binomial code) in [check.py](writeup/code/mersenne/structure/check.py) → [check.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/structure/check.txt) reproduces every count, expectation and p-value.

- Calibration: 51 observed, 50.3 expected (a = 2/6), 47.6 (a = 2). By mod 4: 31 with p ≡ 1 (25.2 expected), 19 with p ≡ 3 (24.1). The data lean towards a = 6 (Bayes factor 1.9) without deciding it.
- 110 tests: p mod 3, 5, 7, 8, 12, 16, 24, 30, 60, 120; the forms 2^k ± 1 and 4^k ± 3; closeness to a power of 2; binary weight and runs; 2p + 1, p + 2, p − 2 prime; the spacing of log log p. Bonferroni threshold 4.5·10⁻⁴; the smallest p-value is 0.023, and 49% of simulated null sets have a smaller one.
- That smallest p-value belongs to a control that is a theorem: p ≡ 3 (mod 4) with 2p + 1 prime (p > 3) never gives an exponent (Euler: 2p + 1 divides 2^p − 1); 0 found, 3.74 expected. A class with no exponents at all becomes visible only once it would hold more than about 8 of them.
- The one feature found in the exponents below 10⁵ (2^k ± 1 or 4^k ± 3: 10 against 7.0) lies entirely at p ≤ 127 and has nothing to test above 10⁵.

## 4. How few tests per exponent (computed)

[touch/](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/): trial factoring to 10⁷ ([tfdeep.py](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/tfdeep.py)), P−1 run for real with gmpy2 ([pm1.py](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/pm1.py), tables [pm1table_1e4.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/pm1table_1e4.out), [pm1table_1e5.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/pm1table_1e5.out)), orderings ([order.out](writeup/code/mersenne/touch/order.out)).

- Best share of exponents among the Lucas–Lehmer tests: 22 of 350 (6.3%) below 10⁴ (trial factoring to 10⁷ plus P−1 to B1 = 10⁵), 28 of 3,931 (0.71%) below 10⁵.
- P−1 (B1 = 1000, B2 = 3000) costs more than the tests it saves below about 4·10⁴, and 0.42 and 0.30 Lucas–Lehmer tests per removal in [4·10⁴, 7·10⁴) and [7·10⁴, 10⁵).
- Ordering the candidates by their posterior probability is the ascending order (0 positions differ); ordering by the prior with the mod-4 factor finds the first 25 exponents in 895 tests instead of 946 but needs 3,557 instead of 3,392 for all 28.
- The only proofs that 2^q − 1 is composite are a factor or a full Lucas–Lehmer (or PRP) test of about q squarings. A base-2 Fermat test never rejects 2^q − 1 (theorem: 2^q ≡ 1 mod 2^q − 1 and q divides 2^q − 2). So a rule that touches only exponents would need a test nobody has; a complete search tests every prime that survives the factors.

## 5. The algorithm on the page (proved; times measured)

Next exponent after p: the next prime q with no factor 2kq + 1 for k ≤ 100,000·(q/7000)² (at least 1,000; sieved by the primes below 2^14, exact double arithmetic below 2^38), and Lucas–Lehmer s₀ = 4, s ← s² − 2 mod 2^q − 1, s_(q−2) = 0. Below q = 4,200 in BigInt; above, the squaring is an irrational-base FFT (Crandall–Fagin, as in GIMPS) in Float64Array, with the largest rounding error checked at every step (a step above 0.35 is redone at twice the length). Every zero found up to 50,000 (all of them in transformation 142 and in the list) is recomputed in exact BigInt. The readable source is [engine/best.js](writeup/code/mersenne/engine/best.js); the page holds the same functions without comments.

## 6. The exponents as plain numbers (computed; A366029 proved)

The same question with the exponents as integers only: 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, … as a sequence, without "prime" and without 2^p − 1. Two searches, each run identically on 250 to 500 random sequences that grow the same way (null A: points spread at random in log a; null B: random primes taken with the heuristic probability), with a family-wise correction and with every rule chosen on a₁ … a₂₅ and then counted on a₂₆ … a₅₂:

- **Step rules aₙ₊₁ = f(aₙ)** ([numbers/](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/), [report.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/report.txt)): 745,984 maps, compositions to depth 3 of 1,187 primitives (x + c, cx + d, ⌊x·r⌋, powers, 2^x ± c, bit and decimal operations, "the next y > x" in a residue class, palindromic, of a given binary weight or of the form 2^k ± c); the same grammar backwards; 16,884 two-term rules. The best, "(the second number of the form 2^k ± 1 above x) − 2", reproduces 7 of the 51 steps (2→3, 3→5, 5→7, 7→13, 19→31, 31→61, 107→127; all below 128); the null medians are 6 (p = 0.48 and 0.43). No rule chosen on the first 25 terms predicts any later term. Closed forms ([formulas.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/formulas.txt)): ⌊1.45717·1.50872ⁿ⌋ hits 8 terms (p = 0.31); a formula that reproduces all 52 needs at least the 804 bits of the list itself (a Mills-type constant restates the list).
- **Relations and patterns** ([relations/](writeup/code/mersenne/relations/), all 252 statistics in [combined.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/relations/combined.txt)): linear recurrences (Berlekamp–Massey over Q: the generic order ⌈m/2⌉ for every m from 8 to 52; integer recurrences with |c| ≤ 10 run for at most 4 terms), relations a_k = c₁aᵢ + c₂aⱼ + d with small coefficients (225 at d = 0, e.g. 19937 = 5·127 + 6·3217, against a null median of 274), the binary strings (Lempel–Ziv complexity, minimal automata, common substrings), the digits in bases 2 to 36, the growth (0.531 bits per term; the gaps behave like those of a Poisson process). The smallest raw p-value is 0.004 (base 23: two equal neighbouring digits in 12 of the 45 terms with at least two digits), 0.19 after the family-wise correction, and it fails out of sample.
- **The OEIS** ([oeis.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/oeis.txt)): the longest agreement is A366029, defined on the integer k alone: k > 1 with (k − 1)^(2^k − 2) ≡ 1 (mod (2^k − 1)·k²). It is 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 61, 89, 107, 127, then 257 instead of 521.
  - It contains every Mersenne exponent (proved): if 2^p − 1 is prime, Fermat gives (p − 1)^(2^p − 2) ≡ 1 mod 2^p − 1; mod p², (p − 1)^N ≡ 1 − N·p for even N, which is 1 because p divides N = 2^p − 2; and gcd(2^p − 1, p²) = 1.
  - The same expansion shows that the congruence is two Fermat tests (proved): 2^k ≡ 2 (mod k) and (k − 1)^(2^k − 2) ≡ 1 (mod 2^k − 1). [a366029.py](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/a366029.py) → [a366029.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/numbers/a366029.txt) checks both forms against each other for every k ≤ 4,500: they agree, and the terms are the 20 exponents up to 4,423 and 257.
  - The extras (proved): when k − 1 = 2^m the base is a power of 2, and since 2 has order k modulo 2^k − 1, (2^m)^(2^k − 2) ≡ 1 holds whenever k divides 2^k − 2. So every k = 2^m + 1 with 2^k ≡ 2 (mod k) is a term: all Fermat numbers 2^(2^n) + 1, prime or not (257, 65537, 4294967297, …), of which 3, 5 and 17 happen to be exponents.
  - So the one integer rule that lands on every exponent is a probable-prime test of 2^k − 1 under another name: k squarings of a k-bit number, the cost of Lucas–Lehmer.
  - On the page it is transformation 143 (next k > p in A366029, up to 150,000), with the source mode "every number on the circle": the chain from 1 runs 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 61, 89, 107, 127, 257, 521, 607, … (14 of its first 15 steps land on exponents; the miss is 127 → 257). The page decides each k exactly: 2^k ≡ 2 (mod k) first; then the congruence modulo a small factor of 2^k − 1 (for composite k, modulo 2^d − 1 with d the smallest factor of k), which refutes most k; k = 2^m + 1 by the proof above; the rest by the FFT of section 5 with an exact digit multiplication by k − 1, and every term found again in BigInt. Checked in Node on the page's own functions: equal to the definition for every k from 2 to 2,000, no base-2 pseudoprime among 25 (341 … 11305) passes, the FFT path equals exact BigInt on 18 values of k from 4,201 to 11,939, and the chain from 1 to 12,000 is the 23 exponents and 257; 65537 is a term, 65539 is not.

In the integer view as well, nothing beyond the growth law: whatever lands on every exponent tests 2^k − 1.

## 7. Outside the known formulas: rounds 3 to 5 (computed; the null is heuristic)

- The most persistent near-miss, k ≡ 1 (mod 7) (13 of 51 against 7.4 expected; 4 of the 13 in the final range against 0.8), shows no effect at all in the 79 other families, where the holdout alone could detect a rate of 1.4 to 1.6. The 52nd exponent, 136,279,841 (beyond the verified bound, so used by no round), is ≡ 5 (mod 7).
- Inside a Lucas–Lehmer run nothing is recognisable before s_(k−3) ≡ ±2^((k+1)/2) (proved; checked on every exponent from 3 to 19,937). For a prime 2^k − 1, s_i is the trace of an element of order 2^(k−i); only orders up to 8 have closed-form traces, and going back one step costs a square root mod 2^k − 1, itself about k squarings. The Jacobi symbols (s_i ± 2 | 2^k − 1) are the same for prime and composite 2^k − 1 (from i = 1 on; checked on 1,184 checkpoints).
- The final range [10⁷, B) holds 13 exponents against 5.86 expected (round 1: p 0.015 for that range alone, 0.12 after its 8 ranges), so the fair final-exam test is conditional on the 13; both rounds 3b and 3c report it that way.
- The forecast (round 6b; heuristic): with w as above, the expected number of exponents between x and y is e^γ·[log₂(y/x) + log₂√12 · ln(ln y / ln x)] (the integral over the primes, within 1% of the exact prime sums from 10³ on; checked up to 10⁹). From no. 51, 82,589,933, the median forecast for the next exponent was 106.3 million (50%: 91.7–136.9 million); no. 52, 136,279,841, came at u = 0.75. GIMPS has tested every exponent below 142,869,329 at least once (milestones page, 15 November 2026), so no. 53 is forecast at a median of 184.0 million (50%: 158.7–237.1 million; 90%: 145.6–427.2 million). The 50 PIT values are uniform (KS p 0.36; recomputed independently: 46 of 50 inside the 90% interval, the step sizes sum to 50.56 against 50). prime2.html shows these forecasts.
- Power: with 10 validation exponents only strong effects show (a class holding nearly all exponents and at most a fifth of the null weight); round 5's 401 exponents raise the power to effects of about 1.5 times the rate.

## 8. Patterns in the pictures (computed; Bateman–Horn and the Hardy–Littlewood constants are conjectures, Dirichlet's theorem is proved)

prime2.html draws 1 … 500,000 in 43 layouts. Every layout was photographed (whole and zoomed four times, the exponent links off, the brightest line drawn by the page's own line test) and its exact dots read from the page ([patterns/extract.js](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/extract.js), [shots2.js](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/shots2.js)); the report with all screenshots is the page prime2-patterns.html beside prime2.html, built by [gen_report.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/gen_report.py) from [texts.json](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/texts.json).

- Grid layouts (23; [lines.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/lines.py), [lines.log](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/lines.log), [lines_out/](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/lines_out/)): every lattice line in 24 directions cut into stretches where the numbers follow one polynomial; 1.6 million stretches of 30 or more numbers. The prime count of every stretch is predicted by Bateman–Horn, C(f)·Σ 1/ln f(t) with C(f) from the densities of the small primes dividing f: in every layout a random 150 stretches differ from the prediction by 0.0 ± 0.5–0.7 standard deviations, and at most 1 in 150 by more than 3. The brightest lines are Euler's m² + m + 41 (spirals, shells, Klauber, between squares: 394 primes against 393 predicted), 2s² − 199 (tree, Floyd, diagonals, zigzag, diamond: no prime up to 31 divides it, constant 7.32 above Euler's 6.65), 3s² + 3s + 23 (hexagonal spiral) and Dirichlet's columns (rows: constant 708/φ(708) = 3.05; bands of 512: 510/φ(510) = 3.98); the dark lines are polynomials that factor or that a small prime always divides (constant 0). The six space-filling and bit-order layouts have no polynomial stretch of 30 numbers at all.
- Round layouts (12; [round/](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/round/)): the arms are remainder classes (polar: 20 of 44 and 280 of 710 arms hold primes; wheel: 232 spokes; sunflower: classes mod Fibonacci numbers), with arm strengths |μ(q)|/φ(q) as measured to about 0.01; Sacks' streaks are quadratics, 15,388 primes on 242 curves against 15,431 predicted.
- Walks and rows (7; [walks/](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/walks/)): the Mertens walk stays far tighter than a random walk and is reproduced by 150 zeta zeros (correlation 0.99); the Liouville walk touches 0 only at 2, 4, 6, 10, 16, 26, 40, 96, 586 and stays below up to 10⁶ (rechecked); the prime race mod 4 is below zero only at 26,861 and 26,862 up to 500,000 (rechecked); the turtle closes 255 rectangles (five primes with gaps a, b, a, b; recounted) against 362 ± 21 for shuffled gaps, because patterns impossible mod 3 or 5 never occur and the allowed ones are about three times as common (the Hardy–Littlewood constants); the row layouts match Legendre's sieve counts exactly.
- Cosine rows with period 12 ([patterns/cos12/analyse.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/analyse.py) → [analyse.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/analyse.txt)): every prime from 5 sits up (p ≡ ±1 mod 12, 3 a square mod p) or down (p ≡ ±5). The gap to the next prime decides the lane exactly (gaps ≡ 2, 10, 12 mod 12 keep it, 4, 6, 8 switch it), so consecutive primes switch lanes 54.6% of the time up to 500,000 (53.7% up to 10⁷), and repeat their remainder mod 12 only 57% as often as independence gives (66% up to 10⁷; Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan, 2016). Twin pairs always share a lane, up and down equally often (2,318 and 2,246). The down lane leads slightly (Chebyshev's bias; the up lane first leads at 61,463). The exponents lean down: 16 of the 49 from 5 to 82,589,933 are up, about 23 expected (P = 0.023 one-sided; both halves lean the same way, and no. 52 is down), but this is one split among the many residue groupings tested since round 1 (all classes mod 12 together: 0.095), so it is not significant; future exponents are a clean test (about half up if the heuristic holds).
- The cosine rows zoomed out ([patterns/cos12/bigdata.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigdata.py) → [bigdata.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigdata.txt), [race.svg](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/race.svg), [rails.svg](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/rails.svg); pictures by [bigpic2.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigpic2.py); every number rechecked by [bigcheck.py](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigcheck.py) → [bigcheck.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/patterns/cos12/bigcheck.txt), 27 checks): at under one pixel per number the picture is an even speckle; with a row width that is a multiple of 12 (240, numbers to 100,000) the waves line up into stripes: 64 of the 240 columns hold primes, each one lane only, the colour pattern repeating every 60 columns, the stripes equally full (137 to 164 primes, mean 149.8). Twin pairs run on 24 double tracks (12 per lane); all 1,222 from (11, 13) to 100,000 are on them. Width 277 (≡ 1 mod 12) tilts the stripes into diagonals, width 100 (≡ 4) changes each column's lane in a cycle of three rows. To 10⁷ the down lane leads, 332,352 primes against 332,225, by ½ Li(√x) on average: every prime square p² (p ≥ 5) is ≡ 1 (mod 12), and the primes in class 1 fall short by about half their number (Chebyshev's bias; Rubinstein–Sarnak 1994). Measured, the lead averaged over ln x from 10³ to 10⁷ is 0.986 times ½ Li(√x) (1.26 times √x / ln x); the up lane is ahead in 102 stretches (first at 61,463, longest 1,172,807 to 1,179,403, last ending at 2,233,787), after 1,476 of the 664,577 primes. - **9. - **6. A Chebyshev bias for twin primes** (heuristic; not decidable by counting; literature: the race is tabulated to 10¹⁰ with lead changes and no explanation, Sahoo, arXiv:2111.09053). - **7. How many primes have a prime order of 2** (heuristic; literature: the sequence is OEIS A122094, bounds of order x/(log x)² by Pomerance, no formula found). These are the prime factors of Mersenne numbers with a prime exponent: q = 2kl + 1 with l prime. For fixed k, Hardy–Littlewood counts the primes l with 2kl + 1 prime (2C₂ times the product of (r − 1)/(r − 2) over odd r | k); q ≡ ±1 (mod 8) holds for every l when 4 | k, for half of them when k is odd, never when k ≡ 2 (mod 4); then the order is l with chance 1/k. Summed: N(x) ≈ Σ_k 2C₂·m(k)g(k)/k·∫₃^(x/2k) dt/(ln t·ln 2kt), asymptotically A·x/ln²x with A = C₂·Σ m(k)g(k)/k² = 0.5382 ([t7form.py](writeup/code/mersenne/theories/t7form.py) → [t7form.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/theories/t7form.txt)). - **10. One Mersenne factor in five is ≡ 1 (mod 3)** (the rule is proved, the limit heuristic; literature: not found, implicit in Shanks and Kravitz 1967). - **11. The race of the zeros** (heuristic, assumes GRH and LI; literature: the substance is Bays, Ford, Hudson and Rubinstein 2001, who give the mod-19 bias 0.8042). For a prime P the race of non-residue against residue primes is, by the explicit formula, E(x) = (ln x/√x)(π_N(x) − π_R(x)) ≈ b + Σ_ρ x^(iγ)/ρ over the zeros of L(s, χ_P), where b = 1 comes from the prime squares (all residues). The total variance of the zero terms needs no zero at all: V = Σ 1/|ρ|² = 2[Re L′/L(1, χ) + ½ ln(P/π) + ½ψ((1 + a)/2)] (Hadamard product; a = 0 or 1 for even or odd χ). Our one-zero law: the lowest zero γ₁ is a wave of amplitude 2/|ρ₁| and the other zeros are noise of variance V − 2/|ρ₁|², so the share of log-time with E < 0 is P(b + A cos θ + G < 0). ## 14. A model with random remainder classes, tested at every scale (computed; the model is Banks, Ford and Tao's, the variance law is Montgomery and Soundararajan's conjecture, the bias in the race is Rubinstein and Sarnak's, the factor rule is Wagstaff's heuristic)

The coin model of section 13 fails near squares. This section replaces the coin and tests the result against the primes from the shortest distances to the longest ([ladder.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/model/ladder.py) → [ladder_2e7.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/model/ladder_2e7.txt), N = 2·10⁷, statistics on 10⁷ to 2·10⁷, five draws per model).

The count up to 2·10⁷ is 1,270,680 ± 291 against 1,270,607 primes, with no calibration. This is the model of Banks, Ford and Tao with the true classes kept for the small primes.

## 15. Binary layers, and primes found by counting only (the floor is proved; the fold is an identity; the pair counts follow Hardy and Littlewood's conjecture; the rest is computed)

No sieve and no known prime test is used in this section: the numbers are written in binary and taken apart layer by layer, and primes are picked out by adding and counting.

**The fold (identity).** Cut the binary digits of n in pieces of w bits and add the pieces; repeat until one piece is left. Because 2^w ≡ 1 (mod 2^w − 1), the result is n mod (2^w − 1), found without dividing. A prime q shows in the fold (its multiples fill fixed columns) exactly when q divides 2^w − 1.

**The floor (proved).** The depth of an odd prime q is the smallest w with q | 2^w − 1 (the order of 2 mod q, the period of 1/q in binary). From q | 2^w − 1 follows q ≤ 2^w − 1, so depth ≥ log₂(q + 1): a prime of b bits needs at least b layers. Equality holds exactly when q = 2^w − 1. The Mersenne primes are the primes on this floor, and the Mersenne exponents are their depths.

- Checked ([check6.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/layers/check6.py) → [check6.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/layers/check6.txt)) for the 13,009 odd primes up to 140,000: the depth is at least log₂(q + 1) for all, with equality for 3, 7, 31, 127, 8,191 and 131,071. The depth is q − 1 for 4,859 of them, 37.35 % (Artin's conjecture: 37.4 %).
- The primes that first show at w layers, for every w up to 200 ([depth2.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/layers/depth2.py) → [depth200.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/layers/depth200.txt), [depth_rows_200.json](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/layers/depth_rows_200.json); PARI): 420 primes; only the rows 1 and 6 are empty (Bang's theorem). The rows that hold the single prime 2^w − 1 are 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 61, 89, 107 and 127. In the other rows of a prime w the w bits are shared, for example 4.5 + 6.5 for w = 11, 7.4 + 75.6 for w = 83 and 12.9 + 184.1 for w = 197.

## 16. Without carries, rotations, runs and other bases (the splitting rule is classical; the carry lemma and the rotation and run statements are proved; the link between the two kinds of division is measured)

**Multiplying without carries.** Add the shifted copies of a long multiplication so that two ones in a column cancel (exclusive or). Bit patterns then multiply as polynomials over the field of two elements.

- The strip of p ones (the number 2^p − 1) splits, without carries, into (p − 1)/d patterns of d + 1 bits, where d is the order of 2 mod p, the binary depth of p itself; it stays whole exactly when d = p − 1 (classical: the factors of x^p − 1). Computed for the exponents up to 127 ([lay2.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/carry/lay2.py) → [lay2.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/carry/lay2.txt)): 127 = 11 ⊗ 13; 131,071 = 313 ⊗ 471; 2,147,483,647 = 37 ⊗ 41 ⊗ 47 ⊗ 55 ⊗ 59 ⊗ 61; 2^89 − 1 is 8 patterns of 12 bits; 2^127 − 1 is the 18 patterns of 8 bits that cannot be split. The strips for p = 3, 5, 13, 19, 61, 107 stay whole.
- The carry lemma (proved). If a · b is a strip of p ones with p prime and a, b > 1, the multiplication carries. Without carries every column of the stack holds at most one 1, so the numbers of ones multiply: ones(a) · ones(b) = p. Then one factor has a single 1 and is a power of 2, which an odd number above 1 is not.
- Of the 51 odd exponents, 15 give a strip that stays whole (2 is a primitive root). Primes with the same remainders mod 8 give 15.7 ± 2.0 (the share is 0.748 for the remainders 3 and 5, measured below 2·10⁶, and 0 for 1 and 7). No link.
- Prime with carries against unsplittable without carries ([two.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/carry/two.py) → [two.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/carry/two.txt)). Below 2²² there are 295,947 primes, 210,871 unsplittable patterns and 31,937 that are both. An unsplittable pattern ends in 1 and has an odd number of ones, so the comparison is made inside those numbers and length by length: the overlap is 1.026, 1.024 and 1.001 times the independent value for 20, 21 and 22 bits (4,517, 7,546 and 14,165 numbers). Pooled over all lengths the same data would show a false link of 5 %, because short numbers hold more of both kinds.

## 17. The first 30, 100 or 250 numbers as gears (a page to watch; the rule is elementary, the history is literature)

[prime3.html](writeup/code/mersenne/prime3.html) shows the numbers 1 to 30 (or 1 to 100, or 1 to 250) as gears that turn when Start is pressed. Gear k has k teeth and every tick moves every gear one tooth, so gear k is home every k ticks: at tick t the gears at home are the divisors of t, and t is prime when only gear 1 and gear t are home. With only the prime gears the machine is a sieve that is right up to tick 960 (below 31²). D. H. Lehmer built such a sieve from bicycle chains in 1926 and from gear wheels in 1932.

## 20. One wheel decides: a wheel the number does not fit (that every prime comes home is proved; "no mistake below 2^64" is computed and rests on the published list of base-2 pseudoprimes; that there is never a mistake is open: for the ring it is Agrawal's conjecture with x + 1 in place of x − 1, the same statement when the number of cells is even; for the two cells it is a Frobenius test, which a start of norm 2 settles below 2^64, given the list, and nothing settles above; the chain of wheels and the test with a receipt are literature and their runs are computed; the four machines that find primes before their tick are literature with computed runs, and the rule for the rounds of machine 23 is a heuristic)

Sections 17 to 19 judged a machine by how many prime wheels are home together. A harder demand is that one wheel decides alone: wheel n must be home after n turns exactly when n is prime. The gear rules were put in a contest on that demand ([tour.c](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/tour.c), [psp2.c](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/psp2.c), [summary.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/summary.py) → [tournament.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/tournament.txt)).

**The machines.** Each wheel n carries a few cells that hold remainders after division by n.

- Doubling wheel: one cell, doubled every turn; home when it shows its start again, 2^n ≡ 2 (mod n).
- Fibonacci wheel: two cells (a, b) → (b, a + b) from (0, 1); home when n divides F(n − 1) for n ≡ ±1 (mod 5) and F(n + 1) for n ≡ ±2 (mod 5); multiples of 5 are turned away.
- Perrin's wheel: three cells (a, b, c) → (b, c, a + b) from (3, 0, 2); home when n divides the first cell after n turns.
- Two-cell wheel with step c: cells (a, b) → (a + c·b, a + b) from (1, 0), which is multiplication by 1 + √c; home when the cells read (1, −1), that is (1 + √c)^n ≡ 1 − √c (mod n).
- Pascal's wheel with r cells: a ring with a 1 in cell 0; a turn replaces every cell by itself plus the cell before it. After t turns the ring holds the coefficients of (x + 1)^t mod (n, x^r − 1). Home means a 1 in cell 0, a 1 in cell n mod r and nothing else: (x + 1)^n ≡ x^n + 1 (mod n, x^r − 1).

**Every prime comes home (proved).** For a prime n every binomial coefficient C(n, k) with 0 < k < n is divisible by n, because n divides n! and not k!(n − k)!. So (x + 1)^n ≡ x^n + 1 (mod n) before the ring is folded, and folding keeps it. The same binomials give (1 + √c)^n ≡ 1 + c^((n − 1)/2) √c (mod n), and c^((n − 1)/2) ≡ −1 when c is not a square mod the prime n (Euler's criterion). The other three wheels are the classical statements 2^n ≡ 2, n | F(n − (5/n)) and n | P(n) for primes. So a wheel that is not home proves that n is not prime; the only possible mistake is a number that is not prime and comes home.

**Two lemmas that make the search finite (proved).**

1. Sum of the cells. A turn doubles the sum of the cells, so after n turns the sum is 2^n; the home position has sum 2. A number that comes home on any Pascal ring therefore satisfies 2^n ≡ 2 (mod n): it fools the doubling wheel first. The odd numbers that do are known below 2^64: 118,968,378 of them (Feitsma and Galway; the 20,516 smallest agree with our own sieve, [list_check.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/list_check.py) → [list_check.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/list_check.txt)).
2. Folding. If d divides r, folding the ring of r cells onto d cells (cell k onto cell k mod d) turns one home position into the other. A mistake on r cells is a mistake on every ring of d cells, d | r.

3. The sign. Agrawal's congruence is (x − 1)^n ≡ x^n − 1 (mod n, x^r − 1); Pascal's wheel is (x + 1)^n ≡ x^n + 1. For odd n they are the same statement on a ring with an even number of cells: replace x by −x. On an odd number of cells r they differ, and the link is: n is home with x − 1 on 2r cells exactly when it is home with x − 1 on r cells and with x + 1 on r cells. Proof: x^(2r) − 1 = (x^r − 1)(x^r + 1), and the two factors differ by 2, a unit mod n; so the ring of 2r cells is the product of the rings mod x^r − 1 and mod x^r + 1, and x → −x carries the second onto the first and x − 1 onto −(x + 1). Checked on every odd number below 10^6 that is not prime, for r = 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 ([agrawal_sign.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/agrawal_sign.py) → [agrawal_sign.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/agrawal_sign.txt)). Lemma 1 has no counterpart for x − 1 on an odd ring, where the sum of the cells is 0 after the first turn: of the 13 numbers below 10^6 that are home with x − 1 on 5 cells, 9 are not base-2 pseudoprimes. So the list reaches Pascal's form on every ring, and Agrawal's form on even rings only.
4. Folding by symmetry. Let r be prime and H a group of residues mod r. Renumbering the cells k → hk (x → x^h) carries the ring onto itself and a home position onto a home position, so a number that is home satisfies (x^h + 1)^n ≡ x^(hn) + 1 for every h. Multiply over h in H: u^n ≡ u(x^n) for u = ∏ (x^h + 1). This u does not change under H, so apart from cell 0 it has one value on each of the e = (r − 1)/|H| classes of H, and so has each of its powers: the test runs on a wheel of e cells (the sums of x^k over a class, taken mod 1 + x + … + x^(r−1)). A mistake on r cells is a mistake on every folded wheel. Exact facts for every prime r below 100 and every prime power e dividing (r − 1)/2, 49 folds ([fold_units.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/fold_units.py) → [fold_units.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/fold_units.txt)): mod 1 + x + … + x^(r−1) the product of all r − 1 factors x^h + 1 is 1, so u is a unit; and u itself is 1, so that the fold says nothing, when 2 lies in H (then ∏ (x^(2h) − 1) = ∏ (x^h − 1), and x^h + 1 is the quotient of the factors), and for these r in no other case. That happens for (r, e) = (17, 2), (31, 3), (41, 2), (43, 3), (73, 2), (73, 4), (89, 2), (89, 4), (97, 2).

**Fit.** Say that n fits a ring of r cells when n² ≡ 1 (mod r). The condition is in the literature: Popovych (2009, Cryptology ePrint 2009/008) restated Agrawal's conjecture as "if (x − 1)^n ≡ x^n − 1 and (x + 2)^n ≡ x^n + 2 (mod n, x^r − 1), then n is prime or n² ≡ 1 (mod r)" (quoted from the Wikipedia article on Agrawal's conjecture; of the paper itself we read only the abstract). Our runs use one offset, x + 1, where he uses two. Of those 42,120 mistakes, 0 are numbers that do not fit their ring. On 5 cells the 628 mistakes below 10^10 split as 611 with n ≡ 1 and 17 with n ≡ −1 (mod 5), none with n ≡ ±2. Why ±1 is special (a heuristic, not a proof): for a prime factor p of n and an r-th root of unity ζ over the field with p elements, home means (ζ + 1)^n = ζ^n + 1. For n ≡ 1 this reads (ζ + 1)^(n − 1) = 1, and for n ≡ −1, because ζ^(−1) + 1 = (ζ + 1)/ζ, it reads (ζ + 1)^(n − 1) = ζ^(−1). Both are conditions on the order of one element, of the kind that Carmichael numbers meet. For another class ζ^n + 1 is not ζ + 1 times a root of unity, and the equation asks for a coincidence between two unrelated elements, unless n ≡ p^j (mod r) for every prime factor, the case in which Lenstra and Pomerance expect mistakes among very large numbers. A simpler guess, that every prime factor p of a mistake has a power p^j ≡ n (mod r), was tested and fails for 14 of the 628 mistakes on 5 cells: a factor p ≡ 1 with n ≡ −1 ([factor_classes.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/factor_classes.py) → [factor_classes.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/factor_classes.txt)).

**Pascal's wheel on 5 cells contains the Fibonacci wheel (proved).** For r = 5 and H = {1, 4} the fold is u = (x + 1)(x⁴ + 1) = 2 + x + x⁴, and x + x⁴ is 2 cos 72° = φ − 1 for the golden ratio φ, so u = φ + 1 = φ². A number without the factor 5 that is home on 5 cells therefore satisfies φ^(2n) ≡ φ² for n ≡ ±1 and φ^(2n) ≡ φ^(−2) for n ≡ ±2 (mod 5), in the integers extended by φ, mod n. With √5 F(k) = φ^k − (−1/φ)^k and k = n ∓ 1, which is even, √5 F(k) φ^k = φ^(2k) − 1 ≡ 0; φ is a unit and 5 has no factor in common with n, so n divides F(n − 1), respectively F(n + 1). **A three-cell wheel (that every prime comes home is proved; the rest is computed).** Folding gives a machine that is not a ring: a cubic cousin of the two-cell wheel. Take a prime r ≡ 1 (mod 3) for which 2 is not a cube mod r (7, 13, 19, 37, 61, 67, …) and let H be the cubes mod r. The folded wheel has three cells, one for each class of H. A turn multiplies by u, a fixed rule that replaces each cell by a sum of the three; for r = 7 it is multiplication by 2 + 2 cos(2π/7), a root of u³ − 5u² + 6u − 1. For n take the first such r that does not divide n and for which n is not a cube mod r; then n is home when after n turns the three cells show what they showed after the first turn, moved round by one or two places. So n acts with order 3: by the rule above this is a wheel that n does not fit. A number divisible by a wheel size tried on the way is turned away, and a cube has no wheel. **Prime powers (proved, then computed).** Write (x + 1)^p = x^p + 1 + p h(x). Then (x + 1)^(p²) = (x^p + 1 + p h(x))^p ≡ (x^p + 1)^p ≡ x^(p²) + 1 + p h(x^p) (mod p²). So p² comes home on r cells exactly when h(x^p), and hence h(x) (x → x^p only permutes the cells, as p does not divide r), vanishes mod (p, x^r − 1). Now h(1) = (2^p − 2)/p, which is divisible by p only for a Wieferich prime, and x(x + 1)(x² + x + 1) divides h as a polynomial. Any higher power needs a Wieferich prime too: if p^k, k ≥ 2, comes home, lemma 1 gives 2^(p^k − 1) ≡ 1 (mod p²); the order of 2 mod p² divides p(p − 1) and p^k − 1, whose greatest common divisor is p − 1, so 2^(p − 1) ≡ 1 (mod p²). For the two known Wieferich primes 1093 and 3511 the square fools the rings of 2, 3 and 4 cells, which every number fits, and no other ring up to 200 cells ([lemma_pp2.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/lemma_pp2.py) → [lemma_pp2.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/lemma_pp2.txt)).

**Mersenne numbers (proved).** For n = 2^p − 1 with p an odd prime, the two-cell wheel is the Lucas–Lehmer test. Here n ≡ 1 (mod 3) and n ≡ 3 (mod 4), so (3/n) = −1 and the step is 3. Home means (1 + √3)^n ≡ 1 − √3, so (1 + √3)^(n+1) ≡ (1 − √3)(1 + √3) = −2. Now (1 + √3)² = 2(2 + √3) and n + 1 = 2^p, so (1 + √3)^(n+1) = 2^(2^(p−1)) (2 + √3)^(2^(p−1)), and 2^(2^(p−1)) ≡ 2 (mod n) because 2^p ≡ 1 (mod n) and 2^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p). So a wheel that is home gives (2 + √3)^(2^(p−1)) ≡ −1 (mod n). That is the Lucas–Lehmer condition, and it proves that n is prime: for a prime factor q of n the element 2 + √3 then has order exactly 2^p among fewer than q² elements, so q² > 2^p > n. On Mersenne numbers machine 11 cannot make a mistake. Check: for the 429 odd primes p below 3,000 the wheel and the Lucas–Lehmer test agree, always with step 3; the wheel is home for p = 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 61, 89, 107, 127, 521, 607, 1279, 2203, 2281 ([duo_mersenne.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/duo_mersenne.py) → [duo_mersenne.txt](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/duo_mersenne.txt)). **A chain of wheels (the three theorems are literature; the runs are computed).** Machines 14, 15 and 16 are one idea with three sources of wheel sizes, and together they give a machine that is proved, fast, and has worked for every number tried. Let n be the number and f, q primes.

1. One cell (Pocklington). Suppose a^(n−1) ≡ 1 (mod n) and gcd(a^((n−1)/f) − 1, n) = 1 for every prime f of a set, and let F be the product of the full powers of these f in n − 1. For a prime p dividing n, the order of a mod p divides n − 1 and not (n − 1)/f, so it contains the full power of f; it divides p − 1. Hence p ≡ 1 (mod F). If F² > n, every prime factor of n exceeds √n, and n is prime.
2. Two cells (the n + 1 partner; Morrison). Let c be coprime to n, u = a + √c with a² − c coprime to n, and β = u²/(a² − c), which is u divided by its conjugate and has norm 1. Suppose β^(n+1) = 1 and β^((n+1)/f) − 1 has a norm coprime to n for every prime f of a set, with F the product of their full powers in n + 1. Mod a prime p dividing n the two cells are either two copies of the remainders mod p (c is a square mod p; orders divide p − 1) or the field of p² elements, where the elements of norm 1 form a group of order p + 1. As before the order of β contains F, so F divides p − 1 or p + 1 and p ≥ F − 1. If (F − 1)² > n, n is prime.
3. A curve (Goldwasser and Kilian). Let gcd(n, 6) = 1, let E be y² = x³ + ax + b with gcd(4a³ + 27b², n) = 1, P a point of E mod n, and m = c · q with q > (n^(1/4) + 1)². Add points by the chord-and-tangent rule, where every division is by a number coprime to n. If m · P is the point at infinity and c · P is not, n is prime. Proof: mod a prime p dividing n every step of the same computation is a valid step on E over the remainders mod p, so the order of P there divides m and not c; q is prime, so q divides that order. The curve has at most (√p + 1)² points (Hasse), so (√p + 1)² ≥ q > (n^(1/4) + 1)² and p > √n.

In each case n is prime if the large prime q (or f) is, and that number is smaller: a chain, ended by trial division below 10^10.

Where the curve sizes come from (Atkin and Morain). Machine 16 counts the points of a curve, which takes about n steps. For a negative discriminant D with 4n = t² + |D| s² the curves with complex multiplication by D have n + 1 − t and n + 1 + t points (for D = −4 also n + 1 ± 2s, for D = −3 also n + 1 ± (t ± 3s)/2); t and s follow from a square root of D mod n by Cornacchia's algorithm. The j-invariants of these curves are the roots mod n of the class polynomial of D. We use the 97 fundamental discriminants with class number at most 4 ([classpoly_gen.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/classpoly_gen.py) with PARI → [classpoly.json](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/classpoly.json); [classpoly_check.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/classpoly_check.py) recomputes every polynomial from the j-function with mpmath: 97 checked, 0 differ). From a root j the curve is y² = x³ − 3kx + 2k with k = j/(j − 1728); for a random x0 and z = x0³ − 3k x0 + 2k the curve y² = x³ − 3k z² x + 2k z³ contains the point (z x0, z²), so no square root is needed, and z decides which of the two sizes it has. For D = −3 and −4 the curve is y² = x³ + b or y² = x³ + ax through a random point.

The search ([chain_wheel.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/chain_wheel.py); the page's version is the same in JavaScript). (i) n must pass the doubling wheel (the strong test to base 2); the page also asks the wheel of machine 19 first, so that a composite which fools the doubling wheel is turned away at once and not after a failed search. (ii) List every size on offer: n − 1, n + 1 and the curve sizes. Divide out the primes below 10^5; keep a size when what remains is a probable prime q that is large enough. (iii) Take the candidates with the smallest q first; prove q by the same procedure, remembering the numbers that failed; then find a, c or the curve and the point, and run the check of the theorem. (iv) If nothing works, split the remaining parts with Pollard's rho (20,000 steps) and try again. The result is a list of steps. A separate program, [chain_verify.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/chain_verify.py), which shares no code with the search, checks the conditions of the three theorems on every step.

**A test with a receipt (the halving protocol and its weakness are literature; the runs are computed).** For a Mersenne number the Lucas–Lehmer test cannot be beaten by any wheel of this section; what can be saved is the second run that a result needs before it is trusted. The statement to check is y = x^(2^T) in a group: T squarings.

The protocol (Pietrzak, 2018). If T is odd, replace y by y² and T by T + 1. The tester sends the middle value μ = x^(2^(T/2)). Then y = μ^(2^(T/2)), and for every number r: (x^r · μ)^(2^(T/2)) = μ^r · y, which is again a statement of the same kind with half the turns. The number r is taken from a hash of x, y, μ and T (64 bits of SHA-256 here), so the tester cannot choose it. After k halvings the checker makes the remaining T/2^k squarings itself; in all it makes T/2^k squarings and 2k powers with exponents of 64 bits. The tester needs no second pass. With h_1, …, h_k the lengths of the halves, the i-th middle value is f(i − 1, h_i), where f(0, m) = x^(2^m) and f(j, m) = f(j − 1, m)^(r_j) · f(j − 1, m + h_j); so it keeps the residues at the 2^k − 1 positions h_i + (a sum of some of h_1, …, h_(i−1)) and builds the receipt from them.

Two groups ([receipt.py](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/receipt.py)). One cell: x = 3 mod n = 2^p − 1 and T = p; for a prime n, 3^(n+1) = 3^(2^p) ≡ 9, so y = 9 is a probable-prime test (not a proof). Two cells: x = 2 + √3, which has norm 1, and T = p − 2; squaring is (a, b) → (2a² − 1, 2ab), because a² − 3b² = 1. Twice the first cell is x^(2^i) + x^(−2^i), the Lucas–Lehmer sequence 4, 14, 194, …, so the first cell is 0 after p − 2 squarings exactly when 2^p − 1 is prime (the Lucas–Lehmer theorem, p odd). This is machine 11's wheel with step 3 again, and its verdict is a proof.

A forged receipt (the weakness is literature: Hoffmann, Hubáček, Kamath and Pietrzak, 2023, who state it for Proth numbers and repair it; the runs are ours). Let ε be an element with ε^d = 1 for a small d, and suppose the tester knows the true y and μ. It reports y·ε and sends μ·δ. The check of the first halving, (x^r · μδ)^(2^h) = (μδ)^r · yε with h = T/2, holds exactly when δ^(2^h − r) = ε. Taking δ = ε^t, this is t·(2^h − r) ≡ 1 (mod d), where r depends on t through the hash; each t without a common factor with d can be tried, and each fits with a chance of about 1/d, because r is a hash value (heuristic). After that the new statement is true and the rest of the receipt is honest. If T is odd the first step squares y, so ε is replaced by ε² and the factor −1 disappears at once: the receipt cannot see the sign. For a cube root of 1 the heuristic gives 1 − (2/3)² = 56% and we saw 57%; for the factor of order 4 on two cells (ε² = −1, one try, chance 1/2) we saw 51%. In the table an attempt is one exponent with one label added to the hash (40 labels per exponent, to count how often the hash falls right; a real receipt has no such label, so a forger has one try per factor).

A guard (ours; tested, not proved). Compare not the result but a power of it that removes every factor of small order: on one cell y^L against 9^L, with L = 2 times the prime factors below 2^16 of (n − 1)/2; on two cells whether y^(2^20) = 1. A result that passes this weaker comparison is sent to a full test. **A two-cell wheel that is settled alone below 2^64 (the norm lemma is literature; the run is computed and rests on the published list).** Pascal's wheel is settled alone below 2^64 by lemma 1 (the sum of its cells is the doubling wheel), but it needs 5 to 47 cells. Machine 11 has two cells and was checked below 2^64 only together with the doubling wheel. A different start gives a wheel of two cells that needs no second test.

The wheel. Let n be odd and not a perfect square. For a = 1, 2, 3, … take c = a² − 2 (from a = 2 on) and then c = a² + 2. If c and n share a factor and n does not divide c, n is not prime. Stop at the first c with Jacobi symbol (c/n) = −1. The two cells start at (a, 1), that is a + √c, and n is called prime when (a + √c)^n ≡ a − √c (mod n). The starts are 1 + √3, 2 + √2, 2 + √6, 3 + √7, 3 + √11, …

1. Every prime comes home (proved). For a prime n and (c/n) = −1 the numbers x + y√c mod n form a field with n² elements, in which the n-th power is the map √c → −√c. A suitable a exists: for a prime n > 3 the sum of the Legendre symbols of a² + 2 over all a mod n is −1, so a² + 2 is a non-residue for at least (n − 1)/2 values of a; n = 3 uses 2 + √2.
2. The norm lemma (literature: a Frobenius pseudoprime for x² − Px + Q is a Fermat pseudoprime to base Q; here P = 2a, Q = ±2). The map √c → −√c is a ring automorphism mod n. Apply it to (a + √c)^n ≡ a − √c: also (a − √c)^n ≡ a + √c. Multiply the two: (a² − c)^n ≡ a² − c (mod n). Now a² − c = 2 or −2 and n is odd, so 2^n ≡ 2 and 2^(n−1) ≡ 1 (mod n). A composite n that comes home is a base-2 pseudoprime.

4. Why it behaves like a pair of wheels (proved). Write a² − c = 2s with s = 1 or −1. Then (a + √c)² = (a² + c) + 2a√c = 2ε with ε = (a² − s) + a√c, and ε has norm (a² − s)² − a²c = 1: the square of the start is twice a unit. So (a + √c)^n = (a + √c) · 2^((n−1)/2) · ε^((n−1)/2), and (a − √c)/(a + √c) = (a − √c)²/(2s) = s/ε. Home is therefore the single equation 2^((n−1)/2) · ε^((n+1)/2) ≡ s (mod n): Euler's test for 2 and a Lucas test for the unit ε, tied together. For the start 1 + √3 the unit is 2 + √3, the wheel of the Lucas–Lehmer test; for 2 + √2 it is 3 + 2√2, the square of 1 + √2. The pair "doubling wheel and a Lucas test" is the idea of Baillie, Pomerance, Selfridge and Wagstaff; here both turn on one shaft, and the first is not run separately.

So, given that the published list is complete: for every odd n below 2^64 this wheel alone says prime exactly when n is prime. The same lemma covers machine 11 when its step is 3, because 1 + √3 has norm −2; for the steps 5, 7, 11, … the norm is −4, −6, −10, … and the list does not apply.

Machine 20, squaring wheels (the verdict is that of machine 19; the count of ticks is proved). The two cells of wheel n hold a + √c, the start of machine 19. Write n in binary as 1 b₁ b₂ … b_k. At tick i the wheel multiplies its two cells with themselves and, when b_i = 1, makes one more plain turn (it multiplies by a + √c).

1. After tick i the cells hold (a + √c)^m with m = (1 b₁ … b_i) in binary (proved: squaring doubles m and the plain turn adds b_i). So after k = ⌊log₂ n⌋ ticks they hold (a + √c)^n, and tick T decides every wheel below 2^(T+1).
2. No wheel that has to make n turns is earlier (proved). Let a tick be one multiplication of two values that the wheel already holds and at most one plain turn. Before the first tick the wheel holds 1 turn, fewer than 2. If the values held after t − 1 ticks stand for fewer than 2^t turns, the new value stands for at most 2(2^t − 1) + 1 < 2^(t+1) turns. So after t ticks fewer than 2^(t+1) turns are made, n turns need t ≥ ⌊log₂ n⌋ ticks, and the wheel is done exactly then. (A test that needs only the power (n ± 1)/2 could be one tick earlier.)

Machine 21, gears from its own lamps (proved; the sieve of Eratosthenes counted in gears). Tick 1 puts in gear 2. At every tick after that the machine takes the smallest lamp that is not yet a gear.

1. Let the gears be the primes p₁, …, p_j and let q be the smallest number above p_j that no gear divides. Then q is prime: a prime factor of q below q is either at most p_j, and then it is a gear, or it lies between p_j and q, and then it is itself a number above p_j that no gear divides, smaller than q. A number m below q² that is not prime has a prime factor at most √m < q, and that factor is a gear. So every number below q² that no gear divides is prime (proved). Tick j lights the primes below p_(j+1)².

Machine 22, counting wheels (proved; Legendre's rule of 1808 in the form known as Lucy's recursion, as in [p9_lucy.c](writeup/code/p9_lucy.c)). Let S(v, p) be the number of m with 2 ≤ m ≤ v that are prime or have no prime factor up to p. Then S(v, 1) = v − 1.

1. For a prime p with p² ≤ v the numbers that leave the count are p · m with p ≤ m ≤ v/p and m without a prime factor below p. There are S(⌊v/p⌋, p − 1) − π(p − 1) of them, and π(p − 1) = S(p − 1, p − 1). So S(v, p) = S(v, p − 1) − (S(⌊v/p⌋, p − 1) − S(p − 1, p − 1)) (proved). For p² > v nothing leaves, so S(v, p) = π(v) as soon as p ≥ √v: a counter is exact once the tick has passed the square root of its number.
2. p is prime exactly when S(p, p − 1) is one more than S(p − 1, p − 1) (proved: among the numbers up to p, only p itself can be without a prime factor below p, so S(p, p − 1) = π(p), and likewise S(p − 1, p − 1) = π(p − 1)). The machine needs no other test.
3. The counters needed for x are those of v = ⌊x/k⌋, because ⌊⌊x/k⌋/p⌋ = ⌊x/(kp)⌋: about 2√x of them. A move is one change of a counter.

Machine 23, wheels that light each other (the proof steps are literature; the count of rounds is computed; its growth is a heuristic). Round 0: only the prime 2 is lit. In every round a dark number N looks at the lit primes that divide N − 1 and at those that divide N + 1. Let F₁ be the part of N − 1 made of lit primes (with their full powers), F₂ the same for N + 1, and L = lcm(F₁, F₂).

1. If L² > N, the lit primes are enough to decide N (proved, from steps 1 and 2 of the chain of wheels above; the combination is that of Brillhart, Lehmer and Selfridge, 1975). Run the one-cell test for the primes of F₁ and the two-cell test, with one step c for all of them, for the primes of F₂. If N fails one of them it is not prime. If it passes, every prime factor r of N is 1 mod F₁ and, with one sign for the whole of F₂, 1 or −1 mod F₂. So r is 1 or x mod L, where x is the number below L that is 1 mod F₁ and −1 mod F₂. A composite N has a prime factor r ≤ √N < L; r cannot be 1 mod L, so r = x. Hence N is not prime when 1 < x < N and x divides N, and prime otherwise: one division finishes the proof.
2. Every prime is lit in the end, and nothing else (proved). For a prime N the witnesses exist: a number that is not an f-th power mod N for the one cell, an element of order N + 1 among the elements of norm 1 for the two cells. By induction the primes below N are lit at some round, and when all prime factors of N − 1 are lit, L ≥ N − 1 > √N.
3. rounds(N) is 1 more than the smallest g such that the primes lit by round g are enough. Round 1 is known without a run (proved from the rule): the primes with (2^m)² > N for the power 2^m in N − 1 or N + 1, that is the Proth primes k · 2^m + 1 with k < 2^m and their mirror images k · 2^m − 1 with k < 2^m: 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 23, 31, 41, 47, 79, 97, …

The rule of thumb (heuristic). Suppose the primes below y are lit after g rounds. The part of a random number that is made of primes below y has, on average, about as many digits as y (the sum of ln p/(p − 1) over p ≤ y is ln y + O(1)). N is lit in the next round when F₁ and F₂ together have more than half the digits of N, and each neighbour supplies about ln y: 2 ln y > ½ ln N, that is N < y⁴. So one more round raises the size that can be reached to about its fourth power, and rounds(N) ≈ log₄(ln N) + constant. With N − 1 alone the same count gives N < y², hence log₂(ln N). The fit (computed): a straight line through 30 size classes (every class from 2^10 to 2^31 and the random sizes from 12 to 60 digits; the table above shows some of them; mean rounds against ln ln N) has slope 0.73, where 1/ln 4 = 0.72; and with the slope fixed at 1/ln 4 the difference rounds − log₄(ln N) lies between 0.47 and 0.56 for every one of these classes. With N − 1 alone and the slope fixed at 1/ln 2 the difference lies between −0.01 and 0.16 for the classes 2^10 to 2^28.

The rule tried on sizes it was not made from (computed; [inherit_big_70.out](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/inherit_big_70.out), [inherit_big_80.out](writeup/code/mersenne/strategies/ten/gears/tour/inherit_big_80.out)). The constant 0.51 comes from the classes up to 60 digits. Afterwards: 40 random primes of 70 digits needed 4.175 rounds on average (standard error 0.08), and the rule says 4.174; 24 random primes of 80 digits needed 4.208 rounds on average (standard error 0.10), and the rule says 4.270. None needed more than 5 rounds.

What is known, and what we did not find. The two proof steps are Pocklington's (1914) and Morrison's (1975). Counting levels of such proofs is known in two forms: the classes of Erdős and Selfridge (all prime factors of p + 1, or of p − 1, down to 2 and 3; OEIS A005113 and A056637) and the Pratt tree (all prime factors of p − 1), whose height Ford, Konyagin and Luca (2010) bound for almost all primes and for which their model suggests about e · ln ln p. The count used here differs: both neighbours, and only as many helpers as it takes to pass √N. We did not find it in a search, and the local copy of the OEIS (31 October 2026) contains neither 2, 3, 19, 173, 21767, 1688173, 2223990533 nor the list of round 1. It is a small step from known things and may well be known.

What machine 23 is not. It is not a faster way to find primes: a round is heavy, because every dark wheel tries in every round, and a machine that may turn as much as it likes within one tick can let each wheel decide alone, as machine 20 does. For a large N the hard part is taking N − 1 and N + 1 apart. What the rounds measure is how deep a proof by these two steps has to go.

**What this is and is not.** No wheel here is new mathematics: the pair is the test of Baillie, Pomerance, Selfridge and Wagstaff, the two-cell wheel is a Frobenius test (Khashin's, here with odd prime steps only; for his own version, which tries the steps −1 and 2 first, he states that there is no mistake below 2^64), and Pascal's wheel is the congruence of Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena, whose proved test needs many rings and offsets. What the contest adds is the common rule in gear terms (in every case found a number cheats only on a wheel it fits, so take a wheel it does not fit; for the ring this is the exception clause of Popovych's version of the conjecture, carried over here to the wheels of two and three cells), the check of Pascal's wheel up to 2^64 on every ring up to 42 cells, the folding of the ring onto small wheels with its link to the Fibonacci wheel and the prize problem, the remark that the two-cell wheel is the Lucas–Lehmer test on Mersenne numbers, and a small statement on prime powers. A proof that either kept machine never errs would give a prime test of a few lines and would be a major result; so would the first number that fools it.
